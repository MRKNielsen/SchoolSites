Starting point

The database got you the rows. Now describe them.

SQL is excellent at selecting, joining and counting. It is weak at the statistics this study names — SQLite has no median and no standard deviation at all.

So the workflow is: query in the database, refine in the spreadsheet, visualise from there.

That workflow is the Outcome 1 task

“Importing data from files into a database where data is organised using queries. Data from these queries is then imported into a spreadsheet for further refinement.”

That is the study design describing what you are about to do.

Key knowledge

Average, median, count, standard deviation

StatisticFormulaTells you
Average (mean)=AVERAGE(D2:D2191)The balance point. Dragged by extremes
Median=MEDIAN(D2:D2191)The middle value. Ignores extremes
Count / frequency=COUNT(D2:D2191)
=COUNTIF(F2:F2191,">0")		How many. How many of a kind
Standard deviation=STDEV.S(D2:D2191)How spread out. Small means clustered

The comparison that matters

When the two disagree, something is going on

Rainfall at Cape Otway: most days are zero, a few are enormous.

  • Mean daily rainfall: about 2.8 mm
  • Median daily rainfall: 0.0 mm

Both are correct. They answer different questions.

What the gap tells you

A mean well above the median means the data is skewed — a few large values pulling the average up.

“Average rainfall 2.8 mm a day” is true and gives entirely the wrong picture of what most days are like.

Worked example

I do “What is a typical day at Mildura?”

  1. 1 Typical temperature: the distribution is roughly symmetric, so the mean is honest. About 24.4 °C.
  2. 2 Typical rainfall: most days are zero, so the mean is misleading. The median is 0.0.
  3. 3 How variable? Standard deviation of maximum temperature — large, because summer and winter differ hugely.
  4. 4 How often does it rain? COUNTIF above zero: 76 days of 365.
  5. ✓ Four statistics, four different questions. Choosing the wrong one is not a calculation error — it is a reasoning error.
What examiners look for

Not the number. The sentence after it that says what the number means, and why you chose that statistic.

Together

We do Which would you use?

1. Typical house price in a suburb with one $12m sale

Median — the mean is dragged by the outlier

2. How many days exceeded 35 °C

COUNTIF — a frequency, not an average

3. Whether two stations have similar or wildly different temperatures

Standard deviation — means can match while spreads differ

Number 3 is the subtle one

Two stations can share a mean of 20 °C and feel nothing alike — one steady, one swinging between 5 and 35.

Key knowledge

Checking before you calculate

CheckFormulaFinds
Existence=COUNTBLANK(F2:F2191)The 42 missing rainfall values
Type=COUNT(E2:E2191) vs =COUNTA(...)Values stored as text — the “82.4 °C” problem
Range=COUNTIF(E2:E2191,">50")Impossible temperatures

Run all three before any statistic. A mean computed over a column containing text silently ignores the text rows, and your denominator is wrong.
