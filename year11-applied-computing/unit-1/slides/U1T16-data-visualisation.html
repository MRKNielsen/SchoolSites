The test for every chart you make

Could a reader reach a wrong conclusion from this and still be reading it correctly? If yes, the chart is the problem.

Key knowledge

Educate, entertain, inform, persuade

PurposeAudience wantsExample
EducateTo understand something newHow a water cycle works
EntertainTo enjoy itA playful chart of film box office
InformThe facts, quicklyToday's forecast
PersuadeTo be convincedA campaign for climate action

Persuade is legitimate. Persuading with a distorted axis is not. The purpose changes your design choices; it does not licence misleading anyone.

Key knowledge

Infographic, poster series, dashboard

TypeIsBest for
Infographic (long-form)One scrolling or printed narrativeA story with a beginning and end
Series of postersSeveral related single pagesOne idea each, displayed together
DashboardLive panels, updatingMonitoring something ongoing

The study design names all three. A dashboard is for data that changes; using one for a fixed historical dataset is a type mismatch, and an examiner will notice.

Key skill

The data type decides the chart

You haveUseNot
One value per categoryColumn chartPie, unless parts of one whole
A value changing over timeLine chartColumn — it hides the trend
Two numbers per recordScatter plotTwo separate lines
Parts of a wholeStacked bar, or a pie if few partsSeveral pies side by side
Distribution of one numberHistogramA line, which implies continuity

Key knowledge

Formats and conventions that are marked

  • Title saying what the chart shows, not what it is
  • Both axes labelled, with units
  • A legend only when more than one series
  • Colour used for one meaning only
  • Source and date stated
Titles, before and after

Weak: “Rainfall chart”

Strong: “Cape Otway received three times the rainfall of Mildura in 2025”

Worked example

I do What is wrong with this one?

The chart

A column chart of mean maximum temperature by region. The vertical axis runs from 16 to 25 °C. Mallee's column looks roughly eight times the height of the Western District's.

  1. 1 The real figures are 24.4 and 16.4 — a difference of about half as much again.
  2. 2 The axis starts at 16, not zero, so the visible bar is only the part above 16.
  3. 3 A reader comparing heights reads an eightfold difference that does not exist.
  4. ✓ Truncating an axis is not always wrong — but on a column chart, where height is the comparison, it is.
The rule worth memorising

Columns compare by area and must start at zero. Lines compare by slope and may be truncated, if you say so.

1. A pie chart whose slices total 140 per cent

Not parts of one whole — wrong chart type entirely

2. Daily temperature for a year as 365 columns

Should be a line — it is a trend over time

3. Red for “rainfall” in one chart and “danger” in the next

Colour carrying two meanings in one document

The question behind all three

What is a reader likely to conclude, and is that conclusion true?

Non-functional requirement

Readable by the person who has to read it

  • Never colour alone — about one boy in twelve cannot distinguish red from green
  • Enough contrast between text and background
  • Type large enough for the viewing distance
  • Plain language in labels
Why this is assessed

“Readable by a Year 7 student in a corridor” is a non-functional requirement. Accessibility is not an extra — it is the requirement being met or not met.
