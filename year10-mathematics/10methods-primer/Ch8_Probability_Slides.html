LESSON 1 — SECTIONS 8A/8B

Review of probability and Venn diagrams

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • calculate probabilities as favourable ÷ total
  • use complement, union ∪ and intersection ∩
  • fill and read a Venn diagram

🗣 New words first

Word / symbolSay itMeaning
sample spaceSAM-pul spayssthe list of ALL possible outcomes
eventee-VENTa set of outcomes we care about, like “rolling an even number”
complement A′KOM-ple-menteverything NOT in A — Pr(A′) = 1 − Pr(A)
A ∩ BA intersection Bin A AND in B — the overlap
A ∪ BA union Bin A OR in B (or both)
Venn diagramVENcircles showing how two events overlap

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — probability and the Venn regions

ProbabilityPr(event) = favourable outcomestotal outcomes
The four regionsonly A · both (A ∩ B — shaded) · only B · neither. The four numbers always add to the total.
Fill ruleAlways put the “both” number in first, then subtract to find “only” regions.

Worked example 1

Worked exampleIn a class of 30 students, 18 play soccer (S), 14 play basketball (B) and 8 play both. Fill the Venn diagram and find Pr(neither).
1Both first: S ∩ B = 8 goes in the overlap.
2Only S: 18 − 8 = 10. Only B: 14 − 8 = 6.
3Neither: 30 − (10 + 8 + 6) = 6.
4Pr(neither): 630 = 15

Your turn

Your turnUsing the same Venn diagram, find Pr(S ∪ B) — a student plays soccer OR basketball (or both).
Hint: add the three regions inside the circles.
✔ Pr(S ∪ B) = 10 + 8 + 630 = 2430 = 45

  • 8A fluency: dice, cards, spinners — favourable ÷ total
  • 8B: fill Venn diagrams — “both” number first, always
  • Say it: “A intersection B means A AND B.”

LESSON 3 — SECTION 8C

The addition rule

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • use Pr(A ∪ B) = Pr(A) + Pr(B) − Pr(A ∩ B)
  • explain why the overlap is subtracted
  • recognise mutually exclusive events

🗣 New words first

Word / symbolSay itMeaning
addition rule—Pr(A ∪ B) = Pr(A) + Pr(B) − Pr(A ∩ B)
mutually exclusiveMYOO-choo-a-lee ex-KLOO-sivevents that cannot both happen — no overlap, Pr(A ∩ B) = 0
double counting—the error the rule fixes: the overlap got counted twice

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — subtract the overlap

The rulePr(A ∪ B) = Pr(A) + Pr(B) − Pr(A ∩ B)
Why subtract?Adding Pr(A) and Pr(B) counts the shaded overlap twice — subtract it once to fix the double count.
Mutually exclusiveNo overlap → Pr(A ∪ B) = Pr(A) + Pr(B). Simple version!

Worked example 3

Worked examplePr(A) = 0.5, Pr(B) = 0.4 and Pr(A ∩ B) = 0.2. Find Pr(A ∪ B).
1Write the rule: Pr(A ∪ B) = Pr(A) + Pr(B) − Pr(A ∩ B)
2Substitute: = 0.5 + 0.4 − 0.2
3Answer: Pr(A ∪ B) = 0.7
4Check with the Venn: 0.3 + 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.7 ✔

Your turn

Your turnEvents C and D are mutually exclusive with Pr(C) = 0.3 and Pr(D) = 0.45. Find Pr(C ∪ D), and state Pr(C ∩ D).
Hint: mutually exclusive → the overlap is empty.
✔ Pr(C ∩ D) = 0 · Pr(C ∪ D) = 0.3 + 0.45 = 0.75
📘 Your work: Exercise 8C
  • Addition rule questions — write the rule line first
  • Spot “mutually exclusive” — it sets the overlap to 0
  • Rearranged versions: find Pr(A ∩ B) given the other three

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • understand “given that” questions
  • use Pr(A|B) = Pr(A ∩ B) ÷ Pr(B)
  • find conditional probabilities from Venn diagrams and tables

🗣 New words first

Word / symbolSay itMeaning
given thatGIV-enwe already know this happened — “given that the student plays basketball…”
Pr(A | B)probability of A given Bthe chance of A when we know B happened
reduced sample spacere-DYOOSDthe new, smaller total — only the B outcomes count now

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — shrink the world to B

The rulePr(A | B) = Pr(A ∩ B)Pr(B)
In words“Given B” means: B is the new total (shaded). Count only inside B.
“Given that ___ happened, the total is now ___, and the favourable part is ___.”

Worked example 4

Worked exampleFrom Lesson 1: 10 play only soccer, 8 both, 6 only basketball, 6 neither. Find Pr(plays soccer | plays basketball).
1Given basketball: new total = everyone in B = 8 + 6 = 14.
2Favourable inside B: soccer AND basketball = 8.
3Answer: Pr(S | B) = 814 = 47
4Compare: Pr(S) = 18/30 = 3/5 — knowing B changed the probability!

Your turn

Your turnSame data: find Pr(plays basketball | plays soccer).
Hint: new total = everyone in S = 10 + 8 = 18.
✔ Pr(B | S) = 818 = 49 — different from Pr(S | B)! Order matters.
📘 Your work: Exercise 8D
  • Underline “given that” in every question — it names the new total
  • From tables: the given event picks the ROW or COLUMN
  • Pr(A|B) ≠ Pr(B|A): check which is asked

Word / symbolSay itMeaning

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — the tree rule

SetupA bag has 3 red and 2 blue counters. Two draws with replacement — the probabilities stay 3/5 and 2/5 on every branch.
The ruleMultiply along a path: Pr(RR) = 3/5 × 3/5 = 9/25. Add between paths for “or”. All four paths sum to 1 ✔

Worked example 6

Worked exampleUsing the tree (3 red, 2 blue, with replacement), find Pr(the two counters are different colours).
1Which paths? RB and BR.
2Multiply along each: Pr(RB) = 35 × 25 = 625;   Pr(BR) = 25 × 35 = 625
3Add between: 625 + 625 = 1225

Your turn

Your turnSame bag (3 red, 2 blue), but now without replacement. Find Pr(both red).
Hint: after taking a red, only 2 red remain out of 4 counters.
✔ Pr(RR) = 35 × 24 = 620 = 310

LESSON 7 — SECTION 8G

Independent events

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • say what independent means
  • test independence: does Pr(A ∩ B) = Pr(A) × Pr(B)?
  • connect independence to “with replacement”

🗣 New words first

Word / symbolSay itMeaning
independentin-de-PEN-dentone event does not change the chance of the other
dependentde-PEN-dentthe first event changes the second — like drawing without replacement
testtesta check with numbers — not a guess

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — the multiplication test

Test for independence A, B independent ⟺ Pr(A ∩ B) = Pr(A) × Pr(B)
Also trueIf independent, Pr(A | B) = Pr(A) — knowing B tells you nothing about A.
Everyday linkWith replacement → independent. Without replacement → dependent.

Worked example 7

Worked examplePr(A) = 0.5, Pr(B) = 0.4, Pr(A ∩ B) = 0.2. Are A and B independent?
1Test: Pr(A) × Pr(B) = 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2
2Compare: Pr(A ∩ B) = 0.2 — the same!
3Conclusion in words: “Since Pr(A ∩ B) = Pr(A) × Pr(B), the events are independent.”

Your turn

Your turnPr(C) = 0.6, Pr(D) = 0.3, Pr(C ∩ D) = 0.12. Independent?
Hint: 0.6 × 0.3 = ?
✔ 0.6 × 0.3 = 0.18 ≠ 0.12 → not independent (dependent). Write the comparison line — that IS the answer.
📘 Your work: Exercise 8G
  • Run the multiplication test and write the conclusion sentence
  • From tables: find the three probabilities first
  • Conclusion frame: “Since ___ = / ≠ ___, the events are ___.”

Key idea — order matters

ArrangementsChoosing r from n where order matters: start at n and multiply r falling numbers.

arrangements = n × (n−1) × …   (r factors) = n!(n−r)!
Spot itWords like 1st/2nd/3rd, president/secretary, code, ranking → order matters → arrangement.

3Answer: 336 ways

Selections

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • count selections where order does NOT matter
  • use the nCr formula (and calculator button)
  • decide: arrangement or selection?

Key idea — divide out the repeats

SelectionsChoosing r from n where order does NOT matter:

ⁿCᵣ = n!r!(n−r)!
Why divide by r!Every group of r was counted r! times as arrangements — divide the repeats out.
Spot itWords like choose, select, committee (no roles), team, hand of cards → selection.

Worked example 10

Worked exampleA committee of 3 is chosen from 7 students (no roles). How many committees are possible?
1No roles → order does not matter → selection.
2Formula: ⁷C₃ = 7 × 6 × 53 × 2 × 1
3Calculate: 210 ÷ 6 = 35 committees

Your turna) How many ways to choose 2 toppings from 10?   b) Arrangement or selection: “choose a captain and vice-captain from 12”?
