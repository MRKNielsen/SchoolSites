LESSON 1 — SECTION 3A

Review of index laws

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • name the base and the index
  • use the three main index laws
  • simplify expressions with several variables

🗣 New words first

Word / symbolSay itMeaning
index / indicesIN-dex / IN-di-seezthe small raised number: the 5 in 3⁵ (plural: indices)
basebayssthe big number being multiplied: the 3 in 3⁵
powerPOW-eranother word for index; 3⁵ is “3 to the power 5”
simplifySIM-pli-fywrite with each base appearing once

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — the index laws

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
a⁰ = 1(ab)ᵐ = aᵐbᵐ(\(\dfrac{a}{b}\))ᵐ = \(\dfrac{aᵐ}{bᵐ}\)
In wordsmultiply → add the indices · divide → subtract · power of a power → multiply. Same base only!

Worked example 1

Worked exampleSimplify: a) 3x²y × 4x³y²   b) (2m³)⁴ ÷ 8m⁷
1a) Numbers first: 3 × 4 = 12; then add indices: x²⁺³ = x⁵, y¹⁺² = y³
2Answer: 12x⁵y³
3b) Power of a power: (2m³)⁴ = 2⁴m¹² = 16m¹²
4Divide: 16m¹² ÷ 8m⁷ = 2m⁵

Your turn

Your turnSimplify: a) 5a⁴b × 2ab³   b) (3k²)³ ÷ 9k⁴
Hint: b) (3k²)³ = 27k⁶.
✔ a) 10a⁵b⁴   b) 27k⁶ ÷ 9k⁴ = 3k²

  • Write which law you used next to each answer
  • Careful: 2x⁰ = 2 × 1 = 2, but (2x)⁰ = 1

LESSON 2 — SECTION 3B

Negative indices

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • explain what a negative index means
  • rewrite with positive indices
  • evaluate numbers with negative indices

🗣 New words first

Word / symbolSay itMeaning
negative index—a⁻ⁿ means “1 over aⁿ” — NOT a negative number!
reciprocalre-SIP-ro-kal1 over the number: the reciprocal of 4 is ¼
positive index form—written with no negative indices

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — negative means reciprocal

a⁻ⁿ = \(\dfrac{1}{aⁿ}\) \(\dfrac{1}{a⁻ⁿ}\) = aⁿ
Why?Keep the divide-law pattern: a² ÷ a³ = a⁻¹, and also = \(\dfrac{a \times a}{a \times a \times a} = \dfrac{1}{a}\). So a⁻¹ = \(\dfrac{1}{a}\) ✔
Careful5⁻² = \(\dfrac{1}{25}\) — positive! The minus moves the term downstairs; it does not make it negative.

Worked example 2

Worked examplea) Write 4x⁻³ and \(\dfrac{2}{y⁻^{2}}\) with positive indices.   b) Evaluate 5⁻².
1a) Only the x has the negative index: 4x⁻³ = \(\dfrac{4}{x^{3}}\) (the 4 stays upstairs!)
2Downstairs negative comes up: \(\dfrac{2}{y⁻^{2}}\) = 2y²
3b) 5⁻² = \(\dfrac{1}{5^{2}} = \dfrac{1}{25}\)

Your turn

Your turna) Evaluate 3⁻³.   b) Write 7x⁻¹y² with positive indices.
Hint: only x has the negative index in b).
✔ a) \(\dfrac{1}{27}\)   b) \(\dfrac{7y^{2}}{x}\)

Key idea — one digit before the point

The forma × 10ⁿ,   1 ≤ a < 10 — exactly ONE non-zero digit before the decimal point.
Direction384 000 → the point moves 5 left → 10⁵.   0.00062 → 4 right → 10⁻⁴.

3b) Numbers together, powers together: 3 × 2 = 6; 10⁸ × 10⁻³ = 10⁵

LESSON 4 — SECTION 3D (10A)

Fractional indices

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • read a fractional index as a root
  • evaluate numbers with fractional indices
  • swap between root form and index form

🗣 New words first

Word / symbolSay itMeaning
fractional indexfrak-SHUN-ala power that is a fraction: 8^(1/3)
square root √ / cube root ∛—the number that squares (cubes) to give yours
ⁿ\(\sqrt{a}\)the n-th root of athe number that gives a when raised to the power n

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — a root is a power

a^\(\dfrac{1}{n}\) = ⁿ\(\sqrt{a}\) a^\(\dfrac{m}{n}\) = (ⁿ\(\sqrt{a}\))ᵐ
Why?The laws demand it: (a^½)² = a¹ = a — so a^½ squares to a. That is exactly what \(\sqrt{a}\) does!
Order tipFor 16^(3/4): root FIRST (⁴\(\sqrt{16}\) = 2), then power (2³ = 8). Small numbers, easy work.

Worked example 4

Worked exampleEvaluate: a) 8^(1/3)   b) 16^(3/4)   c) Write \(\sqrt{x^{5}}\) in index form.
1a) 8^(1/3) = ∛8 = 2 (2 × 2 × 2 = 8)
2b) Root first: ⁴\(\sqrt{16}\) = 2, then 2³ = 8
3c) \(\sqrt{x^{5}}\) = x^(5/2) (root index goes on the bottom)

Your turn

Your turnEvaluate: a) 27^(2/3)   b) 25^(−1/2)
Hint: b) negative index first: 1 over 25^(1/2).
✔ a) (∛27)² = 3² = 9   b) \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{25}} = \dfrac{1}{5}\)

LESSON 5 — SECTION 3E

Exponential equations

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • write both sides with the same base
  • match the indices to solve
  • handle fractional answers confidently

Key idea — same base, match powers

Key ideaIf aˣ = aʸ (same base), then x = y. So: rewrite both sides with the same base, then set the indices equal.
Base spotting4, 8, 16, 32 are all powers of 2 · 9, 27, 81 are powers of 3 · 25, 125 are powers of 5.
“Both sides can be written with base ___, so I match the powers.”

Worked example 5

Worked exampleSolve: a) 2ˣ = 32   b) 9ˣ = 27
1a) Same base: 32 = 2⁵, so 2ˣ = 2⁵ → x = 5
2b) Base 3 both sides: 9ˣ = (3²)ˣ = 3²ˣ and 27 = 3³
3Match powers: 2x = 3
4Solve: x = \(\dfrac{3}{2}\) (fractions are normal here!)

Your turn

Hint: b) both are powers of 2: 2³ˣ = 2⁴.
✔ a) x = 3   b) 3x = 4 → x = \(\dfrac{4}{3}\)

LESSON 6 — SECTION 4A (10A)

Irrational numbers including surds

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • sort numbers into rational and irrational
  • explain what a surd is
  • simplify surds using square factors

🗣 New words first

Word / symbolSay itMeaning
rationalRASH-on-alcan be written as a fraction of whole numbers: ½, 0.75, 7
irrationalir-RASH-on-alcannot — the decimals never repeat or stop: π, \(\sqrt{2}\)
surdserdan irrational root, like \(\sqrt{2}\) or \(\sqrt{12}\) (but \(\sqrt{9}\) = 3 is NOT a surd)
square factor—a factor that is a perfect square: 4, 9, 16, 25, …
exacteg-ZAKTthe true value — keep the √, do not round

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — surds are real lengths

\(\sqrt{2}\) exists!A right triangle with two sides of 1 has hypotenuse exactly \(\sqrt{2}\) (Pythagoras). The decimal never ends — but the length is real and exact.
Simplifying surds\(\sqrt{a \times b} = \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}\)
Pull out the biggest square factor: \(\sqrt{12} = \sqrt{4} \times \sqrt{3} = 2 \sqrt{3}\).

Worked example 6

Worked exampleSimplify: a) \(\sqrt{12}\)   b) \(\sqrt{50}\)   c) \(\sqrt{72}\)
1a) Biggest square factor of 12 is 4: \(\sqrt{12} = \sqrt{4} \times \sqrt{3}\) = 2\(\sqrt{3}\)
2b) 50 = 25 × 2: \(\sqrt{50} = 5 \sqrt{2}\)
3c) 72 = 36 × 2: \(\sqrt{72}\) = 6\(\sqrt{2}\) (36 beats 9 or 4 — go biggest!)

Your turn

Your turnSimplify: a) \(\sqrt{18}\)   b) \(\sqrt{75}\)
Hint: 18 = 9 × 2; 75 = 25 × 3.
✔ a) 3\(\sqrt{2}\)   b) 5\(\sqrt{3}\)
📘 Your work: Exercise 4A
  • Sort rational/irrational first, then simplify surds
  • Memorise the squares: 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100
  • Frame: “The biggest square factor of ___ is ___.”

LESSON 7 — SECTION 4B (10A)

Adding and subtracting surds

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • identify like surds
  • simplify before adding
  • add and subtract like surds only

Key idea — like surds, like like terms

Key ideaOnly like surds add: 3\(\sqrt{2} + 5 \sqrt{2} = 8 \sqrt{2}\) — exactly like 3x + 5x = 8x. But 3\(\sqrt{2} + 5 \sqrt{3}\) stays as it is.
Hidden likes\(\sqrt{27}\) and \(\sqrt{3}\) look different — but \(\sqrt{27} = 3 \sqrt{3}\). Simplify first, then look again!
Warning\(\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16}\) = 3 + 4 = 7, but \(\sqrt{25}\) = 5. So \(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}\) ≠ \(\sqrt{a+b}\). Never add under the root!

Worked example 7

Worked exampleSimplify: a) 3\(\sqrt{2} + 5 \sqrt{2} - \sqrt{2}\)   b) \(\sqrt{27} + 2 \sqrt{3}\)
1a) All like surds (\(\sqrt{2}\)): 3 + 5 − 1 = 7 → 7\(\sqrt{2}\)
2b) Simplify first: \(\sqrt{27} = \sqrt{9} \times \sqrt{3} = 3 \sqrt{3}\)
3Now they match: 3\(\sqrt{3} + 2 \sqrt{3}\) = 5\(\sqrt{3}\)

Your turn

Your turnSimplify: a) 5\(\sqrt{5} - 2 \sqrt{5}\)   b) \(\sqrt{8} + 3 \sqrt{2}\)
Hint: b) \(\sqrt{8} = 2 \sqrt{2}\).
✔ a) 3\(\sqrt{5}\)   b) 2\(\sqrt{2} + 3 \sqrt{2}\) = 5\(\sqrt{2}\)
📘 Your work: Exercise 4B
  • Like surds first, then simplify-first questions
  • Circle the surd part — the numbers in front do the arithmetic
  • Frame: “___ and ___ are like surds because both have √___.”

LESSON 8 — SECTION 4C (10A)

Multiplying and dividing surds

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • multiply and divide surds
  • expand brackets containing surds
  • always simplify the final answer

Key idea — roots multiply happily

\(\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}\) \(\dfrac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{a}{b}}\) \(\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{a}\) = a
RoutineNumbers × numbers, surds × surds — then simplify the result. (Unlike adding, multiplying is allowed under the root!)

Worked example 8

Worked exampleSimplify: a) 2\(\sqrt{3} \times 5 \sqrt{6}\)   b) \(\sqrt{2}\)(3 + \(\sqrt{2}\))   c) 6\(\sqrt{10} \div 2 \sqrt{5}\)
1a) 2 × 5 = 10 and \(\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{6} = \sqrt{18}\) → 10\(\sqrt{18}\)
2Simplify: \(\sqrt{18} = 3 \sqrt{2}\) → 10 × 3\(\sqrt{2}\) = 30\(\sqrt{2}\)
3b) Expand: \(\sqrt{2} \times 3 + \sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2} = 3 \sqrt{2}\) + 2
4c) 6 ÷ 2 = 3 and \(\sqrt{10} \div \sqrt{5} = \sqrt{2}\) → 3\(\sqrt{2}\)

Your turn

Your turnSimplify: a) 3\(\sqrt{2} \times 4 \sqrt{8}\)   b) \(\sqrt{3}\)(2\(\sqrt{3}\) − 1)
Hint: a) \(\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{16}\) = 4 — no surd survives!
✔ a) 12 × 4 = 48   b) 2 × 3 − \(\sqrt{3}\) = 6 − \(\sqrt{3}\)
📘 Your work: Exercise 4C
  • Multiply, divide, then expand-the-bracket questions
  • ALWAYS check: can the answer simplify further?
  • Challenge: (2 + \(\sqrt{3}\))(2 − \(\sqrt{3}\)) — what happens to the surd?

LESSON 9 — SECTION 4D (10A)

Rationalising the denominator

🎯 Learning intentions — today I will…

  • explain why we rationalise
  • multiply by \(\sqrt{b}\) over \(\sqrt{b}\)
  • write answers in simplest exact form

🗣 New words first

Word / symbolSay itMeaning
rationaliseRASH-on-a-lyzremove the surd from the denominator (the bottom)
equivalent fractionee-KWIV-a-lentsame value, different look — like ½ = 2⁄4
simplest form—no surds downstairs, fraction fully cancelled

✏ Copy new words into your maths glossary. Write the word in your home language too.

Key idea — multiply by 1, cleverly

\(\dfrac{a}{\sqrt{b}} \times \dfrac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}} = \dfrac{a\sqrt{b}}{b}\)
Why it works\(\dfrac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}}\) = 1, so the value never changes — but \(\sqrt{b} \times \sqrt{b}\) = b kills the surd downstairs.
ConventionExact answers are written with rational denominators — examiners expect it.

Worked example 9

Worked exampleRationalise: a) \(\dfrac{5}{\sqrt{3}}\)   b) \(\dfrac{3\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{5}}\)
1a) Multiply top and bottom by \(\sqrt{3}\): \(\dfrac{5\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{5\sqrt{3}}{3}\)
2b) Multiply by \(\sqrt{5}\): \(\dfrac{3\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{5}}{5}\)
3Multiply the surds: \(\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{5} = \sqrt{10}\) → answer \(\dfrac{3\sqrt{10}}{5}\)

Your turn

Your turnRationalise: a) \(\dfrac{4}{\sqrt{7}}\)   b) \(\dfrac{6}{2\sqrt{3}}\)
Hint: b) cancel 6 ÷ 2 = 3 first, then rationalise \(\dfrac{3}{\sqrt{3}}\).
✔ a) \(\dfrac{4\sqrt{7}}{7}\)   b) \(\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{3}\) = \(\sqrt{3}\)

  • Rationalise, then simplify — every answer surd-free downstairs
  • Cancel BEFORE rationalising when you can
