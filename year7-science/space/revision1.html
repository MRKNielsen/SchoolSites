Exam technique · 2 min

Read the command word, dodge the traps

What the question wants

WordWhat to write
State / NameJust the fact. No explanation needed.
DescribeWhat it is like, or what happens.
ExplainWhat happens AND why. Use "because" and "so".
CalculateRule, numbers in, answer with units. Round as asked.

Check the marks: 2 marks usually means two separate points.

Six traps that lose marks

Rotate ≠ revolve. Rotate = spin on the axis. Revolve = orbit the Sun.

Seasons come from tilt, never from distance to the Sun.

Phases are not Earth's shadow. We see different amounts of the lit half.

From Australia, a growing (waxing) Moon is lit on the LEFT.

Spring tides have nothing to do with the season spring.

Units and rounding. If it says "in seconds" or "nearest whole number", do exactly that.

Teacher notes

Spend no more than two minutes. Ask students to underline the command word in every question for the rest of the lesson, and in the test itself. Each trap on the right comes back in a task later in the lesson.

Day, night and seasons · 4 min · On your own

Read the diagram: places P and Q

1

Which place, P or Q, is in daylight right now? [1]

2

Which place is having summer? Use the tilt of the axis to explain. [2]

3

Roughly which month of the year could this be? [1]

4

Fix this sentence: "Earth revolves on its axis once every 24 hours." [1]

Teacher notes

Note the Sun is on the RIGHT this time, so students cannot rely on a remembered picture: they have to find the lit half from the direction of the light. The hemisphere whose pole leans toward the Sun is having summer. Question 4 targets the rotate/revolve confusion directly.

Booklet §2.1–2.2 p.8–9

Answers · Places P and Q

Find the light first, then the tilt

1 · P is in daylight

Sunlight comes from the right, so the right half of Earth is day. P is on that half; Q is on the night side.

2 · P is having summer

The South Pole leans toward the Sun, so the Southern Hemisphere (where P is) gets more direct sunlight and longer days.

3 · Around December

Southern Hemisphere tilted toward the Sun means Australian summer: about December to February.

4 · Rotates, not revolves

"Earth rotates on its axis once every 24 hours." It revolves around the Sun once every 365.25 days.

Teacher notes

If a student said Q is in summer because it is nearer the top of the picture, or because it is "closer" to anything, go straight back to the rule: which pole leans toward the Sun.

Day, night and seasons · 5 min · Pairs

Earth's year in four positions

a At which position is Melbourne having summer?

b Follow the arrows. At which position is Melbourne having spring?

c At which two positions are day and night equal everywhere?

d Japan is in the Northern Hemisphere. At which position is Japan having winter?

e "Melbourne is cold in July because Earth is furthest from the Sun then." Earth really is furthest in July. So what is wrong with this reasoning?

Teacher notes

Strategy to model: first find the position where the South Pole end leans toward the Sun (that is Melbourne's summer). Then follow the arrows: summer, autumn, winter, spring. The positions are numbered out of order on purpose so students must reason rather than count. Question e is the key misconception: the fact (Earth is about 3% further away in early July) is true, but it is not the cause.

Booklet §2.2–2.3 p.9–10

Answers · Four positions

Which pole leans toward the Sun?

a · Position 2. The South Pole end leans toward the Sun.

b · Position 3. Along the arrows: 2 summer, 4 autumn, 1 winter, 3 spring.

c · Positions 4 and 3. The equinoxes: neither pole leans toward the Sun.

d · Position 2. When the south leans in, the north leans away. Hemispheres always have opposite seasons.

e · A true fact, but the wrong cause

Earth is only about 3% further from the Sun in July, which makes very little difference. Melbourne is cold because the Southern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun: the Sun sits lower, its rays are spread over more ground, and days are shorter. The proof: in July the Northern Hemisphere has summer, at exactly the same distance.

Teacher notes

Ask two students to read their answer to e aloud and have the class judge it: does it name the tilt, describe what the tilt does (angle of rays, length of day), and rule out distance? Those are the marking points for any seasons explanation.

Teacher notes

Two minutes, teacher-led. Trace the orbit with a finger from New, following the arrows: the lit part we can see grows (waxing) until Full on the far side of Earth, then shrinks (waning). Each Moon on the left-hand diagram is drawn as it looks from Earth at that position (as seen from Melbourne). Remind students that in reality half the Moon is always lit on the side facing the Sun; the shape is only our view of that lit half. The next slide asks students to apply this to six positions, so leave this slide up for a moment and then move on.

Teacher notes

Sunlight comes from the right this time and positions 2, 3, 5 and 6 are on the diagonals, so each is a crescent or a gibbous phase; positions 1 and 4 are the new and full Moon positions; the numbers run in order around the orbit, following the arrows, which students should use as anchors. Method to model: (1) new Moon is on the Sun's side of Earth, full Moon on the far side; (2) follow the arrows from new: waxing first, then full, then waning; (3) a Moon closer to the Sun's side than the quarter points is a crescent, further away is gibbous. For the drawings: from Australia the waxing Moon is lit on the LEFT and the waning Moon on the RIGHT, the opposite of the Northern Hemisphere.

Tides · 3 min · Pairs

Three line-ups: spring or neap?

(i)

Sun · Moon · Earth in a line

(ii)

Moon at right angles to the Sun–Earth line

(iii)

Sun · Earth · Moon in a line

1 Which line-up(s) cause spring tides? Name the Moon's phase in each.

2 Which causes neap tides? Why are neap tides smaller?

3 New Moon on 2 November. About when are the next neap tides?

4 High tide is at 6 am. When is the next one, and which motion causes it?

5 If the Moon vanished, would tides stop? Explain.

Teacher notes

Link back to the phases slide: line-up (i) is a new Moon and (iii) is a full Moon, so spring tides come twice a month, roughly every 14–15 days. Question 5 tests whether students understand that the bulge is caused by gravity: the Moon's gravity is the main cause, but the Sun's gravity also pulls on the oceans.

Booklet §3.2 p.12–13

Answers · Tides

In a line: pulls add. At right angles: pulls fight.

1 · (i) and (iii). (i) is a new Moon, (iii) a full Moon. The Sun and Moon pull along the same line, so the effects add: the highest highs and lowest lows.

2 · (ii). A first or third quarter Moon. The Sun's pull is at right angles to the Moon's, so they partly cancel: a smaller tidal range.

3 · About 9 November. A quarter of the 29.5-day cycle is about 7 days later, at first quarter.

4 · About 6:25 pm. Earth spinning on its axis carries the beach through the second bulge, on the side away from the Moon. Two bulges, so two high tides a day.

5 · No, but they would be much smaller. The Moon's gravity pulling on the oceans makes the main bulge. The Sun's gravity also pulls on the oceans, so there would still be small tides.

Teacher notes

For question 4, accept "about 12 hours later". The extra 25 minutes or so is because the Moon moves along its orbit while Earth turns; this is extension, not required. The required idea is that the daily rhythm comes from Earth's rotation through two bulges.

Calculations · 2 min · I do

Rule, numbers in, calculate, units

How many times wider?

Jupiter is 139,820 km across. Earth is 12,742 km. How many times wider is Jupiter? Nearest whole number.

times wider = bigger ÷ smaller

= 139,820 ÷ 12,742

= 10.97…

≈ 11 times wider

AU to kilometres

Mars is 1.52 AU from the Sun. 1 AU = 150,000,000 km. How far is Mars from the Sun in km?

km = AU × 150,000,000

= 1.52 × 150,000,000

= 228,000,000 km

Light travel time

Jupiter is 780,000,000 km from the Sun. Light travels 300,000 km/s. How long does sunlight take to get there?

time = distance ÷ speed

= 780,000,000 ÷ 300,000

= 2,600 s (43 min 20 s)

Teacher notes

Model these on the board quickly; the method matters more than the numbers. Point out that the working earns marks even if the final answer slips. Common errors: dividing the small number by the big one for "times wider"; forgetting to convert seconds to minutes when asked (divide by 60, the remainder is seconds); losing zeros. Tip: cancel the same number of zeros top and bottom before dividing, e.g. 780,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 7,800 ÷ 3.

Explain it · 5 min · On your own

Two marks means two points, joined by "because"

A · Planet temperatures

Mercury is closer to the Sun than Venus. Yet Mercury's night side drops to −180 °C, while Venus stays at about 465 °C day and night. Explain why. [2]

B · Space debris

A fleck of paint once chipped a window on the International Space Station. Explain how something so tiny could do damage, and why the crew could not steer around it. [2]

C · Sky knowledge

A friend says the dark patches along the Milky Way are "just empty gaps". Correct them, and explain how Aboriginal astronomers use these dark shapes as a calendar. [2]

Sentence frame

"This happens because … This means that …"

Teacher notes

Card A uses the same physics as the Moon's huge temperature range: an atmosphere traps and spreads heat; without one, the day side bakes and the night side freezes. If students finish early, ask them to write one sentence applying the same idea to the Moon. Card C: accept any accurate dark-constellation example; the Emu in the Sky is the one in the booklet.

Model answers · Explain it

Underline the two marking points in yours

A · Planet temperatures

Venus has a very thick carbon dioxide atmosphere that traps heat and carries it right around the planet, so the night side stays hot. Mercury has almost no atmosphere, so heat escapes to space as soon as the Sun sets.

Distance decides how much energy arrives. The atmosphere decides how much stays.

B · Space debris

Objects in orbit move at about 7.8 km/s (up to 15 km/s head-on), so even a tiny mass hits with huge energy. Pieces this small are too small to track, so there is no warning to move out of the way.

Bonus: one hit can make more fragments, leading to more collisions (Kessler syndrome).

C · Sky knowledge

The dark patches are real clouds of gas and dust blocking the light of stars behind them. Western constellations join bright stars; dark constellations like the Emu in the Sky are shapes made by these clouds. The Emu's position at dusk changes through the year and signals events on Country, such as when emus lay their eggs.

Teacher notes

Have students swap books and underline the two marking points (in bold on screen) in each other's answers. An answer that only says "Venus is hotter because of its atmosphere" gets 1 mark: it needs the contrast with Mercury, or the mechanism (trapping heat), for the second.

Booklet §2.2–2.3 p.9–10

Model answer · Swap and mark out of 4

Same tilt, opposite sides of the Sun

South leans in: Melbourne summer, Japan winter

1 mark Diagram: Sun, two positions, same tilt, labelled

1 mark July: north tilted toward the Sun, so Japan has summer

1 mark July: south tilted away, so Melbourne has winter

1 mark Why: direct rays, longer days (not distance)

Model explanation

At position A (July), Earth's axis leans so the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun. Japan gets the Sun high in the sky, its rays hit more directly so the energy is concentrated, and its days are long: summer.

At the same time the Southern Hemisphere tilts away, so Melbourne gets low, spread-out sunlight and short days: winter.

At position B the axis still points the same way, but Earth is on the other side of the Sun, so the seasons swap. It is not distance: Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.

Teacher notes

Swap and mark against the four marking points. Look especially for the axis drawn the same way in both positions, and for the word "toward" or "away" attached to a named hemisphere. Many students write "Earth tilts toward the Sun in summer", which is not enough: name the hemisphere.
