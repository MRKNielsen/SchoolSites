Booklet p.35 · §7.2

Concept

The habitable zone and promising targets

The habitable zone (“Goldilocks zone”) is the range of distances from a star where liquid water can exist on a planet’s surface (neither too hot nor too cold). For our Sun, Earth sits within the habitable zone; Mars is on the outer edge.

However — liquid water can exist outside the habitable zone if there is another heat source:

  • Europa (Jupiter’s moon): an ocean of liquid water beneath 10–30 km of ice, kept liquid by tidal heating from Jupiter’s gravity. No sunlight reaches the ocean floor — but we know life thrives at Earth’s hydrothermal vents in similar conditions
  • Enceladus (Saturn’s moon): geysers of water vapour erupt from cracks in the ice; Cassini detected H₂, CO₂, and organic molecules — potential energy source for life
  • Mars: evidence of ancient liquid water (riverbeds, deltas, lake beds). Possibly still liquid water under the southern ice cap. Perseverance is collecting samples that will be returned to Earth
Extremophiles — life in harsh conditions

Extremophiles are organisms that thrive in conditions once thought impossible for life: boiling acidic hot springs, Antarctic ice, deep-ocean hydrothermal vents (no light, extreme pressure, 400°C water), highly radioactive environments. Their existence vastly expands where we search for life in the solar system.

Booklet p.37 · §7.5

Concept

Exoplanets and searching for life beyond the solar system

More than 5,500 exoplanets have been confirmed (as of 2024). Methods of detection:

  • Transit method: a planet passing in front of its star dims the starlight slightly and regularly. Kepler and TESS telescopes used this. Detects planet size and orbital period
  • Radial velocity: a planet makes its star wobble slightly; detected as Doppler shifts in starlight. Detects planet mass

Biosignatures are chemical or physical signs of life detectable from a distance:

  • Oxygen + methane together (both destroyed by each other unless being continually replenished — by life)
  • JWST has already detected CO₂ on exoplanet WASP-39b; oxygen and water detection on Earth-like planets is the next goal
How many Earths?

Statistical analysis suggests there are likely billions of Earth-sized planets in the habitable zones of Sun-like stars in the Milky Way alone. The closest potentially habitable exoplanet is Proxima Centauri b — just 4.2 light-years away. Whether it actually has liquid water, an atmosphere, or any life is unknown.

I do Worked example — the Drake equation

Estimating civilisations in the Milky Way

The Drake equation estimates the number of communicable civilisations (N) in our galaxy: N = R* × f_p × n_e × f_l × f_i × f_c × L. Using optimistic estimates: R* = 3 stars/yr; f_p = 1; n_e = 0.1; f_l = 1; f_i = 0.1; f_c = 0.1; L = 10,000 years. Calculate N.

  1. Multiply all terms\(N = 3 \times 1 \times 0.1 \times 1 \times 0.1 \times 0.1 \times 10\,000\)
  2. \(\phantom{N} = 0.003 \times 10\,000\)
  3. \(\phantom{N} = 30\ \text{civilisations}\)
Read it carefully

30 — but only on these optimistic estimates. With pessimistic ones, especially a short civilisation lifetime \(L\), \(N\) drops below 1. The Drake equation is less a calculation than a framework for naming what we don’t know.

Your turn

You do Now you try

Evaluate: Is there life in Europa’s ocean?

Using evidence from the unit, construct a scientific argument for whether life might exist in Europa’s subsurface ocean. Include: (1) what conditions exist there; (2) an Earth analogue; (3) what evidence is missing; (4) how you would get that evidence.

  1. 1 Conditions in Europa’s ocean: Liquid water (confirmed by magnetic field data and surface geology); chemical energy from tidal heating; possible hydrothermal vents at ocean-rock interface; organic compounds — all of CHNOPS elements likely present from meteorite delivery over 4.5 billion years.
  2. 2 Earth analogue: Deep-sea hydrothermal vents (e.g., Lost City field, Mid-Atlantic Ridge). No sunlight, extreme pressure, chemical-only energy — yet thriving ecosystems of bacteria, tube worms, shrimp, crabs. Life doesn’t need the Sun.
  3. ✓ Missing evidence + how to get it: We need direct sampling of the ocean. NASA’s Europa Clipper (launched 2024) will fly by 49 times, sampling the icy plumes. A future Europa Lander (proposed, 2030s+) would drill through the ice to reach the ocean. We cannot confirm or rule out life without direct sampling.

Booklet p.38 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

The Fermi paradox and SETI

The Fermi paradox (Enrico Fermi, 1950): Given the age and size of the universe, if intelligent life is even moderately common, there should be evidence of it everywhere — but we detect nothing. “Where is everybody?”

Proposed solutions:

  • Rare Earth: complex life is extraordinarily rare; Earth’s conditions (Jupiter shielding, Moon stabilising tilt, plate tectonics, magnetic field) are exceptional
  • Great Filter: some catastrophic barrier eliminates civilisations — before or after our current stage
  • They’re here but we can’t detect them: communication methods we don’t understand yet
  • The universe is too large: signals travel at light speed; we have been broadcasting for only 100 years — a 100 light-year bubble in a 100,000 light-year galaxy
SETI

The Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI) uses radio telescopes to scan the sky for non-natural signals. No confirmed signal has ever been received, but the search continues. The Breakthrough Listen initiative uses the MeerKAT array and Parkes telescope in systematic searches — Australia is part of the global hunt.

Year 8 Extension

Exoplanet detection methods in detail

Detecting exoplanets directly (imaging them) is extremely hard — the star is billions of times brighter. Indirect methods dominate:

  • Transit photometry: planet crosses star face → brightness dip of 0.01–1%. Period of dips = orbital period. Depth = planet size relative to star. Kepler found 2,662 confirmed exoplanets this way
  • Radial velocity (Doppler wobble): planet pulls star; star’s spectral lines shift slightly. Sensitive to massive planets close to star (hot Jupiters). Measures mass
  • Direct imaging: block out starlight with a coronagraph; image the faint planet. Requires large separations; works for gas giants far from star
  • Gravitational microlensing: a planet’s gravity bends background starlight; detects distant planets including Earth-mass ones
The Holy Grail: atmosphere spectroscopy

During a transit, starlight passes through the planet’s atmosphere (if it has one). Specific wavelengths are absorbed by specific molecules — detected as additional dips in the star’s spectrum. JWST has already detected CO₂ on WASP-39b. Detecting O₂ + CH₄ together would be the strongest possible biosignature — the “holy grail” of exoplanet science.

Lesson recap

What we covered

  • Life requires: liquid water, carbon chemistry, and an energy source. Extremophiles show life is possible in conditions once thought impossible
  • The habitable zone defines where liquid surface water is possible; Europa and Enceladus have liquid oceans outside this zone, heated by tidal forces
  • 5,500+ exoplanets confirmed; transit photometry and radial velocity are the main detection methods
  • JWST biosignature detection on exoplanet atmospheres is the next frontier
  • Aboriginal Australian cosmologies have always understood the sky as inhabited and relational — a different, valid framework for the question of what exists beyond our world
