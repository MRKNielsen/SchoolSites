Booklet p.36 · §6.1

Concept

From the Space Race to robotic exploration

The Space Race (1957–1969) was a Cold War competition between the USA and USSR to achieve space milestones:

  • 1957: USSR launches Sputnik 1 — first artificial satellite
  • 1961: Yuri Gagarin (USSR) — first human in orbit
  • 1969: Apollo 11 (USA) — first humans on the Moon (Neil Armstrong & Buzz Aldrin, 20 July)

Modern robotic missions explore where humans can’t (yet):

  • Mars rovers: Curiosity (2012–), Perseverance (2021–); searching for signs of past life
  • Voyager 1 & 2: launched 1977; Voyager 1 is now in interstellar space (>23 billion km from Sun)
  • JWST (2021): successor to Hubble; infrared telescope at L2 point
Crewed vs. uncrewed missions

Crewed: humans on board; higher cost and risk; humans can respond to unexpected events; greater public engagement. Uncrewed/robotic: cheaper, safer, can go to environments where humans would die; can be controlled from Earth over vast distances.

Booklet p.38 · §6.2

Concept

Australia’s role in space exploration

Australia has played a significant and often underappreciated role in space exploration:

  • Parkes radio telescope (‘The Dish’) — received and relayed live TV footage of the Apollo 11 Moon landing to 600 million viewers worldwide
  • CSIRO — invented Wi-Fi technology from radio astronomy research; operates 6 major telescopes across Australia
  • Australian Space Agency (ASA) — established 2018; growing commercial space sector
  • Square Kilometre Array (SKA) — world’s largest radio telescope network; core in WA on Wajarri Country
JWST discoveries (2022–)

JWST has imaged the earliest galaxies ever seen (just 300 million years after the Big Bang), found evidence of CO₂ on an exoplanet’s atmosphere, and revealed stunning detail in stellar nurseries. Its infrared vision sees through gas and dust that blocked Hubble. Australian astronomers contribute to its science programs.

Work through with the class

I do Worked example — evaluating a space mission

Structured evaluation

Evaluate whether sending humans to Mars is worth doing. Use evidence to argue both for and against, then reach a conclusion. (This is the type of extended response expected in the assessment task.)

  1. 1 Benefits (For): Scientific returns (geology, life search, atmospheric data); technological spin-offs (medical, materials science, comms); potential long-term human survival on another planet; international collaboration and national prestige.
  2. 2 Risks/Costs (Against): Estimated cost US$450 billion+; radiation exposure during 6-month transit; unknown health effects of Martian gravity (38% of Earth); risk of contaminating Mars with Earth microbes (planetary protection); delay would mean 7-year missions due to orbital mechanics.
  3. ✓ Conclusion (example): The scientific and long-term survival benefits justify a crewed Mars mission if costs are shared internationally. Robotic missions should continue in parallel to reduce risk and cost. Strong planetary protection protocols are essential.

Mission design brief

You’ve been asked to design a mission to Jupiter’s moon Europa. In your booklet, complete the mission design template: state the scientific goal, whether it will be crewed or uncrewed and why, what instruments you would include, and one major challenge you would need to solve.

  1. 1 Scientific goal: Search for life in Europa’s subsurface ocean beneath the ice crust. Look for organic molecules, thermal vents, and chemical energy sources.
  2. 2 Crewed or uncrewed? Uncrewed — the radiation environment around Jupiter is deadly (Europa gets 540 rem/day; fatal human dose is ~500 rem total). An uncrewed penetrator probe can drill into the ice.
  3. ✓ Challenge: Communication delay (Jupiter is 4–48 light-minutes from Earth depending on orbital position) means the probe needs to operate autonomously. Ice shell could be 10–30 km thick. Solution: AI-controlled probe + nuclear (RTG) power source.

First Nations perspectives

Wajarri Country and the Square Kilometre Array

The Square Kilometre Array (SKA), the world’s largest radio telescope when complete, is being built in the Murchison region of Western Australia — on Wajarri Yamatji Country. This site was chosen for its exceptionally radio-quiet environment (no mobile networks, minimal human-made radio interference).

The Wajarri Yamatji people have a formal Indigenous Land Use Agreement with the Australian government. Their Country has some of the darkest, quietest skies on Earth — used for millennia for sky knowledge and now hosting cutting-edge global science.

Sky Country — ancient and modern

The Wajarri people call the Milky Way Karlaya’s path. Their sky knowledge — maintained for tens of thousands of years on this Country — and the SKA share the same remarkable sky. The project requires genuine partnership: the Wajarri have veto rights over activities on their Country, and the agreement includes community benefits (employment, training, infrastructure). This is a model of how Western science can partner respectfully with Traditional Custodians.

Year 8 Extension

Rocket physics — Tsiolkovsky’s rocket equation

Rockets work by Newton’s Third Law: burning fuel is expelled backwards (thrust), and the rocket accelerates forwards (reaction). But to reach space, a rocket must overcome Earth’s gravity well — requiring enormous fuel mass.

Tsiolkovsky rocket equation

Δv = vₛ × ln(m₀/mṣ)
where Δv = change in velocity, vₛ = exhaust speed, m₀ = initial (full) mass, mṣ = final (empty) mass. This shows that to double Δv, you need to square the mass ratio — rocket fuel is very expensive in mass terms.

  • Multi-stage rockets: drop empty fuel tanks to reduce mass mid-flight (Saturn V had 3 stages)
  • Reusable rockets (SpaceX Falcon 9): land back after launch; dramatically reduce cost
Escape velocity from Earth

To escape Earth’s gravity completely (not orbit, but leave), a rocket needs 11.2 km/s (40,320 km/h). For comparison, a bullet travels about 0.9 km/s. This is why getting to space is so energy-intensive.

Year 8 Extension

Future missions — Artemis, Mars, and beyond

The coming decades will see unprecedented space exploration missions:

  • Artemis programme (NASA): return humans to the Moon by 2026; establish a lunar gateway station; prepare for Mars crewed missions in the 2030s–2040s
  • Europa Clipper (NASA, 2024): flyby mission to search for habitability in Europa’s ocean
  • Mars Sample Return: Perseverance has collected rock cores; future mission will return them to Earth for direct analysis
  • Interstellar probes: Breakthrough Starshot proposes laser-driven light sails to reach Alpha Centauri in 20 years
Australia in future space

The Australian Space Agency’s goal is to grow Australia’s space industry to A$12 billion by 2030. Australian companies are developing satellite communications, Earth observation platforms, and launch sites. Southern Cross Astronautics, Gilmour Space Technologies (QLD), and others are building Australian launch vehicles.

Section 6 · Recap

What we covered

  • Key milestones: Sputnik (1957) → Gagarin (1961) → Moon landing (1969) → ISS (1998) → Mars rovers → JWST (2021)
  • Crewed missions: higher cost/risk but humans can respond in real time. Uncrewed: cheaper, safer, can go further
  • Australia’s contributions: Parkes Dish (Moon landing comms), CSIRO (Wi-Fi & radio astronomy), SKA (Wajarri Country, WA)
  • The SKA is built on Wajarri Yamatji Country under a formal land use agreement — combining ancient sky knowledge with modern science
