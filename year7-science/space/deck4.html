Booklet p.16 · §4.1

Concept

Newton’s law of universal gravitation

Every object with mass attracts every other object with mass. This gravitational force is described by:

Newton’s law of gravitation

F = G × m₁ × m₂ ÷ r²
F = force (N), G = gravitational constant, m = masses (kg), r = distance between centres (m)

  • Double the mass of one object → gravity doubles
  • Double the distance → gravity decreases to one quarter (inverse square law)
  • Gravity is always attractive — it pulls objects together; never pushes
  • Weight is a gravitational force: W = mg (where g is gravitational field strength)
Weightlessness — the misconception

Astronauts on the ISS appear weightless not because there is no gravity (there is — about 90% of surface gravity), but because they are in free fall together with the station. They are constantly falling toward Earth, but moving forward so fast that they keep missing it.

Check

If you moved the Moon twice as far from Earth, by what factor would the gravitational force between Earth and the Moon change?

Booklet p.18 · §4.3

Concept

Orbits — speed, altitude, and satellite types

For a circular orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force needed to keep the satellite curving around Earth. Higher altitude = slower orbital speed (and longer period).

  • LEO (Low Earth Orbit, 200–2,000 km): ISS, most crewed missions; fastest (≈7.9 km/s; 90 min period)
  • MEO (Medium, 2,000–35,786 km): GPS satellites; ≈12 hour period
  • GEO (Geostationary, 35,786 km): communications & weather satellites; exactly 24 h period; appears stationary above same point on Earth’s equator
Why 35,786 km for GEO?

At this specific altitude, orbital period equals Earth’s rotation period (24 h). A satellite here stays above the same spot — ideal for communications dishes that don’t need to track.

Work through with the class

I do Worked example — gravitational force and distance

Applying the inverse square law

An astronaut weighs 700 N on Earth’s surface. Earth’s radius is 6,371 km. What is the gravitational force on the astronaut at the altitude of the ISS (408 km above Earth’s surface)?

  1. Distance from Earth’s centre\(r = 6371 + 408 = 6779\ \text{km}\)
  2. Inverse square law\(\dfrac{F_{\text{ISS}}}{F_{\text{surface}}} = \left(\dfrac{6371}{6779}\right)^{2} = 0.883\)
  3. New force\(F = 700 \times 0.883 = 618\ \text{N}\)
What this shows

That is about 88% of the astronaut’s surface weight — gravity is still very much there at ISS altitude. Weightlessness is free fall, not the absence of gravity.

Comparing gravitational forces

The Moon has a mass about 1/81 that of Earth, and a radius about 1/3.67 that of Earth. If you weigh 600 N on Earth, how much would you weigh on the Moon’s surface? (Use: g_moon / g_earth = (M_moon/M_earth) / (R_moon/R_earth)²)

  1. Ratio of surface gravities\(\dfrac{g_{\text{Moon}}}{g_{\text{Earth}}} = \dfrac{1/81}{(1/3.67)^{2}} = \dfrac{13.47}{81} = 0.166\)
  2. Weight on the Moon\(W = 600 \times 0.166 = 99.6 \approx 100\ \text{N}\)
What this shows

About one sixth of your Earth weight. Moon gravity is roughly 1.6 m/s² against Earth’s 9.8 m/s².

First Nations perspectives

Celestial navigation and the predictable sky

The fact that stars, Moon, and planets follow predictable paths is a direct consequence of gravity governing orbital motion. This regularity allowed Aboriginal Australians to use the sky for precise navigation, timing, and seasonal calendars.

The Wurundjeri and other Kulin Nation peoples navigate across Country using star positions, noting which stars rise and set where on the horizon across different seasons. The Southern Cross (Crux) — used to find true south — and the Two Pointers (Alpha and Beta Centauri) have been navigation aids for tens of thousands of years in the southern sky.

Gravity as the basis of sky knowledge

When the Boorong people recorded the positions of stars across seasons, they were implicitly relying on the same gravitational physics Newton later formalised in equations. Planetary orbits, stellar rise-times, the Moon’s cycle — all are consequences of gravity. Aboriginal sky knowledge systems are therefore applied gravitational astronomy, built through systematic observation over generations.

Year 8 Extension

Tidal locking and Lagrange points

Tidal locking occurs when a body’s rotation period equals its orbital period — one face always points at the parent body. The Moon is tidally locked to Earth (we always see the same face). Pluto and Charon are mutually tidally locked.

  • Tidal locking happens when gravitational tidal forces slow rotation until it synchronises with orbital period
  • Most large moons in the solar system are tidally locked
  • The timescale depends on distance: closer moons lock faster

Lagrange points are 5 positions where the combined gravity of two large bodies (e.g., Sun + Earth) creates a stable location for a third small body. JWST orbits at the Sun–Earth L2 point, always in Earth’s shadow — ideal for an infrared telescope.

Far side of the Moon

Because the Moon is tidally locked, humans had never seen the far side of the Moon until spacecraft orbited it (Luna 3, 1959). The far side has far more craters and fewer dark maria — a mystery still being studied. China’s Chang’e 4 landed on the far side in 2019 — the first soft landing there.

Year 8 Extension

Escape velocity and gravity wells

To escape a planet’s gravity entirely (not just orbit), an object needs escape velocity:

Escape velocity formula

vₛ = √(2GM/r)
G = gravitational constant, M = planet mass, r = distance from centre

  • Earth: 11.2 km/s | Moon: 2.4 km/s | Mars: 5.0 km/s | Jupiter: 59.5 km/s
  • Black holes: gravity so extreme that escape velocity exceeds the speed of light — nothing (not even light) can escape from within the event horizon
  • “Gravity well” visualisation: deeper well = more energy required to escape
Why the Moon has no atmosphere

The Moon’s escape velocity (2.4 km/s) is so low that gas molecules — which move at thermal speeds of 0.5–1.5 km/s — can escape over time. Any atmosphere the Moon once had has long since been lost to space. Mars (5.0 km/s) retains a thin CO₂ atmosphere but has lost most of its original air.

Lesson recap

What we covered

  • Gravitational force: F = Gm₁m₂/r²; increases with mass, decreases with distance squared (inverse square law)
  • Orbiting objects are in constant free fall — gravity + forward motion keeps them curving around Earth
  • Higher orbit = slower orbital speed; GEO (35,786 km) has a 24 h period and appears stationary
  • “Weightlessness” on ISS is free fall, not absence of gravity (astronauts experience 88% of surface gravity)
  • Aboriginal Australians used the predictable sky — a consequence of orbital mechanics — for navigation and seasonal calendars
Exit question

A satellite in GEO orbits once every 24 hours at 35,786 km altitude. If we moved it to 80,000 km altitude, would its orbital period be longer, shorter, or still 24 hours? Explain using what you know about orbital speed and altitude.
