Booklet p.18 · §3.1

Concept

Why the Moon appears to change shape

The Moon does not produce its own light — it reflects sunlight. At all times, the half of the Moon facing the Sun is lit. As the Moon orbits Earth over 29.5 days, we see different fractions of that lit half — these are the phases.

  • New Moon: Moon between Earth and Sun — dark side faces us
  • Full Moon: Earth between Moon and Sun — fully lit side faces us
  • Quarter phases: we see exactly half the lit surface
  • “Waxing” = growing; “Waning” = shrinking
Common misconception

Earth’s shadow does NOT cause phases. A lunar eclipse (when Earth’s shadow covers the Moon) is a different event. Phases are caused by our changing view of the lit half as the Moon orbits.

Check

If you were an astronaut standing on the Moon during a “New Moon” as seen from Earth, what phase of Earth would you see from the Moon?

Booklet p.20 · §3.2

Concept

Tides — the Moon’s gravitational pull

The Moon’s gravity pulls on Earth’s oceans, creating a bulge of water on the side of Earth facing the Moon, and a second bulge on the opposite side (due to inertia). Earth rotates through these bulges — causing two high tides and two low tides per day.

  • Spring tides: Sun, Earth, Moon aligned (new or full Moon). Sun and Moon pull together — very high tides and very low tides
  • Neap tides: Moon at right angles to Sun (first or third quarter). Tides partially cancel — moderate tides
  • “Spring” has nothing to do with the season — it means “to leap up”

Work through with the class

I do Worked example — predicting lunar phases

Using the 29.5-day cycle

The new Moon was on 1 January. What phase will the Moon be on 22 January? Will spring tides or neap tides occur on this date?

  1. 1 Count the days: From 1 Jan to 22 Jan = 21 days after new Moon.
  2. 2 Map to cycle: Full Moon ≈ 14.75 days; third quarter ≈ 22 days. So 21 days is just before third quarter. Phase: waning gibbous (nearly third quarter)
  3. 3 Tidal type: Third quarter = Sun and Moon at right angles to Earth. → Neap tides
  4. ✓ Answer: On 22 January the Moon will be a waning gibbous, approaching third quarter. Neap tides will occur (moderate tidal range).

Your turn

You do Now you try

Eclipses and phases

A lunar eclipse is occurring tonight. A solar eclipse occurred two weeks ago. Identify the phase of the Moon at each event and explain why both can’t happen on the same night.

  1. 1 Lunar eclipse: Earth must be between the Sun and Moon — this only happens at Full Moon. Earth’s shadow falls on the fully lit Moon.
  2. 2 Solar eclipse: Moon must be between Earth and the Sun — this only happens at New Moon. The Moon blocks sunlight from reaching Earth.
  3. ✓ They can’t happen on the same night because New Moon and Full Moon are about 14–15 days apart — opposite positions in the lunar cycle. The Moon can’t be between us and the Sun AND on the far side of Earth at the same time.

Booklet p.22

First Nations perspectives

Moon knowledge in Aboriginal traditions

Many Aboriginal Australian groups have detailed knowledge of the Moon’s cycle, woven into ceremony, ecology, and governance. For many Wurundjeri women and across Kulin Nation groups, the lunar calendar has been a framework for timing ceremonies, food harvesting, and health practices.

In some traditions, the Moon is a male ancestral being who travels across the sky. His waxing and waning embody cycles of life, rest, and renewal observed over millennia of careful sky-watching.

Lunar calendars as science

Using the 29.5-day lunar cycle to organise food gathering, ceremony, and travel is applied astronomical science. The same cycle that drives tides and illuminates Country at night was used to create a sophisticated, precise calendar valid for the southern sky long before European contact.

Discuss

Why might a lunar calendar (based on Moon phases) be more practical than a solar calendar (based on the Sun’s yearly cycle) for organising monthly activities?

Booklet p.23 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

Eclipses — geometry and prediction

Eclipses don’t happen every New or Full Moon because the Moon’s orbit is tilted 5.1° relative to Earth’s orbit. The Moon only crosses the ecliptic plane at two points called nodes. Eclipses only occur when a New or Full Moon coincides with a node.

  • Umbra: the full shadow of the Moon — inside this, you see a total solar eclipse
  • Penumbra: the partial shadow — you see a partial solar eclipse
  • Saros cycle: eclipses repeat in almost the same pattern every 18 years, 11 days, 8 hours
Why the Moon fits perfectly

The Moon is 400 times smaller than the Sun, but also 400 times closer. This remarkable coincidence means the Moon’s disc appears almost exactly the same size as the Sun’s disc in Earth’s sky — making total solar eclipses possible. This is unique in our solar system.

Booklet p.23 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

Tidal forces — slowing Earth and the Moon’s recession

Earth’s tidal bulges are dragged slightly ahead of the Moon by Earth’s rotation. This tidal friction has two major effects:

  • Earth’s rotation is gradually slowing down — days are getting longer (by ~2 ms per century). 1.4 billion years ago, a day was only 18 hours
  • The Moon is moving away from Earth at about 3.8 cm per year (confirmed by laser ranging from Apollo retroreflectors)
  • Eventually (billions of years away), Earth’s rotation period will equal the Moon’s orbital period — mutual tidal locking
Apollo legacy

Astronauts left retroreflectors on the Moon’s surface. Scientists can bounce lasers off them to measure the distance to the Moon with centimetre accuracy. This directly confirms the Moon is receding at 3.8 cm/year.

Section 3 · Recap

What we covered

  • Moon phases are caused by our changing view of the lit half of the Moon as it orbits Earth — NOT Earth’s shadow
  • The eight phases (in order): New → Waxing crescent → First quarter → Waxing gibbous → Full → Waning gibbous → Third quarter → Waning crescent → New
  • Spring tides: Sun–Earth–Moon aligned (new or full Moon); very large tidal range
  • Neap tides: Moon at 90° to Sun (quarter phases); moderate tidal range
  • Lunar eclipse: Earth’s shadow on Moon (full Moon only); Solar eclipse: Moon between Earth and Sun (new Moon only)
  • Wurundjeri and other Aboriginal peoples have used the lunar cycle for ceremony, food harvesting, and governance for thousands of years
