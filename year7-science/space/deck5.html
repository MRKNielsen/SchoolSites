Starter

How would you travel to the edge of our solar system? If a spacecraft can travel at 60,000 km/h (roughly New Horizons’ speed), how many years would it take to reach Pluto (about 5.9 billion km away)? Try the calculation.

Answer

\(5{,}900{,}000{,}000 \div 60{,}000 = 98{,}333\ \text{hours}\) — that is about 4,100 days, or 11.2 years.

The real mission took 9.5 years: New Horizons launched in January 2006 and flew past Pluto in July 2015. It managed it faster by leaving Earth at about 58,000 km/h and then stealing speed from Jupiter in a gravity assist.

And Pluto is nowhere near the edge. Voyager 1 launched in 1977 and took 35 years to reach the edge of the Sun’s bubble of particles.

Booklet p.20 · §5.1

Concept

Inner planets — the terrestrial four

Terrestrial planets are small, rocky, and dense. They are close to the Sun and have few or no moons.

  • Mercury: smallest planet; no atmosphere; extreme temperature swings (−180°C to +430°C)
  • Venus: hottest planet (465°C); thick CO₂ atmosphere; rotates backwards; volcanically active
  • Earth: only known planet with liquid water; one Moon; protective magnetic field
  • Mars: largest volcano (Olympus Mons, 22 km tall); thin CO₂ atmosphere; two small moons; evidence of ancient water
Terrestrial vs Giant

Terrestrial = rocky surface + metallic core. Gas giants = mostly hydrogen and helium gas with no solid surface. Ice giants (Uranus, Neptune) = icy compounds (water, ammonia, methane) with small rocky cores.

Think

Why is Venus hotter than Mercury, even though Mercury is closer to the Sun?

Answer

Its atmosphere. Venus is wrapped in carbon dioxide at about 90× the pressure of Earth’s air. Sunlight gets in; the infrared it turns into cannot get back out. Heat builds up — a runaway greenhouse effect.

The giveaway is how even Venus is: 465 °C almost everywhere, day side and night side alike, because the thick atmosphere carries heat right around the planet.

Mercury has essentially no atmosphere, so it cannot hold heat at all: the day side reaches 430 °C and the night side falls to −180 °C.

Distance from the Sun decides how much energy arrives. The atmosphere decides how much of it stays.

Booklet p.20 · §5.1

Concept

Outer planets and astronomical units

Distances are measured in Astronomical Units (AU): 1 AU = Earth–Sun distance ≈ 150 million km. The ruler below uses a logarithmic scale so all bodies fit.

  • Jupiter: 318× Earth’s mass; 95 moons; Great Red Spot storm has lasted 400+ years
  • Saturn: ring system of ice & rock; 146 moons; less dense than water — it would float!
  • Uranus: ice giant; rotates on its side (98° tilt); faint rings
  • Neptune: fastest winds in solar system (2,100 km/h); Triton orbits backwards

Booklet p.21 · §5.2

Definition

What counts as a planet?

A planet — IAU definition, 2006

A body in our solar system is a planet only if it meets all three of these:

  1. It orbits the Sun — so a moon is out, however large.
  2. It is massive enough that its own gravity has pulled it round.
  3. It has cleared its orbital neighbourhood — it is gravitationally in charge of its own lane, having swept up or flung out everything else of comparable size.

Fail any one of the three and it is not a planet.

What “cleared” really means

Not tidiness — dominance. Asteroids still cross Earth’s orbit, but Earth outweighs everything else in its lane by about 1.7 million to one. Pluto manages roughly 0.08 to one: the rest of the Kuiper Belt in its lane outweighs Pluto.

Test Pluto against the three, one at a time:

CriterionPluto
1. Orbits the SunYes — once every 248 years✓
2. Round under its own gravityYes — 2,377 km across and clearly spherical✓
3. Cleared its orbital neighbourhoodNo — it shares the Kuiper Belt with thousands of similar icy bodies✗
Say it correctly

Pluto fails criterion 3 only. A dwarf planet is exactly that: an object that meets 1 and 2 but not 3.

“Pluto is a dwarf planet because it has not cleared its orbital neighbourhood.”

Not “because it is too small”. Size is not one of the three criteria, and Pluto passes the roundness test easily. Ceres, out in the asteroid belt, is a dwarf planet for the same reason.

The 2006 definition says “orbits the Sun”, so strictly it says nothing about planets around other stars — there is still no agreed definition for those. We meet them in Lesson 8.

Discuss

Pluto was called a planet from 1930 until 2006. Using the definition above, why was it reclassified — and does that make the earlier astronomers wrong?

What we’re listening for

Why: Pluto meets criteria 1 and 2 but fails 3. It does not dominate its lane — it shares the Kuiper Belt with thousands of similar icy bodies, and Neptune’s gravity controls its orbit rather than the other way round.

Not wrong — working with less evidence. In 1930 Pluto looked like a single object out there on its own, and was estimated to be far bigger than it turned out to be. From the 1990s astronomers found the rest of the Kuiper Belt, and in 2005 Eris, which is about Pluto’s size.

That forced a choice: call them all planets and keep adding, or tighten the definition. The IAU tightened it — and Pluto changed category without changing at all.

The idea to land: a classification is a tool, not a fact about the object. Science revising a definition when new evidence arrives is the system working, not failing.

Booklet p.21 · §5.2

Concept

Other objects in the solar system

The eight planets are not the whole story. The Sun’s gravity also holds on to a great deal of rock and ice.

  • Asteroid Belt — between Mars and Jupiter; rocky and metallic objects. Jupiter’s gravity stopped them ever forming into a planet
  • Dwarf planets — Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea, Makemake. Round, and orbiting the Sun, but they have not cleared their orbital neighbourhood — criterion 3 on the previous slide
  • Kuiper Belt — beyond Neptune, from 30 to 50 AU; icy objects, including Pluto
  • Comets — icy bodies that grow a tail of gas and dust as they approach the Sun and warm up
  • Oort Cloud — a distant spherical shell of icy objects surrounding the whole solar system; the source of long-period comets

Year 8 Extension

Kepler’s laws of planetary motion

Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) used Tycho Brahe’s precise data to derive three laws:

  • Law 1 — Ellipses: Planets orbit the Sun in ellipses, with the Sun at one focus (not the centre). Earth’s orbit is nearly circular — only slightly elliptical
  • Law 2 — Equal areas: A line from a planet to the Sun sweeps equal areas in equal times. Planets move faster when closer to the Sun
  • Law 3 — Harmonics: T² ∝ a³ (orbital period² ∝ semi-major axis³). Neptune has a longer “year” than Earth because it is farther from the Sun
Applying Law 3

If Mars is 1.52 AU from the Sun, its orbital period is: T = a¹⅛² = 1.52¹⅛² ≈ 1.87 years. A Martian year is about 687 Earth days. This is why Mars rovers experience very different mission timelines to Earth years.

Year 8 Extension

How the solar system formed — the nebular hypothesis

About 4.6 billion years ago, a giant cloud of gas and dust (a nebula) began to collapse under gravity. As it collapsed, it started to spin, flattening into a protoplanetary disc with a hot, dense centre — the proto-Sun.

  • Near the Sun: rocky planetesimals collided and accreted to form terrestrial planets (metals and silicates, as lighter elements were blown away)
  • Far from Sun: beyond the frost line (∼2.7 AU), ices remained solid; gas giants quickly grew massive enough to pull in vast amounts of hydrogen and helium
  • The Late Heavy Bombardment (4.1–3.8 Gya) scarred all inner planets with craters
Debris belts

The asteroid belt is material that was prevented from forming a planet by Jupiter’s massive gravity. The Kuiper Belt is a region of icy objects beyond Neptune; the Oort Cloud is a distant spherical shell of icy objects, source of long-period comets. Both are remnants of the early solar system.

Lesson recap

What we covered

  • 8 planets in order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars | Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune
  • Terrestrial planets: small, rocky, dense; inner solar system. Gas giants: huge, mostly hydrogen/helium; outer solar system. Ice giants (Uranus, Neptune): icy compounds round small rocky cores
  • Venus is hotter than Mercury because of its atmosphere, not its distance — distance sets how much energy arrives, the atmosphere decides how much stays
  • 1 AU = 150,000,000 km — the Earth–Sun distance; used to measure solar system distances
  • A planet must meet all three IAU criteria: it orbits the Sun, its gravity has pulled it round, and it has cleared its orbital neighbourhood
  • Pluto fails only the third — it shares the Kuiper Belt with thousands of similar bodies. That makes it a dwarf planet. Not “too small”: size is not one of the criteria
  • The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter; the Kuiper Belt runs 30–50 AU beyond Neptune; the Oort Cloud is a distant shell around the whole system and the source of long-period comets
  • Year 8 extension — Kepler’s three laws (orbits are ellipses; equal areas in equal times; \(T^2 \propto a^3\)); the nebular hypothesis for how the system formed
