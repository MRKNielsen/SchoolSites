Booklet p.15 · §4.1

Concept

From the Space Race to robotic exploration

The Space Race (1957–1969) was a Cold War competition between the USA and USSR to achieve space milestones:

  • 1957: USSR launches Sputnik 1 — first artificial satellite
  • 1961: Yuri Gagarin (USSR) — first human in orbit
  • 1969: Apollo 11 (USA) — first humans on the Moon (Neil Armstrong & Buzz Aldrin, 20 July)

Modern robotic missions explore where humans can’t (yet):

  • Mars rovers: Curiosity (2012–), Perseverance (2021–); searching for signs of past life
  • Voyager 1 & 2: launched 1977; Voyager 1 is now in interstellar space (>23 billion km from Sun)
  • JWST (2021): successor to Hubble; infrared telescope at L2 point
Crewed vs. uncrewed missions

Crewed: humans on board; higher cost and risk; humans can respond to unexpected events; greater public engagement. Uncrewed/robotic: cheaper, safer, can go to environments where humans would die; can be controlled from Earth over vast distances.

Booklet p.16 · §4.2

Concept

Australia’s role in space exploration

Australia has played a significant and often underappreciated role in space exploration:

  • Parkes radio telescope (‘The Dish’) — received and relayed live TV footage of the Apollo 11 Moon landing to 600 million viewers worldwide
  • CSIRO — invented Wi-Fi technology from radio astronomy research; operates 6 major telescopes across Australia
  • Australian Space Agency (ASA) — established 2018; growing commercial space sector
  • Square Kilometre Array (SKA) — world’s largest radio telescope network; core in WA on Wajarri Country
JWST discoveries (2022–)

JWST has imaged the earliest galaxies ever seen (just 300 million years after the Big Bang), found evidence of CO₂ on an exoplanet’s atmosphere, and revealed stunning detail in stellar nurseries. Its infrared vision sees through gas and dust that blocked Hubble. Australian astronomers contribute to its science programs.

Structured evaluation

Evaluate whether sending humans to Mars is worth doing. Use evidence to argue both for and against, then reach a conclusion. (This is the type of extended response expected in the assessment task.)

  1. 1 Benefits (For): Scientific returns (geology, life search, atmospheric data); technological spin-offs (medical, materials science, comms); potential long-term human survival on another planet; international collaboration and national prestige.
  2. 2 Risks/Costs (Against): Estimated cost US$450 billion+; radiation exposure during 6-month transit; unknown health effects of Martian gravity (38% of Earth); risk of contaminating Mars with Earth microbes (planetary protection); delay would mean 7-year missions due to orbital mechanics.
  3. ✓ Conclusion (example): The scientific and long-term survival benefits justify a crewed Mars mission if costs are shared internationally. Robotic missions should continue in parallel to reduce risk and cost. Strong planetary protection protocols are essential.

Year 8 Extension

Rocket physics — Tsiolkovsky’s rocket equation

Rockets work by Newton’s Third Law: burning fuel is expelled backwards (thrust), and the rocket accelerates forwards (reaction). But to reach space, a rocket must overcome Earth’s gravity well — requiring enormous fuel mass.

Tsiolkovsky rocket equation

Δv = vₛ × ln(m₀/mṣ)
where Δv = change in velocity, vₛ = exhaust speed, m₀ = initial (full) mass, mṣ = final (empty) mass. This shows that to double Δv, you need to square the mass ratio — rocket fuel is very expensive in mass terms.

  • Multi-stage rockets: drop empty fuel tanks to reduce mass mid-flight (Saturn V had 3 stages)
  • Reusable rockets (SpaceX Falcon 9): land back after launch; dramatically reduce cost
Escape velocity from Earth

To escape Earth’s gravity completely (not orbit, but leave), a rocket needs 11.2 km/s (40,320 km/h). For comparison, a bullet travels about 0.9 km/s. This is why getting to space is so energy-intensive.

Year 8 Extension

Future missions — Artemis, Mars, and beyond

The coming decades will see unprecedented space exploration missions:

  • Artemis programme (NASA): return humans to the Moon by 2026; establish a lunar gateway station; prepare for Mars crewed missions in the 2030s–2040s
  • Europa Clipper (NASA, 2024): flyby mission to search for habitability in Europa’s ocean
  • Mars Sample Return: Perseverance has collected rock cores; future mission will return them to Earth for direct analysis
  • Interstellar probes: Breakthrough Starshot proposes laser-driven light sails to reach Alpha Centauri in 20 years
Australia in future space

The Australian Space Agency’s goal is to grow Australia’s space industry to A$12 billion by 2030. Australian companies are developing satellite communications, Earth observation platforms, and launch sites. Southern Cross Astronautics, Gilmour Space Technologies (QLD), and others are building Australian launch vehicles.

Booklet p.17 · §4.3

Concept

Space debris and Kessler syndrome

Space debris includes defunct satellites, spent rocket stages, fragments from collisions, and lost equipment. There are:

  • ~27,000 tracked objects >10 cm
  • ~500,000 objects 1–10 cm (too small to track, too large to ignore)
  • ~100 million fragments <1 cm

Kessler syndrome (proposed by NASA scientist Donald Kessler, 1978): if orbital density becomes too high, one collision creates debris that causes more collisions in a cascade — potentially making certain orbital shells unusable for centuries. This already has some evidence in LEO.

Collision velocity and damage

Objects in LEO travel at ~7.8 km/s. Head-on collisions between objects in different orbits can involve relative velocities of 15 km/s. At this speed, even a 1 cm aluminium sphere delivers the kinetic energy equivalent to a hand grenade. The ISS has had to manoeuvre to avoid debris 30+ times.

Concept

How satellites help manage Earth

Earth observation (EO) satellites have transformed how we monitor and manage our planet:

  • Weather forecasting: geostationary weather satellites (e.g., Himawari) provide real-time cloud cover, temperature, and storm tracking. Without them, weather forecasts beyond 24h would be unreliable
  • Climate monitoring: tracking sea level rise, ice cap area, deforestation rates, coral bleaching, wildfire extent
  • Precision agriculture: farmers use multispectral satellite imagery to monitor crop health, water stress, and soil condition
  • Emergency response: floods, earthquakes, and wildfires are mapped in near-real-time to direct emergency services
  • Navigation (GPS): 24+ GPS satellites provide positioning accurate to <1 m; used in transport, shipping, aviation, and smartphones
Australia’s EO dependence

Australia’s vast geography makes it especially satellite-dependent. The Bureau of Meteorology uses satellite data for all its forecasts. Geoscience Australia uses EO to monitor bushfire risk and coastal erosion. Indigenous rangers use satellite data to manage Country — tracking water, vegetation, and fire across remote land.

I do Worked example — evaluating space sustainability

Structured evidence-based evaluation

Evaluate whether current human use of orbital space is sustainable. Use evidence to support your argument, and suggest what changes would make it more sustainable.

  1. 1 Evidence it is NOT sustainable: Debris accumulation is outpacing removal; recent anti-satellite missile tests (China 2007, India 2019, Russia 2021) created thousands of new debris fragments. Orbital shell capacity is finite. Kessler cascade risk in some LEO shells is rising.
  2. 2 Evidence it COULD be managed: Space agencies increasingly require deorbit plans; active debris removal technologies in development (e.g., ESA ClearSpace-1, JAXA ELSA-d magnetic capture); UN guidelines for sustainable use of outer space exist but are non-binding.
  3. ✓ Conclusion: Current use is not yet sustainable but could become so with binding international agreements, mandatory debris removal funding, and active remediation technology. The commons problem (no single owner of orbit) makes governance essential.

Design a debris management solution

You have been hired by the Australian Space Agency to design a system to address space debris in LEO. In your booklet, describe: (a) the mechanism your system uses; (b) one advantage; (c) one limitation; (d) how you would fund and govern it internationally.

  1. 1 Example mechanism — Harpoon + tether system: A small spacecraft fires a harpoon into a defunct satellite, attaches a deorbit tether, and drags it into the atmosphere where it burns up. ESA’s ClearSpace-1 uses a robotic arm variation of this approach.
  2. 2 Advantage: Physically removes large debris objects — the ones most likely to cause cascade collisions. Limitation: Each mission removes only 1–2 objects; at US$100M per mission, removing 20,000 tracked objects would cost trillions and take centuries at current rates.
  3. ✓ Governance model: A UN space debris levy — satellite operators pay per object launched; funds an international remediation agency. Non-binding guidelines have failed; economic incentives are more effective. This mirrors how carbon trading works for climate. Students: compare your solution with the example.

Booklet p.19 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

Active debris removal technologies

Current and near-future active debris removal (ADR) technologies:

  • Robotic arm capture (ESA ClearSpace-1): a chaser spacecraft matches orbits with a defunct satellite and grabs it with a robotic arm before deorbiting both. Launch 2026
  • Electromagnetic tether (JAXA ELSA-d): docking clamp attaches to a target; a long electrodynamic tether drags it down using Earth’s magnetic field — no fuel required for deorbit
  • Laser ablation: ground-based or space-based laser heats debris surface, creating a small plasma jet that changes the object’s orbit slightly — eventually causing it to reenter. Does not require physical contact
  • Foam cloud: a foam-dispensing spacecraft creates a temporary cloud that slows small debris, increasing atmospheric drag
The economics problem

The fundamental problem with ADR is economic: the company that removes debris doesn’t benefit financially — the entire orbital community benefits. This is a classic “tragedy of the commons.” Without regulation or funding mechanisms (debris levies, insurance mandates), ADR remains underfunded relative to the scale of the problem.

Booklet p.19 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

Orbital capacity and international governance

Orbital space — particularly the geostationary belt (35,786 km) and popular LEO shells — is a finite resource. Current governance frameworks:

  • Outer Space Treaty (1967): space is the “province of all mankind”; no national sovereignty; no weapons of mass destruction in orbit. Signed by 110+ nations — but written before mega-constellations, commercial space tourism, or space mining
  • ITU (International Telecommunication Union): allocates GEO orbital slots and radio frequencies. Slots are crowded; companies sometimes file for slots speculatively
  • UN COPUOS: Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space; develops guidelines but cannot enforce them
  • Artemis Accords (2020): US-led bilateral agreements on responsible Moon behaviour; 20+ signatories but China and Russia not included
The mega-constellation challenge

SpaceX Starlink (42,000 planned), Amazon Kuiper (3,200), and OneWeb (648) together represent a 10× increase in active satellites. Astronomers warn of “satellite trails” ruining optical and radio observations. Light pollution from satellite reflections is a growing concern for dark-sky Country — impacting both optical astronomy and Aboriginal sky knowledge practices.

What we covered

  • Key milestones: Sputnik (1957) → Gagarin (1961) → Moon landing (1969) → ISS (1998) → Mars rovers → JWST (2021)
  • Crewed missions: higher cost/risk but humans can respond in real time. Uncrewed: cheaper, safer, can go further
  • Australia’s contributions: Parkes Dish (Moon landing comms), CSIRO (Wi-Fi & radio astronomy), SKA (Wajarri Country, WA)

  • Space debris: 27,000+ tracked objects; at orbital speeds (>7 km/s) even tiny fragments are dangerous
  • Kessler syndrome: collision cascade — one collision generates debris that causes more collisions; could make orbital shells unusable
  • Earth observation satellites support weather forecasting, climate monitoring, agriculture, emergency response, and navigation (GPS)
  • Indigenous rangers use satellite data combined with Traditional Knowledge to manage Country more effectively
  • Orbital space is a finite commons; international governance is essential but currently inadequate
