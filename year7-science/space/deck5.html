Concept

Stars and measuring distance

A light-year is the distance light travels in one year — approximately 9.46 × 10²³ km. It is a unit of distance, not time.

  • The Sun: light takes 8 min 20 s to reach us. Proxima Centauri: 4.2 light-years
  • Stars are classified by colour & temperature using the OBAFGKM sequence — blue/white (hottest) → red (coolest)
  • Our Sun is type G (yellow dwarf) — medium-sized, halfway through its life

Concept

Star life cycles and galaxies

Life cycle of a sun-like star:

Massive stars end their lives as neutron stars or black holes (via supernova). All elements heavier than hydrogen and helium are created in stars — we are made of stardust.

Galaxy types

Spiral (e.g. Milky Way, Andromeda): disc with spiral arms, ongoing star formation. Elliptical: oval, older stars, little star formation. Irregular: no defined shape, often result of collisions. The Milky Way contains 200–400 billion stars; the observable universe contains ~2 trillion galaxies.

I do Worked example — light-year calculation

Converting light-years to kilometres

The Andromeda Galaxy is 2.5 million light-years away. How many kilometres is this? Express your answer in scientific notation. (Speed of light = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; 1 year ≈ 3.15 × 10⁷ s)

  1. 1 light-year in metres\(1\ \text{ly} = (3 \times 10^{8}) \times (3.15 \times 10^{7}) = 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}\)
  2. Convert to kilometres\(\phantom{1\ \text{ly}} = 9.46 \times 10^{12}\ \text{km}\)
  3. Multiply by 2.5 million\(d = (2.5 \times 10^{6}) \times (9.46 \times 10^{12})\)
  4. \(\phantom{d} = 2.37 \times 10^{20} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{20}\ \text{km}\)

That is 24 billion trillion kilometres — which is exactly why the light-year is a more practical unit than the kilometre for cosmic distances.

Star life stage identification

A star is observed to be red, very large (100 times the Sun’s radius), and has stopped fusing hydrogen in its core. What stage of its life is this star at? What will happen next if it has a similar mass to the Sun? What if it is 20 times more massive?

  1. 1 Identify the current stage: Red + very large + no longer fusing hydrogen in core = Red giant stage. The outer layers have expanded as the star ages off the main sequence.
  2. 2 Sun-like mass: The outer layers will be ejected as a planetary nebula, leaving behind a white dwarf. The white dwarf slowly cools over billions of years.
  3. ✓ Massive star (20× Sun): The core collapse triggers a supernova explosion. The remnant core becomes a neutron star (or black hole if mass is high enough). Heavy elements are scattered into space to form future stars and planets.

First Nations perspectives

The Magellanic Clouds in Aboriginal knowledge

The Large and Small Magellanic Clouds are dwarf galaxies visible to the naked eye in the Southern Hemisphere sky. Aboriginal Australians across the continent have observed, named, and incorporated these objects into star lore for thousands of years — long before European astronomers “discovered” them.

In Boorong knowledge (Victorian, Wergaia people), the two Magellanic Clouds represent Yugarilya — the two Bram-bram-bult (sons of Bunjil, the creator) who fly through the sky as wedge-tailed eagles. Their positions tracked seasons.

Indigenous astronomy as genuine science

The Boorong star catalogue, documented in 1857 with Elder William Barak’s assistance, is one of the most detailed records of Aboriginal astronomical knowledge. It matches 47 celestial objects to Boorong names. Researchers have since confirmed Boorong knowledge of the Magellanic Clouds predates their European naming by millennia. This is a living knowledge system — ongoing, not historical.

Year 8 Extension

The Hertzsprung–Russell (H–R) diagram

The H–R diagram plots stars by luminosity (energy output) vs surface temperature. Stars don’t scatter randomly — they cluster in recognisable regions:

  • Main sequence: a diagonal band from hot-bright (upper left) to cool-dim (lower right). Stars here fuse hydrogen. Our Sun sits in the middle
  • Giant branch: upper right — stars that have evolved off the main sequence; cool but very luminous (large)
  • White dwarfs: lower left — hot but very dim (small)
  • A star’s position on the H–R diagram tells us its age and evolutionary stage
Reading the diagram

Recall: hotter stars are blue/white, cooler stars are red. On the H–R diagram, temperature runs right to left on the x-axis (hot on left, cool on right) — the opposite of what you might expect. This is a historical convention.

Booklet p.19 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

The expanding universe — Hubble’s law

In 1929, Edwin Hubble found that distant galaxies are moving away from us, and that galaxies farther away are receding faster. This is Hubble’s Law: v = H₀ × d, where v is recession speed, d is distance, and H₀ is the Hubble constant (∼70 km/s/Mpc).

  • Galaxies aren’t moving through space — space itself is expanding (like dots on a balloon being inflated)
  • Running the expansion backwards gives an age for the universe: 13.8 billion years (the Big Bang)
  • The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is the afterglow of the Big Bang — detectable as faint microwave radiation in all directions
Redshift

As a galaxy moves away, its light is stretched to longer (redder) wavelengths — redshift. Measuring how redshifted a galaxy’s spectral lines are tells us its recession speed. Galaxies moving toward us (like Andromeda) are blueshifted instead.

Concept

How telescopes work

Telescopes collect and focus light (or other EM radiation) to make distant objects appear brighter and larger. Two main types for visible light:

  • Refracting telescopes: use glass lenses to bend (refract) light to a focus. Good for small instruments; limited by glass quality and chromatic aberration
  • Reflecting telescopes: use mirrors to reflect light to a focus. Can be made much larger (no weight limit from glass); all modern large telescopes are reflectors. Example: Hubble Space Telescope (2.4 m mirror), JWST (6.5 m gold-coated mirrors)
  • Radio telescopes: large dish antennas that collect radio waves; Parkes (64 m dish); multiple dishes linked = interferometry (much sharper images)
Why use telescopes in space?

Earth’s atmosphere absorbs or distorts much of the EM spectrum: UV, X-ray, and gamma rays barely reach the ground; infrared is partially blocked by water vapour; even visible light is blurred by atmospheric turbulence (“twinkle”). Space telescopes see the full spectrum undistorted.

Booklet p.23 · §5.6

Concept

Spectroscopy — reading starlight

When starlight passes through a prism or diffraction grating, it is split into a spectrum. This spectrum contains dark absorption lines — specific wavelengths absorbed by elements in the star’s outer atmosphere.

Each element has a unique set of absorption lines — a “spectral fingerprint.” By analysing these lines, astronomers can determine:

  • Composition: what elements are in the star
  • Temperature: hotter stars show different line sets
  • Speed: blueshifted = moving toward us; redshifted = moving away (Doppler effect)
  • Exoplanet atmospheres: JWST can detect CO₂, water, and methane on planets orbiting other stars

Work through with the class

I do Worked example — interpreting spectral data

What can we learn from starlight?

An astronomer observes a star and notes: (i) its spectrum shows hydrogen absorption lines shifted slightly to the red end; (ii) it has a blue-white colour; (iii) it is brighter than our Sun at the same distance. What can you conclude about this star?

  1. 1 Redshifted hydrogen lines: The star is moving away from us (Doppler effect). The shift is slight, so it’s moving away at relatively modest speed relative to our solar system.
  2. 2 Blue-white colour: Recall OBAFGKM — blue-white stars are type O or B — very hot surface temperature (20,000–50,000 K). Much hotter than our Sun (5,778 K).
  3. 3 Brighter than the Sun: Higher luminosity — this star is much more massive and luminous. Massive stars burn fuel rapidly and have short lifespans (millions, not billions, of years).
  4. ✓ Summary: A massive, hot O or B-type star, moving away from us, far more luminous than the Sun, and likely to die as a supernova in a cosmically short time.

Telescope choice

An astronomer wants to: (a) study a stellar nursery hidden inside a dense dust cloud; (b) detect signals from a distant pulsar; (c) observe an active volcanic eruption on Jupiter’s moon Io. For each, which type of telescope is best and why?

  1. 1 (a) Stellar nursery in dust: Use an infrared telescope. Infrared light penetrates dust clouds that block visible light. JWST is ideal — it images star formation hidden inside nebulae.
  2. 2 (b) Pulsar signals: Use a radio telescope. Pulsars emit powerful radio wave pulses with extreme regularity. Ground-based dishes like Parkes are ideal.
  3. ✓ (c) Volcanic eruption on Io: Use a near-infrared or thermal infrared telescope. Volcanic eruptions are hot — they glow brightly in infrared. Hubble and ground-based adaptive optics telescopes have imaged Io’s volcanoes this way.

First Nations perspectives

Two-way astronomy: Wajarri and the MWA

The Murchison Widefield Array (MWA) — a radio telescope precursor to the SKA — sits on Wajarri Yamatji Country in Western Australia. It is one of the most sensitive radio telescopes on Earth, used to map hydrogen across the Milky Way and search for signatures of the early universe.

The Wajarri Yamatji have maintained detailed sky knowledge of this landscape for tens of thousands of years, observing seasonal changes in the Milky Way’s position and the movements of bright stars above their Country. The MWA is operated under partnership with the Wajarri — not despite their traditions but in concert with them.

Two knowledge systems, one sky

Radio astronomy detects structures in the Milky Way that are invisible to optical telescopes — vast clouds of hydrogen gas, the remnants of supernova explosions, and radio pulses from pulsars. Wajarri sky knowledge includes the Milky Way’s seasonal changes and the dark constellations visible to the naked eye. Both systems observe the same sky from the same Country, each revealing different layers of the same cosmos.

Booklet p.19 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

JWST — engineering and science

The James Webb Space Telescope launched 25 December 2021 and is the most powerful space telescope ever built:

  • Mirror: 18 gold-coated beryllium hexagonal segments; total 6.5 m; 100× more sensitive than Hubble
  • Location: Sun–Earth L2 point, 1.5 million km from Earth; always in Earth’s shadow; tennis-court-sized sun shield maintains mirror at −233°C
  • Wavelengths: near and mid-infrared (0.6–28 µm); sees redshifted light from earliest galaxies; peers through dust clouds
  • Key discoveries to 2024: earliest galaxies (z>13), CO₂ on exoplanet WASP-39b’s atmosphere, stellar nurseries in unprecedented detail
Why infrared for early universe?

Light from galaxies formed shortly after the Big Bang has been travelling for 13+ billion years. As the universe expands, space itself stretches this light to longer (redder) wavelengths — by the time it reaches us, UV and visible light has been redshifted into the infrared. Only an infrared telescope can see these first galaxies.

Booklet p.19 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

Interferometry and the Event Horizon Telescope

Linking many radio telescopes together via interferometry creates a virtual telescope as large as the separation between the dishes. Signal timing is synchronised with atomic clocks; recordings are combined computationally.

  • VLBI (Very Long Baseline Interferometry): links dishes on different continents; resolution can reach milliarcseconds
  • Event Horizon Telescope (EHT): linked 8 radio observatories worldwide into a “virtual Earth-sized dish.” In 2019 it produced the first image of a black hole shadow (M87 galaxy). In 2022 it imaged Sgr A*, our own Milky Way’s central black hole
  • Australian telescopes (ATCA, Parkes) contribute to VLBI networks
Why it’s so hard

The M87 black hole shadow subtends an angle of 40 microarcseconds in our sky — like reading a newspaper in New York from Sydney. A single dish would need to be the size of Earth to achieve this resolution. Interferometry achieves it by combining signals mathematically — one of the greatest technical feats in the history of astronomy.
