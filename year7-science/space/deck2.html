Day, night and seasons

In this part you will:

  • Explain that Earth’s rotation causes day and night
  • Explain that Earth’s revolution (orbit) takes one year
  • Describe how Earth’s axial tilt causes seasons
  • Identify solstices and equinoxes on a diagram

Booklet p.5 · §2.1

Concept

Earth’s rotation — day and night

  • Earth rotates on its own axis once every 24 hours (one day)
  • When your part of Earth faces the Sun — daytime. When it faces away — night-time
  • Viewed from above the North Pole, Earth rotates anti-clockwise
  • This means the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west
  • At Melbourne’s latitude, the Sun crosses the northern part of the sky at midday (unlike the Northern Hemisphere)
Rotation vs revolution

Rotation = spinning on its own axis (24 hours = 1 day).
Revolution = orbiting around the Sun (365.25 days = 1 year).
These are two completely different motions happening at the same time.

A student says “At 6am it gets lighter because the Sun rises”. Is the student’s explanation correct? Can you improve it?

Booklet p.5 · §2.2

Concept

Why we have seasons — axial tilt

Earth’s axis is tilted at 23.5° relative to its orbit. As Earth revolves around the Sun over a year, different parts of Earth receive more direct sunlight at different times.

  • Seasons are NOT caused by Earth’s distance from the Sun (Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January — Australian summer!)
  • When a hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun: summer (more direct sunlight, longer days)
  • When a hemisphere is tilted away: winter (less direct sunlight, shorter days)
  • Southern and Northern hemispheres always have opposite seasons
Key misconception

“It’s summer because Earth is closer to the Sun.” FALSE. Australia has summer in December when Earth is actually closest to the Sun (perihelion), but it’s summer because the Southern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun — giving more direct, concentrated sunlight.

Booklet p.5 · §2.3

Concept

Solstices and equinoxes

Definitions

Solstice: the day with the longest (summer) or shortest (winter) period of daylight. Equinox: day and night are approximately equal everywhere on Earth (~12 hours each).

I do Worked example — reading a seasons diagram

Use the diagram to answer the question

At position A, the Southern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun. At position B (6 months later), it is tilted away. For each position, state the Australian season and whether days are long or short in Melbourne. Explain why.

  1. 1 Position A (tilted toward Sun): The Southern Hemisphere receives more direct sunlight and has a larger arc above the horizon each day.
  2. 2 Position A = Summer in Australia: Long days, high Sun angle, warm temperatures. (Around December 21 — the summer solstice for Australia.)
  3. 3 Position B (tilted away): The Southern Hemisphere receives less direct sunlight at a lower angle. Days are short.
  4. ✓ Position B = Winter in Australia (around June 21). Short days, low Sun angle, cold temperatures. Meanwhile the Northern Hemisphere is having its summer.

First Nations perspectives

Wurundjeri seasonal calendar

Wurundjeri-Woiwurrung people of the Kulin Nation traditionally recognise six to seven seasons on Country, marked not by the European calendar but by ecological signals: flowering plants, animal behaviour, and the rising and setting of specific stars.

The position of the Pleiades and other star clusters in the evening sky signals when to fish in the Yarra, when to harvest yam daisies, and when cool fire management should occur.

Astronomy as seasonal science

Using stellar positions to mark seasons is astronomical observation applied to ecological management. Wurundjeri people used the same physical phenomenon scientists now study — Earth’s tilt causing different stars to be visible at different times — to build a precise, place-based seasonal calendar valid for Country in the Melbourne region.

Booklet p.7 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

The analemma — why the Sun “wanders”

If you photograph the Sun at exactly the same time each day for a year, it traces a figure-8 shape called the analemma. This happens because of two factors:

  • Axial tilt (23.5°) — causes the Sun to appear higher or lower in the sky through the year
  • Elliptical orbit — Earth moves faster when closer to the Sun (January) and slower when further (July). This means solar noon drifts relative to clock time
The equation of time

The difference between “solar noon” (when the Sun is highest) and “clock noon” varies by up to 16 minutes through the year. This is called the equation of time. Sundials are only accurate on four days per year!

Booklet p.7 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

Milankovitch cycles — ice ages and long-term seasons

Over tens of thousands of years, Earth’s orbit and tilt slowly change through three cycles, affecting how much solar energy reaches Earth and driving ice ages:

  • Eccentricity (~100,000 yr): the ellipse shape of Earth’s orbit changes from nearly circular to more elongated
  • Obliquity (~41,000 yr): the tilt angle varies between 22.1° and 24.5°
  • Precession (~26,000 yr): Earth’s axis wobbles like a spinning top
Ice age driver

When all three cycles align to reduce summer sunlight at high northern latitudes, ice sheets can grow, triggering an ice age. The last glacial maximum was about 20,000 years ago. Aboriginal peoples were living in Australia throughout this period.

Booklet p.6 · §2.4

Concept

Why the Moon appears to change shape

The Moon does not produce its own light — it reflects sunlight. At all times, the half of the Moon facing the Sun is lit. As the Moon orbits Earth over 29.5 days, we see different fractions of that lit half — these are the phases.

  • New Moon: Moon between Earth and Sun — dark side faces us
  • Full Moon: Earth between Moon and Sun — fully lit side faces us
  • Quarter phases: we see exactly half the lit surface
  • “Waxing” = growing; “Waning” = shrinking
Common misconception

Earth’s shadow does NOT cause phases. A lunar eclipse (when Earth’s shadow covers the Moon) is a different event. Phases are caused by our changing view of the lit half as the Moon orbits.

Check

Booklet p.6 · §2.5

Concept

Tides — the Moon’s gravitational pull

The Moon’s gravity pulls on Earth’s oceans, creating a bulge of water on the side of Earth facing the Moon, and a second bulge on the opposite side (due to inertia). Earth rotates through these bulges — causing two high tides and two low tides per day.

  • Spring tides: Sun, Earth, Moon aligned (new or full Moon). Sun and Moon pull together — very high tides and very low tides
  • Neap tides: Moon at right angles to Sun (first or third quarter). Tides partially cancel — moderate tides
  • “Spring” has nothing to do with the season — it means “to leap up”

Work through with the class

I do Worked example — predicting lunar phases

Using the 29.5-day cycle

The new Moon was on 1 January. What phase will the Moon be on 22 January? Will spring tides or neap tides occur on this date?

  1. 1 Count the days: From 1 Jan to 22 Jan = 21 days after new Moon.
  2. 2 Map to cycle: Full Moon ≈ 14.75 days; third quarter ≈ 22 days. So 21 days is just before third quarter. Phase: waning gibbous (nearly third quarter)
  3. 3 Tidal type: Third quarter = Sun and Moon at right angles to Earth. → Neap tides
  4. ✓ Answer: On 22 January the Moon will be a waning gibbous, approaching third quarter. Neap tides will occur (moderate tidal range).

Eclipses and phases

A lunar eclipse is occurring tonight. A solar eclipse occurred two weeks ago. Identify the phase of the Moon at each event and explain why both can’t happen on the same night.

  1. 1 Lunar eclipse: Earth must be between the Sun and Moon — this only happens at Full Moon. Earth’s shadow falls on the fully lit Moon.
  2. 2 Solar eclipse: Moon must be between Earth and the Sun — this only happens at New Moon. The Moon blocks sunlight from reaching Earth.
  3. ✓ They can’t happen on the same night because New Moon and Full Moon are about 14–15 days apart — opposite positions in the lunar cycle. The Moon can’t be between us and the Sun AND on the far side of Earth at the same time.

Booklet p.5

First Nations perspectives

Moon knowledge in Aboriginal traditions

Many Aboriginal Australian groups have detailed knowledge of the Moon’s cycle, woven into ceremony, ecology, and governance. For many Wurundjeri women and across Kulin Nation groups, the lunar calendar has been a framework for timing ceremonies, food harvesting, and health practices.

In some traditions, the Moon is a male ancestral being who travels across the sky. His waxing and waning embody cycles of life, rest, and renewal observed over millennia of careful sky-watching.

Lunar calendars as science

Using the 29.5-day lunar cycle to organise food gathering, ceremony, and travel is applied astronomical science. The same cycle that drives tides and illuminates Country at night was used to create a sophisticated, precise calendar valid for the southern sky long before European contact.

Booklet p.7 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

Eclipses — geometry and prediction

Eclipses don’t happen every New or Full Moon because the Moon’s orbit is tilted 5.1° relative to Earth’s orbit. The Moon only crosses the ecliptic plane at two points called nodes. Eclipses only occur when a New or Full Moon coincides with a node.

  • Umbra: the full shadow of the Moon — inside this, you see a total solar eclipse
  • Penumbra: the partial shadow — you see a partial solar eclipse
  • Saros cycle: eclipses repeat in almost the same pattern every 18 years, 11 days, 8 hours
Why the Moon fits perfectly

The Moon is 400 times smaller than the Sun, but also 400 times closer. This remarkable coincidence means the Moon’s disc appears almost exactly the same size as the Sun’s disc in Earth’s sky — making total solar eclipses possible. This is unique in our solar system.

Booklet p.7 · Year 8 extension

Year 8 Extension

Tidal forces — slowing Earth and the Moon’s recession

Earth’s tidal bulges are dragged slightly ahead of the Moon by Earth’s rotation. This tidal friction has two major effects:

  • Earth’s rotation is gradually slowing down — days are getting longer (by ~2 ms per century). 1.4 billion years ago, a day was only 18 hours
  • The Moon is moving away from Earth at about 3.8 cm per year (confirmed by laser ranging from Apollo retroreflectors)
  • Eventually (billions of years away), Earth’s rotation period will equal the Moon’s orbital period — mutual tidal locking
Apollo legacy

Astronauts left retroreflectors on the Moon’s surface. Scientists can bounce lasers off them to measure the distance to the Moon with centimetre accuracy. This directly confirms the Moon is receding at 3.8 cm/year.

Lesson recap

What we covered

  • Rotation (24 h) causes day and night; Earth spins anti-clockwise viewed from above the North Pole
  • Revolution (365.25 days) is Earth’s orbit around the Sun
  • Earth’s axial tilt of 23.5° causes seasons — NOT distance from the Sun
  • Southern Hemisphere has summer in December (tilted toward Sun); winter in June (tilted away)
  • Solstices: longest and shortest days (around June 21 and December 21); Equinoxes: equal day and night (around March 21 and September 21)
  • Wurundjeri-Woiwurrung people use star positions and ecological signals to mark a detailed six-season calendar on Country
  • Moon phases are caused by our changing view of the lit half of the Moon as it orbits Earth — NOT Earth’s shadow
  • The eight phases (in order): New → Waxing crescent → First quarter → Waxing gibbous → Full → Waning gibbous → Third quarter → Waning crescent → New
  • Spring tides: Sun–Earth–Moon aligned (new or full Moon); very large tidal range
  • Neap tides: Moon at 90° to Sun (quarter phases); moderate tidal range
  • Lunar eclipse: Earth’s shadow on Moon (full Moon only); Solar eclipse: Moon between Earth and Sun (new Moon only)
  • Wurundjeri and other Aboriginal peoples have used the lunar cycle for ceremony, food harvesting, and governance for thousands of years
