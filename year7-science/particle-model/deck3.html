Check after reading

Galileo’s container became lighter when he let the extra air out. Why does that show that air has mass?

AnswerAir was the only thing that left the container. The container lost mass, so the air that left must have had mass.

Booklet p.25 · §3.1 · Worked Example 3.1

Part B · Theory

I do Measuring mass

Using a balance
  1. Flat bench; nothing touching the pan.
  2. Empty container on, press tare — it reads 0.00 g.
  3. Add the substance: the reading is its mass alone.
  4. Write the unit, and keep every decimal place.
Worked Example 3.1

A student forgets to tare the balance. An empty beaker reads 52.40 g. With salt in it, it reads 67.15 g. What is the mass of the salt?

  1. 1 Mass of salt = mass with salt − mass of empty beaker
  2. 2 \(67.15 - 52.40 = 14.75\)
  3. ✓ The salt has a mass of 14.75 g.

We do 3.1

An empty plastic cup has a mass of 8.30 g. Filled with sand, it has a mass of 126.80 g. What is the mass of the sand?

  2. 2 \(126.80 - 8.30 = 118.50\)
  3. ✓ The sand has a mass of 118.50 g.

Booklet p.25 · §3.2 · Worked Example 3.2

Part B · Theory

I do Reading a measuring cylinder

Worked Example 3.2

A 100 mL measuring cylinder has a big mark every 10 mL and one small mark halfway between each. The bottom of the meniscus sits on the small mark between 40 and 50. What is the volume?

  1. 1 One division: the small mark splits each 10 mL into two, so each division is worth 5 mL.
  2. 2 Eye level, bottom of the curve: halfway between 40 and 50.
  3. ✓ The volume is 45 mL.

Booklet p.26 · §3.3 · The Experiment

Part B · Planning the experiment

Part 1: Does air have mass?

Prediction — before you begin

Will a ball weigh more, less or the same after you pump more air into it? Write your prediction and a reason in your booklet.

Aim

To show that air has mass and takes up space.

Equipment: soft ball with a valve · ball pump with a needle · electronic balance (0.01 g) · large clear tub of water · clear plastic cup · paper towel · sticky tape

Method — Part 1
  1. Let some air out of the ball so it is soft but still round.
  2. Tare the balance, put the ball on it, and record its mass.
  3. Pump the ball until it is firm. Count the pumps.
  4. Weigh the ball again and record its mass.
  5. Repeat the whole test two more times.

Booklet pp.26–27 · Method Part 2 · Safety

Part B · Planning the experiment

Part 2: Does air take up space?

Method — Part 2
  1. Tape a scrunched paper towel into the bottom of the cup.
  2. Turn the cup upside down and push it straight down to the bottom of the tub. Don’t tilt it.
  3. Lift it straight out, and feel the paper towel.
  4. Repeat, but tilt the cup under water. Watch what comes out.
Safety

Wipe up spilt water straight away — a wet floor is a slip hazard. Keep the pump needle pointed away from people, and keep water away from the balance and its power cord.

Booklet pp.30–31 · Part D · like Extensions 3.8–3.11

Part D · Above level

We do Going further — air by the numbers

These go beyond the Year 7 standard. Use the numbers, then judge the experiment.

A ball went from 598.20 g to 601.65 g after 20 pumps. About how much mass did each pump add?

Increase: \(601.65 - 598.20 = 3.45\) g

Each pump: \(3.45 \div 20 \approx 0.17\) g

About 0.17 g of air per pump.

One litre of air has a mass of about 1.2 g. What is the mass of the air in a 2 L bottle? Why does weighing the bottle open, then with its lid on, show no difference?

\(2 \times 1.2 = 2.4\) g of air.

Both times the bottle holds the same 2 L of air, so the mass is the same — you would need to add extra air, as with the ball, to see a change.

Lesson 3 · Recap

What we covered

  • Tare the balance first, or find a mass by difference
  • Read a measuring cylinder at eye level, at the bottom of the meniscus
  • Pumping air into a ball made it heavier: air has mass
  • The paper towel stayed dry because trapped air filled the cup: air takes up space
Exit question

How do your results show that the air you pumped into the ball was matter?

AnswerThe ball’s mass went up when air was pumped in, so the air has mass. The air also blew the ball up and kept water out of the cup, so it takes up space. It passes both tests, so it is matter.
