Booklet p.6 · §1.1 · Worked Example 1.1

Part B · Theory

I do What counts as matter?

The two tests for matter
  1. Does it have mass? Could it be weighed on a balance?
  2. Does it take up space? Does it have a volume?

It must pass both. Light, sound, heat and electricity fail both — they are energy, not matter.

Worked Example 1.1

Is each one matter? (a) the air in a bike tyre (b) the light from a torch (c) a shadow

  1. a A pumped-up tyre is heavier than a flat one, and the air pushes the tyre out. It has mass and takes up space — matter.
  2. b A torch weighs the same on or off, and light doesn’t fill a box — not matter, it is energy.
  3. c A shadow is only where light is blocked: no mass, no space — not matter.

Booklet p.7 · §1.2 · Worked Example 1.2

Part B · Theory

I do Using properties as evidence

Worked Example 1.2

A sealed syringe holds 50 mL of air. The plunger is pushed in and the air now fills only 30 mL. Let go, and the plunger springs back. What state is the air in? Give two properties as evidence.

  1. 1 It was squashed from 50 mL to 30 mL, so it can be compressed.
  2. 2 It filled the whole syringe both times — its volume changed to fill the space it had.
  3. ✓ The air is a gas.

Booklet p.8 · We do 1.2

Part B · Work through together

We do What state is it in?

We do 1.2

A sealed syringe is completely full of 40 mL of water. You push hard on the plunger, but it hardly moves, and the water still fills 40 mL. Then you pour the water into a wide dish, where it spreads out into a puddle. What state is the water in? Give two properties as evidence.

Write your working in the We do 1.2 box in your booklet.

  1. 1 It could not be squashed — still 40 mL — so it can’t be compressed and has a fixed volume.
  2. 2 In the dish it flowed and took the shape of its container.
  3. ✓ The water is a liquid.

Booklet p.8 · §1.3 · Worked Example 1.3

Part B · Theory

I do Tricky materials

Testing a tricky material
  1. Look at one piece — one grain, one crystal, one drop.
  2. Does that one piece keep its own shape and volume?
  3. If it does, the material is a solid, even if lots of pieces together can be poured.
Worked Example 1.3

Sand can be poured from a bucket, and it takes the shape of the bucket. Is sand a liquid?

  1. 1 One grain: under a magnifying glass, each grain is a tiny hard rock with its own shape. It doesn’t flow.
  2. 2 Why it pours: millions of grains slide past each other. A pile also makes a heap, not a flat surface.
  3. ✓ Sand is a solid made of very small pieces.

Part D · Above level

We do Going further — matter at the edges

These go beyond the Year 7 standard. Combine the tests, use numbers, and judge a claim.

A bike pump squashes 3 L of air into a tyre tube with a volume of 0.5 L. How many times smaller is the air’s volume? Could you do the same with 3 L of water?

\(3 \div 0.5 = 6\), so the air takes up 6 times less space.

No — a liquid can hardly be compressed, so 3 L of water would not fit in 0.5 L.

Is the flame of a candle matter? A flame is made of very hot gases, some of them glowing.

The gases in a flame have mass and take up space, so the flame is matter.

Yes — the light and heat it gives off are energy, but the hot gases themselves are matter.

Lesson 1 · Recap

What we covered

  • Matter has mass and takes up space; light, sound and heat are energy, not matter
  • Solids keep their shape and volume; liquids keep their volume and flow; gases fill their container and can be compressed
  • Name a state with at least two properties as evidence
  • For a tricky material, test one piece — sand and salt are solids
Exit question

Is the air in an “empty” bottle matter? Which state is it in? Give one property as evidence.

AnswerYes — air has mass and takes up space, so it is matter. It is a gas: it fills the whole bottle, and it can be squashed if you squeeze the bottle with the lid on.
