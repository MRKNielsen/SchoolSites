Booklet p.15 · §2.1 · Worked Example 2.1

Part B · Theory

I do Observation or inference?

Telling them apart

Ask: did I see, hear, feel or measure this, or did I work it out? If you worked it out, it is an inference.

Words like probably, must be and because usually signal an inference.

Worked Example 2.1

Sort these into observations and inferences: (a) the grass is wet (b) it rained last night (c) the box weighs 450 g (d) there is a book inside the box.

  1. a You can see and feel it — observation.
  2. b You didn’t see the rain; you worked it out from the wet grass. A sprinkler could also explain it — inference.
  3. c You read it from a balance — observation.
  4. d Worked out from the mass and the sound — inference.

Part B · Theory

Kinds of model

Kind of modelExampleWhat it helps us do
Physical (an object)A globesee the shape of the Earth and where the continents are
Diagram (a drawing)A map of a train linefind the stops and the order they come in
Mathematical or computerA weather forecastpredict tomorrow’s temperature and rain
Every model leaves things out

That is what makes it simpler than the real thing — and what makes it useful. A good question to ask of any model is: what does it show well, and what does it leave out?

Booklet p.16 · §2.2 · Worked Example 2.2

Part B · Theory

I do What does a model leave out?

Worked Example 2.2

A globe is a model of the Earth. Give one thing it shows well and one thing it leaves out.

  1. 1 Shows well: the Earth is a sphere, and where the oceans and continents are.
  2. 2 Leaves out: the real size — the Earth is about 12 700 km across — and things too small to show, like weather, cities and mountains.
  3. ✓ A model shows some things well and leaves others out.

Booklet p.16 · §2.3 · Worked Example 2.3

Part B · Theory

I do From evidence to a model

The mystery box

You may tilt it, shake it, roll it and weigh it — but you may not open it. Your observations are your evidence.

Worked Example 2.3

A group tilts a box slowly. They hear one object slide, then hit the end with a thud. When they roll the box, it doesn’t roll smoothly. What can they infer?

  1. 1 Slides, then thuds: the object is probably flat on one side, and fairly heavy.
  2. 2 No smooth roll: it is probably not round.
  3. ✓ Model: one flat, heavy object, such as a block of wood or a rubber eraser.

Booklet p.18 · §2.4 · Worked Example 2.4

Part B · Theory

I do Models change with new evidence

How science works

Scientists keep a model while it explains the evidence, and change it when new evidence doesn’t fit. That is a strength of science, not a weakness.

Worked Example 2.4

For a long time many people pictured the Earth as flat. About 2350 years ago, Aristotle noticed that during an eclipse of the Moon, the Earth’s shadow on the Moon is always curved. How did this change the model?

  1. 1 Evidence: only a ball casts a round shadow from every direction.
  2. ✓ New model: the Earth is a sphere, not a flat disc. The old model couldn’t explain the evidence, so it was replaced.

Booklet p.18 · We do 2.4

Part B · Work through together

We do Ships over the horizon

We do 2.4

Sailors noticed that when a ship sails away, its hull (the bottom) disappears first and the top of its mast disappears last. Explain how this supports the model of a round Earth rather than a flat one.

Write your working in the We do 2.4 box in your booklet.

  1. 1 On a round Earth the sea curves away, so the ship sinks behind the curve from the bottom up.
  2. 2 On a flat Earth the whole ship would just get smaller and fade out all at once.
  3. ✓ The evidence fits a round Earth, not a flat one.

Part D · Above level

We do Going further — judging evidence

These go beyond the Year 7 standard. Use numbers, and think about how sure you can be.

A sealed box has a mass of 300 g. The same box empty is 180 g. A group says it holds marbles of 20 g each. How many marbles? Is that number an observation or an inference?

Mass inside: \(300 - 180 = 120\) g, and \(120 \div 20 = 6\).

Six marbles — but that is an inference. The masses are observations; other objects with a total of 120 g would fit the evidence too.

Scientists once modelled atoms as tiny solid balls. Later experiments showed atoms have even smaller parts inside. Was the first model “wrong”?

The ball model explained the evidence scientists had at the time, and it still explains a lot.

Not useless — incomplete. New evidence didn’t fit, so the model was changed, which is how science is meant to work.

Lesson 2 · Recap

What we covered

  • An observation is noticed or measured; an inference is worked out from it
  • A model is a simpler version of something — physical, a diagram, or mathematical
  • Every model shows some things well and leaves things out
  • Models are built from evidence, tested with predictions, and changed when new evidence doesn’t fit
Exit question

A student says, “The box has a tennis ball in it.” Is that an observation or an inference? Give one piece of evidence that would support it.

AnswerAn inference — nobody has seen inside. Supporting evidence: it rolls smoothly when the box is tilted (round), it makes a soft thud rather than a clink, and the box is light.
