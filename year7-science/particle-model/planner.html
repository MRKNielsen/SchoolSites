Year 7 Science · Unit planning document

Unit: The Particle Model

30 lessons over 10 weeks (3 a week) · Victorian Curriculum F–10 v2.0 · Year 7 core with Year 8 extension · two assessed investigations

Every lesson has its own slide deck and its own booklet section, in four parts: A Reading · B Theory and examples · C Practice · D Above level. Every worked example (I do) is followed straight away by a matching We do with writing space in the booklet. The Lesson Booklet is complete for every taught lesson (1–21 and 25–27; the investigations in Weeks 8 and 10 have their own booklets, still to be written). Slide decks are being built a lesson at a time: a lesson is linked below once its deck exists, and is plain text until then. Two investigations are assessed, each finishing with an open-booklet test on the student’s own results plus an unseen data set: solubility and temperature (Week 8, after the solutions lessons it depends on) and recovering salt from rock salt (Week 10, after the separation lessons it tests). Above level: elements, compounds and chemical formulas are VC2S8U07 and appear only in Part D; Lesson 16 uses particle diagrams of elements and compounds as a tool for classifying, not as a Year 7 standard.

VC2S8U05
The particle and kinetic theories describe the arrangement and motion of particles in a substance, including the attraction between them, and explain properties such as melting and boiling points, density, compressibility, gas pressure, diffusion and expansion
VC2S8U06
Pure substances and mixtures (including solutions) can be modelled with particles; mixtures can be separated using the properties of their parts — filtration, decanting, evaporation, crystallisation, magnetic separation, distillation and chromatography
VC2S8H01
Scientific knowledge, including models and theories, can change in light of new evidence
VC2S8I01–I08
Science inquiry — questioning and predicting (I01); planning and conducting (I02–I03); processing, modelling and analysing (I04–I05); evaluating (I06–I07); communicating (I08)
VC2S8U07
Atomic theory explains the difference between elements, compounds and mixtures; symbols and chemical formulas (used above level in this unit)

Week1
  • Lesson 1 — States of matter
  • Lesson 2 — What is a scientific model?
  • Lesson 3 — Matter has mass and takes up space

VC2S8U05 VC2S8H01 VC2S8I03 VC2S8I04
  • Lesson 1 · States of matter — Know that matter is anything with mass that takes up space; describe solids, liquids and gases by shape, volume, flow and compressibility; classify everyday materials, including tricky ones.
  • I can sort materials into solid, liquid or gas and give evidence from their properties.
  • Lesson 2 · Models — Explain what a scientific model is and why scientists use one; infer what is inside a sealed box from evidence; say what makes one model better than another.
  • I can use evidence to build a model, and say one strength and one limitation of it.
  • Lesson 3 · Mass and space — Show that gases have mass and take up space; measure mass with a balance and volume with a measuring cylinder.
  • I can describe an experiment showing that air has mass, and read a balance and a measuring cylinder.
  • Lesson 1 · States of Matter — A: reading The Stuff Everything Is Made Of (C1–C8); B: what matter is, the three states, their properties, tricky materials (Worked Examples 1.1–1.3, each with a We do); C: Tasks 1.1–1.9; D: Extensions (Lesson Booklet)
  • Lesson 2 · What is a Scientific Model? — mystery-box activity: shake, tilt and listen, then draw what is inside
  • Lesson 3 · Matter Has Mass and Takes Up Space — weigh a flat and a pumped-up ball; the upturned cup in a tub of water
  • Support: every lesson opens with a short reading before any new idea; Part B has worked examples followed by a We do; every write-in table has a red first row.
  • Extend: Part D — the plasma state, non-Newtonian fluids, evaluating a classmate’s model.
  • PhET States of Matter: Basics, run from the board
  • Stile: States of Matter unit — pick the lessons that match
  • Formative: the opening quiz (what students already know in Lesson 1, then a review of earlier lessons); Part C checked in class.
  • Exit questions — classify a material and give two pieces of evidence (Lesson 1).
Week2
  • Lesson 4 — The particle model
  • Lesson 5 — Particles are always moving
  • Lesson 6 — The limits of the model

VC2S8U05 VC2S8H01 VC2S8I04
  • Lesson 4 · The particle model — Describe the arrangement, spacing, movement and attraction of particles in solids, liquids and gases; draw particle diagrams; use them to explain each state’s properties.
  • I can draw a particle diagram of each state and use it to explain why liquids flow and gases compress.
  • Lesson 5 · Moving particles — Explain that particles move faster when heated; describe Brownian motion as evidence for moving particles.
  • Lesson 6 · Limits of the model — Correct common misconceptions (particles do not melt, expand or change colour; there is nothing between gas particles); say what the model cannot show.
  • I can spot and fix a wrong particle explanation.
  • Lesson 4 · The Particle Model — students act out the three states
  • Lesson 5 · Particles Are Always Moving — smoke cell or a simulation of Brownian motion
  • Lesson 6 · The Limits of the Model — misconception card sort
  • Support: particle-diagram templates with the container drawn; a word bank of arrangement words.
  • Extend: Part D — why the model is a theory, not a picture; who first proposed particles, and the evidence that changed minds.
  • PhET States of Matter — Phase Changes screen
  • Formative: opening quiz; particle diagrams checked against the three rules; misconception sort discussed as a class.
