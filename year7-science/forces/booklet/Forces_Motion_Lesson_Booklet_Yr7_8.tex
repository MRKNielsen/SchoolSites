\lessonpart{A}{Reading and comprehension}{Teal}\label{read:1}

\begin{readingbox}{Reading --- The Forces You Cannot See}
Right now, while you sit perfectly still, you are being pushed and pulled from several directions at once. Gravity is pulling every part of your body downwards towards the centre of the Earth. At the same time, the chair beneath you is pushing upwards on you with exactly the same strength. Because these two forces are equal and opposite, you stay put. If the chair suddenly vanished, the pushing force would disappear, gravity would win, and you would fall.

People often think that astronauts on the International Space Station float because there is no gravity in space. This is not true. At the height the Space Station orbits, Earth's gravity is still about 90\% as strong as it is on the ground. Astronauts float because they --- and the whole Space Station --- are falling around the Earth at enormous speed, and they fall together. Everything inside falls at the same rate, so nothing presses against anything else. The astronauts do not feel the push of a floor, so they seem weightless.

Forces are invisible, so scientists need a way to record them. The solution is simple but powerful: draw each force as an arrow. The direction of the arrow shows which way the force acts, and the length of the arrow shows how strong it is. A picture full of invisible pushes and pulls becomes something you can measure, compare and calculate with.
\end{readingbox}

\subsection{Every Force is a Push or a Pull}\label{sub:1.1}

This is the whole idea, and it really is that simple. Whenever one object affects another --- by touching it, or from a distance --- it does so by \textbf{pushing} it or \textbf{pulling} it. There is no third option.

\begin{skillbox}{Think about it}
A magnet \emph{pulls} a paperclip. A hand \emph{pushes} a door. The Earth \emph{pulls} you downwards. A rope \emph{pulls} a sledge. The ground \emph{pushes} up on your feet.
\end{skillbox}

\subsection{What Forces Can Do}\label{sub:1.2}

A force can only change an object in a small number of ways. Learn these five and you can describe almost any situation in this unit.

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{ctab}
\begin{tabular}{|C{0.8cm}|L{5.6cm}|L{8.4cm}|}
  \thead{\#} & \thead{A force can\ldots} & \thead{Example} \\ \hline
  \talt 1 & Make a still object start moving   & A kicked football leaves the ground \\ \hline
        2 & Make a moving object stop          & Brakes bring a bicycle to a halt \\ \hline
  \talt 3 & Make an object speed up or slow down & Pedalling harder makes you accelerate \\ \hline
        4 & Change the direction of motion     & A tennis racquet sends the ball back \\ \hline
  \talt 5 & Change the shape of an object      & Squashing a drink can \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{ctab}

\subsection{Measuring Force: The Newton}\label{sub:1.3}

Force is measured in \textbf{newtons}, written \textbf{N}. The unit is named after Sir Isaac Newton, whose laws of motion you will meet in Lesson~4.

\begin{infobox}{Getting a feel for a newton}
\textbf{1 N} is roughly the force you feel holding a small apple (about 100 g) against gravity.\\
\textbf{10 N} is about the weight of a 1 kg bag of sugar.\\
\textbf{500 N} is roughly the weight of an average adult.
\end{infobox}

We measure force with a \textbf{spring scale} (also called a \emph{newton meter}). Inside is a spring: the harder you pull, the further it stretches, and a pointer moves down a scale marked in newtons.

\vspace{4pt}
\diagSpringScales
\figcap[fig:spring-scale]{Reading a spring scale. Always read at eye level, and check the size of each small division first.}

\begin{skillbox}{How to read a spring scale correctly}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, leftmargin=1.4em, topsep=1pt]
  \item Check the \textbf{largest} number on the scale --- this is the maximum it can measure.
  \item Work out what \textbf{one small division} is worth. If there are 10 small gaps between 0 and 5 N, each is 0.5 N.
  \item Hold the scale still and read at \textbf{eye level} to avoid parallax error.
  \item Read the value where the \textbf{pointer} sits, and always write the \textbf{unit (N)}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{workedex}{Reading a spring scale}
A spring scale is marked from 0 to 10\,N, with \textbf{20} small divisions between 0 and 10. The pointer sits on the \textbf{7th} small division. What is the reading?

\medskip
\textbf{Step 1 --- one division:}\quad $10 \div 20 = 0.5$\,N per division

\textbf{Step 2 --- the reading:}\quad $7 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{3.5\ \text{N}}$
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Reading a spring scale}

\subsection{Mass and Weight Are Not the Same Thing}\label{sub:1.4}

In everyday speech we use these words as if they mean the same thing. In science they are quite different, and mixing them up causes a lot of lost marks.

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{ctab}
\begin{tabular}{|L{3.2cm}|L{6.0cm}|L{6.0cm}|}
  \thead{} & \thead{Mass} & \thead{Weight} \\ \hline
  \talt What it is   & The amount of matter in an object & The \emph{force} of gravity pulling on that mass \\ \hline
        Unit         & kilograms (kg)                    & newtons (N) \\ \hline
  \talt Measured with& A balance                         & A spring scale \\ \hline
        Changes?     & Same everywhere in the universe   & Changes with the strength of gravity \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{ctab}

\begin{infobox}{The link between them}
\[ \text{Weight} = \text{mass} \times \text{gravitational field strength} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \boxed{W = mg} \]
On Earth, $g = 9.8$\,N/kg. On the Moon, $g = 1.6$\,N/kg.
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Mass and weight}
A student has a mass of 50\,kg. Find their weight on Earth, and on the Moon.

\medskip
\textbf{On Earth:}\quad $W = mg = 50 \times 9.8 = \mathbf{490\ \text{N}}$

\textbf{On the Moon:}\quad $W = mg = 50 \times 1.6 = \mathbf{80\ \text{N}}$

\medskip
Their \emph{mass} is 50\,kg in both places --- only the \emph{weight} changes.
\end{workedex}

\subsection{Contact and Non-contact Forces}\label{sub:1.5}

Forces fall into two groups. \textbf{Contact forces} only act when two objects touch. \textbf{Non-contact forces} act across a gap --- the objects do not need to touch at all.

\vspace{4pt}
\diagForceTypes
\figcap{Contact and non-contact forces}

\needspace{14\baselineskip}
\begin{workedex}{Classifying forces}
A fridge magnet holds a shopping list on the fridge door without sliding down. Name two forces acting on the magnet and classify each one.

\medskip
\textbf{Weight} pulls the magnet down --- gravity acts across a gap, so it is a \textbf{non-contact} force.

\textbf{Friction} between the magnet and the door stops it sliding --- the surfaces must touch, so it is a \textbf{contact} force.

\medskip
\textit{(The magnetic pull towards the door is also non-contact --- it is what presses the magnet on hard enough for friction to hold it.)}
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Classifying forces}
A book is pushed across a desk. After you let go, it slides and slows down. Name two forces acting on the book while it slides, and say whether each one is a contact or a non-contact force.
\answerbox{4.0cm}
\end{wedo}

\lessonpart{A}{Reading and comprehension}{Teal}\label{read:2}

\begin{readingbox}{Reading --- Winning a Tug-of-War}
In a tug-of-war, two teams pull on the same rope in opposite directions. While the rope stays still, the two pulls are exactly the same size. They are \emph{balanced}, so the net force on the rope is zero --- the rope does not move, even though both teams are pulling as hard as they can.

The moment one team pulls even a little harder, the forces become \emph{unbalanced}. There is now a net force towards the stronger team, and the rope starts to move their way. The bigger the difference between the two pulls, the faster the rope speeds up.

So how does a team pull harder? Strength helps, but the real secret is under their feet. To pull on the rope, each person pushes backwards against the ground with their shoes. Friction between the shoes and the ground stops them from sliding forwards. A team with good grip --- rough soles, a dry surface, bodies low and leaning back --- can pull harder before their feet slip. A team on a slippery surface will be dragged across, no matter how strong they are.

Coaches explain this with force arrows. They draw the rope, the two pulls and the friction on the feet, each as an arrow whose length shows its size. One look at the arrows shows which team is going to win.
\end{readingbox}

\subsection{Drawing Force Arrows --- The Three Rules}\label{sub:2.2}

\begin{skillbox}{The three rules for every force arrow}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt, leftmargin=1.4em, topsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Start the arrow on the object.} The tail sits on the object the force acts on.
  \item \textbf{Point the arrowhead the way the force acts.} Down for weight, up for the normal force, and so on.
  \item \textbf{Make the length match the size.} A 20\,N force is drawn twice as long as a 10\,N force. \emph{Always}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{skillbox}

Rule 3 is the one students most often forget --- and it matters, because the picture should tell you the answer at a glance. If two arrows are the same length, the forces are balanced. If one is longer, that is the direction the object accelerates.

\vspace{4pt}
\diagArrowScale
\figcap{Using a scale for force arrows. Here 1\,cm represents 5\,N, so a 15\,N force is drawn 3\,cm long.}

\begin{workedex}{Drawing arrows to scale}

\medskip
\textbf{Length} $=$ force $\div$ scale $= 25 \div 5 = \mathbf{5\ \text{cm}}$
\end{workedex}

\subsection{Free Body Diagrams}\label{sub:2.3}

A \textbf{free body diagram (FBD)} shows \emph{all} the forces acting on \emph{one} object, each drawn as a labelled arrow using the three rules. The object is drawn as a simple box or dot --- nothing else from the picture is included.

\diagFBD
\figcap[fig:fbd]{Balanced forces (left) and unbalanced forces (right)}

\begin{workedex}{Drawing a free body diagram}
A book with a weight of 10\,N rests on a table. Draw its free body diagram.

\medskip
\textbf{Step 1 --- find every force.} Gravity pulls the book down (\emph{weight}). The table pushes up on it (\emph{normal force}).

\textbf{Step 2 --- size each force.} The book is not moving up or down, so the table pushes up exactly as hard as gravity pulls down: both are 10\,N.

\textbf{Step 3 --- draw.} A box for the book, a 10\,N arrow down labelled $W$, and an arrow of the \emph{same length} up labelled $N$ --- as in the left of Figure~\ref{fig:fbd}.
\end{workedex}

\subsection{Balanced and Unbalanced: Net Force}\label{sub:2.4}

When you add up all the forces on an object, the single force left over is called the \textbf{net force} (or resultant force).

\begin{infobox}{Working out the net force}
\textbf{Same direction} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{add} the forces.\\
\textbf{Opposite directions} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{subtract} the smaller from the larger. The net force acts in the direction of the larger force.\\[3pt]
If the net force is \textbf{zero}, the forces are \textbf{balanced} and the motion does not change.\\
If the net force is \textbf{not zero}, the forces are \textbf{unbalanced} and the object speeds up, slows down or changes direction.
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Finding the net force}
A sledge is pulled forwards with 45\,N. Friction pushes back with 18\,N. Find the net force.

\medskip
Opposite directions, so subtract: \quad $45 - 18 = \mathbf{27\ \text{N forwards}}$

\medskip
The net force is not zero, so the forces are unbalanced and the sledge \textbf{speeds up}.
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Finding the net force}
A cyclist pedals forwards with a force of 120\,N. Air resistance and friction push back with a total of 75\,N.
\pt{a}Find the size and direction of the net force.
\answerbox{1.8cm}
\pt{b}Are the forces balanced or unbalanced? Describe what happens to the cyclist's speed.
\answerbox{1.8cm}
\end{wedo}

\begin{readingbox}{Reading --- Why Rockets Work in Empty Space}
\small % one size down so the whole reading fits on the lesson's first page
When rockets were first proposed as a way of reaching space, some newspapers scoffed at the idea. In 1920 an editorial in the \emph{New York Times} argued that a rocket could never work beyond the atmosphere, because out in the vacuum there would be nothing for the exhaust gases to push against. It seemed like common sense. A swimmer pushes against water, a runner pushes against the ground --- so surely a rocket must push against the air?

The reasoning was wrong, and the mistake came from misunderstanding Newton's Third Law. A rocket does not push against the air behind it. It pushes against \emph{its own exhaust}. The engine hurls a huge mass of hot gas backwards, and by the Third Law the gas pushes the rocket forwards with exactly the same force. Nothing outside the rocket is needed at all. In fact rockets work \emph{better} in space, because there is no air resistance slowing them down.

Out in deep space, something even more surprising happens: a spacecraft can switch its engines off and simply keep going. Far from any planet there is no air and almost nothing pushing or pulling on it, so there is almost no unbalanced force to slow it down. The two \emph{Voyager} space probes were launched in 1977. No engine has pushed them forwards for more than thirty years, yet they are still travelling away from the Sun at more than 55\,000 kilometres per hour. Here on Earth, a ball rolled along the ground soon stops --- but only because friction and air resistance act on it. Take those away, and a moving object just keeps moving.

The \emph{New York Times} eventually printed a correction --- in July 1969, the day after Apollo 11 launched for the Moon, and forty-nine years after the original editorial. It noted, briefly, that further investigation had confirmed the work of Newton in the seventeenth century, and regretted the error.
\end{readingbox}

\subsection{Newton's First Law --- The Law of Inertia}\label{sub:4.1}

In Lesson~2 you learned that when the forces on an object are balanced, its motion does not change. Nearly 340 years ago, Sir Isaac Newton made this the first of his three laws of motion.

\diagNewtonOne
\figcap[fig:newton-one]{Newton's First Law}

\begin{infobox}{First Law}
\textit{``An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted upon by an unbalanced (net) force.''}

\medskip
\textbf{Inertia} is the tendency of an object to resist changes to its motion. Greater mass $=$ greater inertia.
\end{infobox}

The First Law has two halves, and both say the same thing: \emph{without an unbalanced force, nothing about the motion changes.}

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{ctab}
\begin{tabular}{|L{3.6cm}|L{5.2cm}|L{6.2cm}|}
  \thead{If the object is\ldots} & \thead{With no unbalanced force, it\ldots} & \thead{Example} \\ \hline
  \talt At rest & stays at rest & A book on a shelf never starts sliding by itself \\ \hline
        Moving  & keeps moving at the same speed, in the same direction & A space probe coasting with its engines off \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{ctab}

\begin{skillbox}{Think about it --- why do things on Earth seem to stop by themselves?}
For almost 2000 years, people followed the Greek thinker Aristotle, who taught that a moving object needs a force to \emph{keep} it moving. It seems right: a ball rolled along the ground soon stops. Newton showed that the ball stops because \textbf{friction} and \textbf{air resistance} are acting on it. They are unbalanced forces, and they slow it down. With no friction and no air resistance, the ball would roll on forever.
\end{skillbox}

\newpage % Mass and inertia starts its own page
\subsubsection*{Mass and inertia}
The more mass an object has, the more inertia it has --- so the harder it is to change its motion in \emph{any} way.

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{ctab}
\begin{tabular}{|L{4.4cm}|L{5.0cm}|L{5.0cm}|}
  \thead{} & \thead{Empty shopping trolley} & \thead{Full shopping trolley} \\ \hline
  \talt Mass and inertia      & Small & Large \\ \hline
        Getting it moving     & Easy  & Hard \\ \hline
  \talt Stopping it           & Easy  & Hard \\ \hline
        Steering it round a corner & Easy  & Hard \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{ctab}

\begin{workedex}{At rest stays at rest}
A coin sits on a playing card on top of a glass. The card is flicked quickly sideways. Explain why the coin drops into the glass instead of flying off with the card.

\medskip
\textbf{Step 1 --- before the flick.} The coin is at rest. Its weight pulls down and the card pushes up: the forces are balanced.

\textbf{Step 2 --- the flick.} The big force acts on the \emph{card}, not the coin. Only a tiny friction force acts on the coin, for a very short time.

\textbf{Step 3 --- inertia.} The coin's inertia keeps it at rest where it is. With the card gone, nothing pushes up on the coin, so its weight is now \textbf{unbalanced} and it falls straight into the glass.
\end{workedex}

\begin{workedex}{Moving keeps moving}
A car travelling at 50\,km/h crashes into a wall and stops suddenly. Explain what happens to a passenger who is not wearing a seatbelt, and how a seatbelt helps.

\medskip
\textbf{Step 1 --- what the crash does.} The wall exerts a huge unbalanced force on the \emph{car}, so the car stops.

\textbf{Step 2 --- inertia.} No force has acted on the passenger yet, so by the First Law the passenger \textbf{keeps moving forwards at 50\,km/h} --- until they hit the dashboard or windscreen.

\textbf{Step 3 --- the seatbelt.} A seatbelt supplies the unbalanced force that slows the passenger down \emph{with} the car, spread across the strong bones of the chest and hips.
\end{workedex}

\subsection{Newton's Third Law --- Action and Reaction}\label{sub:4.2}

Forces always come in pairs. You cannot push on something without it pushing back on you. Press your hand flat against a wall and you can feel the wall pushing back.

\begin{infobox}{Third Law}
\textit{``For every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force.''}

\medskip
Action--reaction pairs \textbf{always act on different objects} --- they do \textbf{not} cancel each other.
\end{infobox}

\diagThirdLaw
\figcap[fig:third-law]{Two Third Law pairs. In each pair the forces are the same size, point in opposite directions and act on different objects.}

\begin{skillbox}{How to find the reaction force}
Write the action as ``\emph{A pushes B}'', then swap the names: the reaction is ``\emph{B pushes A}''. The reaction force is always:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, leftmargin=1.4em, topsep=2pt]
  \item the \textbf{same size} as the action force;
  \item in the \textbf{opposite direction};
  \item acting on the \textbf{other object};
  \item the \textbf{same type} of force --- a push answers a push, and a gravity pull answers a gravity pull.
\end{enumerate}
\end{skillbox}

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{ctab}
\begin{tabular}{|L{7.4cm}|L{7.4cm}|}
  \thead{Action force} & \thead{Reaction force} \\ \hline
  \talt Your foot pushes the ground backwards     & The ground pushes your foot forwards \\ \hline
        A swimmer's hands push the water backwards & The water pushes the swimmer's hands forwards \\ \hline
  \talt A rocket pushes its exhaust gas backwards  & The gas pushes the rocket forwards \\ \hline
        The Earth pulls you down (your weight)     & You pull the Earth up \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{ctab}

\subsubsection*{Why don't the two forces cancel?}
Forces only cancel when they act on the \emph{same} object. A Third Law pair always acts on \emph{two different} objects, so the forces can never cancel. When the skaters in Figure~\ref{fig:third-law} push apart, each skater has one unbalanced force acting on them --- so \emph{both} skaters move, in opposite directions. If one skater has less mass, they move away faster. Lesson~5 shows why.

\begin{workedex}{Describing a reaction force}
A student on roller skates pushes against a wall with a force of 40\,N. Describe the reaction force: name it, and give its size and direction. What happens to the student?

\medskip
\textbf{Action:} the student pushes on the wall, 40\,N, towards the wall.

\textbf{Reaction:} the \textbf{wall pushes on the student}, with \textbf{40\,N}, \textbf{away from the wall}.

\textbf{Motion:} this force acts on the student, and the skate wheels give very little friction, so it is \textbf{unbalanced}. The student rolls backwards, away from the wall.
\end{workedex}

\subsection{What is Acceleration?}\label{sub:5.1}

\textbf{Acceleration} is how quickly an object's speed changes. It is measured in \textbf{metres per second squared (m/s$^2$)}, which means ``metres per second, every second''.

\begin{infobox}{Reading an acceleration}
An acceleration of \textbf{3\,m/s$^2$} means the speed goes up by \textbf{3\,m/s every second}. Starting from rest:

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|c|}
  \hline
  \textbf{Time (s)}    & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \\ \hline
  \textbf{Speed (m/s)} & 0 & 3 & 6 & 9 \\ \hline
\end{tabular}

\medskip
Slowing down is also an acceleration. It is sometimes called a \textbf{deceleration}.
\end{infobox}

\subsection{Force, Mass and Acceleration}\label{sub:5.2}

Newton's Second Law links three things: the \textbf{net force} on an object, its \textbf{mass}, and its \textbf{acceleration}.

\begin{infobox}{Second Law}
The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it, and inversely proportional to its mass.
\[
  \boxed{F = m \times a}
  \qquad\qquad
  \boxed{a = \frac{F}{m}}
  \qquad\qquad
  \boxed{m = \frac{F}{a}}
\]
Force in newtons (N) \quad Mass in kilograms (kg) \quad Acceleration in m/s$^2$
\end{infobox}

\subsection{Using $F = ma$}\label{sub:5.3}

\begin{workedex}{Finding acceleration}
A 5\,kg trolley is pushed with a net force of 20\,N. What is its acceleration?

\medskip
\textbf{Know:}\quad $F = 20$\,N, \quad $m = 5$\,kg

\textbf{Formula:}\quad $F = m \times a$ \quad $\Rightarrow$ \quad $a = F \div m$

\textbf{Substitute:}\quad $a = 20 \div 5 = \mathbf{4\ \text{m/s}^2}$

\subsection{Always Use the Net Force}\label{sub:5.4}

The $F$ in $F = ma$ is the \textbf{net force} --- the force left over after you add up all the forces (Lesson~2). If the forces are balanced, the net force is zero, so the acceleration is zero: the object stays still, or keeps moving at a steady speed. That is exactly what the First Law says.

\begin{workedex}{Net force first}
A 10\,kg trolley is pushed forwards with 50\,N. Friction pushes back with 10\,N. Find its acceleration.

\medskip
\textbf{Step 1 --- net force:}\quad opposite directions, so subtract: \quad $50 - 10 = 40$\,N forwards

\textbf{Step 2 --- acceleration:}\quad $a = F \div m = 40 \div 10 = \mathbf{4\ \text{m/s}^2}$ \textbf{forwards}
\end{workedex}

\lessonpart{A}{Reading and comprehension}{Teal}\label{read:6}

\begin{readingbox}{Reading --- Grip}
Friction is the force that makes walking possible. With every step, your shoe pushes backwards against the ground, and friction pushes you forwards. On ice, where friction is tiny, your foot slips backwards and you go nowhere.

Friction comes from the roughness of surfaces. Even surfaces that look smooth are covered in tiny bumps when you look at them under a microscope. When two surfaces press together, the bumps catch on each other and resist sliding. Press the surfaces together harder and the bumps push further into each other and catch more, so the friction is bigger. That is why a full box is harder to slide across the floor than an empty one.

Sometimes engineers want as much friction as possible --- in the rubber of a car tyre, the soles of running shoes or the grip tape on a skateboard. Other times they want as little as possible: oil in an engine and wax on skis reduce friction, so parts slide easily and less energy is wasted as heat.

In this experiment you will measure friction with a spring balance, then add masses to a wooden block so it presses harder on the bench. When you pull the block along at a \emph{steady} speed, your pull and the friction are balanced --- so the reading on the spring balance is equal to the friction force.
\end{readingbox}

\small % one size down so the whole reading fits on the lesson's first page
Look carefully at a traditional roller coaster --- the kind that is pulled up its first hill by a chain --- and you will notice something: the first hill is always the highest one on the track. This is not a matter of taste or thrill design. It is a rule imposed by physics, and no engineer can get around it.

The train itself has no engine. A chain drags it slowly up that first hill, and after that the ride is entirely powered by gravity. Hauling the train to the top stores energy in it --- gravitational potential energy --- and the amount stored depends on how high the train is lifted. As the train plunges down, that stored energy is converted into kinetic energy, the energy of movement, and the train reaches its greatest speed at the bottom. Climbing the next hill converts kinetic energy back into potential energy, and so the ride continues, trading one form for the other.

The catch is that the swap is never perfect. Every time the wheels turn, a little energy is lost as heat through friction, and a little more is lost as sound --- that familiar roar. Because energy cannot be created, the train can never climb higher than it started. Each successive hill must be a little lower than the one before, and the ride must end before the energy runs out entirely.

At the end of the ride the train still has some kinetic energy, and it has to be removed safely. Many modern coasters use magnetic brakes: metal fins under the train pass between strong magnets, and the magnets slow the fins down without ever touching them. The train's kinetic energy is not destroyed --- it ends up as thermal energy, warming the fins and the air around them.
\end{readingbox}

\subsection{Forms of Energy}\label{sub:7.1}

\textbf{Energy} is what makes things happen: it is needed to move something, heat it, light it up or make a sound. Energy is measured in \textbf{joules (J)}. It comes in several forms. Some are \emph{stored} energy, waiting to be used; others show that something is \emph{happening}.

\subsection{Transformations and Energy Chains}\label{sub:7.2}

Energy can change from one form to another. This is called an \textbf{energy transformation}. An \textbf{energy chain} shows each step, with an arrow for every change. The trebuchet in Figure~\ref{fig:energy-types} turns the gravitational potential energy of its raised counterweight into kinetic energy of the arm, and then of the ball.

\begin{workedex}{An energy chain for a bouncing ball}
A ball is held above the floor, dropped, and bounces back up. Write the energy chain from the moment it is let go to the top of its bounce.

\medskip
\textbf{Falling:} gravitational potential $\rightarrow$ kinetic \quad(it speeds up as it falls)

\textbf{Hitting the floor:} kinetic $\rightarrow$ elastic potential \quad(the ball squashes)

\textbf{Bouncing up:} elastic potential $\rightarrow$ kinetic $\rightarrow$ gravitational potential

\medskip
At every step a little energy is \textbf{wasted} as thermal energy and sound --- you can hear the bounce.
\end{workedex}

\subsection{Conservation of Energy and Efficiency}\label{sub:7.3}

\begin{infobox}{The Law of Conservation of Energy}
Energy \textbf{cannot be created or destroyed}. It can only be transformed from one form to another, or transferred from one object to another. The total amount of energy at the end is always the same as at the start.
\end{infobox}

So where does energy ``go''? Not all of it ends up where we want it. The energy that does the job we want is \textbf{useful} energy. The rest is \textbf{wasted} energy --- usually thermal energy and sound, which spread out into the surroundings and are hard to use again. A \textbf{Sankey diagram} shows this with arrows whose width matches the amount of energy.

\diagSankey
\figcap[fig:sankey]{A Sankey diagram. The useful and wasted arrows always add up to the energy in.}

\begin{infobox}{Efficiency}
\[ \text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful energy out}}{\text{total energy in}} \times 100\% \]
A machine that wastes no energy would be 100\% efficient. No real machine is.
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Calculating efficiency}
An old-style light bulb is supplied with 100\,J of electrical energy. It gives out 5\,J of light. How much energy is wasted, and what is its efficiency?

\medskip
\textbf{Wasted:}\quad $100 - 5 = \mathbf{95\ \text{J}}$ \quad (as thermal energy --- the bulb gets hot)

\textbf{Efficiency:}\quad $5 \div 100 \times 100\% = \mathbf{5\%}$
\end{workedex}

\subsection{Calculating Gravitational Potential and Kinetic Energy}\label{sub:7.4}

\begin{infobox}{Two energy formulas}
\[ \text{GPE} = m \times g \times h \qquad\qquad \text{KE} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times m \times v^2 \]
$m$ = mass (kg) \quad $g = 9.8$\,N/kg on Earth \quad $h$ = height (m) \quad $v$ = speed (m/s) \quad Energy in joules (J)
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Gravitational potential energy}
A 2\,kg ball is held at a height of 5\,m. Find its gravitational potential energy.

\medskip
$\text{GPE} = mgh = 2 \times 9.8 \times 5 = \mathbf{98\ \text{J}}$
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Gravitational potential energy}
A 5\,kg box is lifted onto a shelf 1.5\,m high. Find its gravitational potential energy. Use $g = 9.8$\,N/kg.
\answerbox{2.4cm}
\end{wedo}

\begin{workedex}{Kinetic energy}
A 4\,kg dog runs at 3\,m/s. Find its kinetic energy.

\medskip
\textbf{Square the speed first:}\quad $v^2 = 3 \times 3 = 9$

\textbf{Then:}\quad $\text{KE} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times m \times v^2 = 0.5 \times 4 \times 9 = \mathbf{18\ \text{J}}$
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Kinetic energy}
A 6\,kg bowling ball rolls at 2\,m/s. Find its kinetic energy.
\answerbox{2.4cm}
\end{wedo}

When the 2\,kg ball in Worked Example~\ref{we:7.3} is dropped, its 98\,J of gravitational potential energy becomes kinetic energy. Just before it lands it has close to 98\,J of kinetic energy --- a little less, because some is wasted pushing through the air.

\lessonpart{A}{Reading and comprehension}{Teal}\label{read:8}

\begin{readingbox}{Reading --- The Rules of the Bounce}
Every ball used in serious sport has to pass a bounce test. A tennis ball, for example, is dropped from a height of 254 centimetres onto concrete. To be approved for play, it must bounce back up to between 135 and 147 centimetres --- a little more than half the height it fell from.

Why doesn't a ball bounce all the way back up? As it falls, its gravitational potential energy becomes kinetic energy. When it hits the ground the ball squashes, and for a split second its energy is stored as elastic potential energy, like a squashed spring. The ball then springs back into shape and pushes itself off the ground. But the rubber does not spring back perfectly: some energy becomes thermal energy inside the rubber, and some becomes the sound of the bounce. Less energy comes out than went in, so the ball cannot rise as high as it fell.

Squash players know this well. A cold squash ball hardly bounces at all, so players hit it hard for a few minutes before a match. As the ball warms up, the air inside it pushes harder on the rubber, and the rubber itself becomes springier, so the ball bounces higher. The ball used by the best players in the world bounces the least of all, which makes it the hardest to keep in play.

In this experiment you will run your own bounce test, and use the heights to work out how efficient each bounce is.
\end{readingbox}

\subsection{Measuring a Bounce}\label{sub:8.1}

When the ball is held still at the drop height, all of its useful energy is gravitational potential energy. At the top of the bounce it is still again, so all of its energy is gravitational potential energy once more. Because $\text{GPE} = mgh$, and the mass and $g$ do not change, comparing the two \emph{heights} tells you how much of the energy was kept.

\diagBounceSetup
\expfigcap{Measuring the drop height $h_1$ and the bounce height $h_2$ against metre rulers fixed to a wall.}

\begin{infobox}{Bounce efficiency}
\[ \text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{bounce height } h_2}{\text{drop height } h_1} \times 100\% \]
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Calculating bounce efficiency}
A ball is dropped from 100\,cm and bounces back up to 62\,cm. Find the efficiency of the bounce, and the percentage of energy wasted.

\medskip
\textbf{Efficiency:}\quad $62 \div 100 \times 100\% = \mathbf{62\%}$

\textbf{Wasted:}\quad $100\% - 62\% = \mathbf{38\%}$ \quad (as thermal energy and sound)
\end{workedex}

Tennis ball (or another rubber ball) \quad Two metre rulers, or a tape measure, fixed upright to a wall \quad Sticky tape \quad Hard, flat floor \quad Phone with slow-motion video (optional)

\subsubsection*{Method}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Fix the metre rulers upright against the wall, with 0\,cm touching the floor.
  \item Hold the ball beside the rulers so that the \textbf{bottom} of the ball is level with the 40\,cm mark.
  \item Let go --- do not push or throw the ball.
  \item Watch (or film) the bounce, and read the height of the bottom of the ball at the top of its first bounce. Record it.
  \item Repeat steps 2--4 so that you have 3 trials.
  \item Repeat for drop heights of 80\,cm, 120\,cm and 160\,cm.
\end{enumerate}

Keep every drop height within reach while you are standing on the floor --- do not stand on chairs or desks. Clear the area of bags and chairs, and let the ball come to rest before you pick it up.
\end{infobox}

\subsection{Simple Machines}\label{sub:9.1}

A \textbf{simple machine} is a device that changes the \emph{size} or the \emph{direction} of a force, making a job easier. There are six of them.

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{ctab}
\begin{tabular}{|L{3.4cm}|L{6.6cm}|L{5.0cm}|}
  \thead{Simple machine} & \thead{How it helps} & \thead{Example} \\ \hline
  \talt Lever            & A bar that turns on a pivot         & Seesaw, crowbar, scissors \\ \hline
        Inclined plane   & A slope: less force, longer distance & Ramp, wheelchair ramp \\ \hline
  \talt Wedge            & Two slopes back to back that push things apart & Axe, knife, doorstop \\ \hline
        Wheel and axle   & A large wheel turns a small axle    & Doorknob, steering wheel \\ \hline
  \talt Pulley           & A wheel and rope that change the direction of a pull & Flagpole, crane \\ \hline
        Screw            & An inclined plane wrapped around a rod & Jar lid, screw, bolt \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{ctab}

\begin{skillbox}{Think about it --- no free energy}
A simple machine never gives you more energy than you put in. If it lets you push with \textbf{less force}, you must push through a \textbf{longer distance}. That is the trade every simple machine makes.
\end{skillbox}

\subsection{The Parts of a Lever}\label{sub:9.2}

A \textbf{lever} is a rigid bar that turns about a fixed point called the \textbf{fulcrum} (or pivot). The \textbf{effort} is the force you apply; the \textbf{load} is the force the lever moves or lifts. The distance from the fulcrum to the effort is the \textbf{effort arm}; the distance from the fulcrum to the load is the \textbf{load arm}.

\diagLeverParts
\figcap[fig:lever-parts]{The parts of a lever. A long effort arm lets a small effort lift a large load.}

\begin{infobox}{The law of the lever}
A lever is balanced when \quad $\text{effort} \times \text{effort arm} = \text{load} \times \text{load arm}$.
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Balancing a seesaw}
A 300\,N child sits 2\,m from the pivot of a seesaw. Where must a 400\,N child sit on the other side to balance it?

\medskip
\textbf{Law of the lever:}\quad $300 \times 2 = 400 \times d$

\textbf{Solve:}\quad $600 = 400 \times d$ \quad so \quad $d = 600 \div 400 = \mathbf{1.5\ \text{m}}$

\medskip
The heavier child sits \emph{closer} to the pivot.
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Balancing a seesaw}
A 200\,N child sits 3\,m from the pivot of a seesaw. Where must a 500\,N adult sit on the other side to balance it?
\answerbox{2.8cm}
\end{wedo}

\subsection{Mechanical Advantage}\label{sub:9.3}

\begin{infobox}{Mechanical advantage (MA)}
\[ \text{MA} = \frac{\text{load}}{\text{effort}} = \frac{\text{effort arm}}{\text{load arm}} \]
MA bigger than 1: the lever \textbf{multiplies your force}. \quad MA smaller than 1: you lose force but \textbf{gain speed and distance}.
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Mechanical advantage}
A lever has an effort arm of 2.0\,m and a load arm of 0.5\,m. Find its MA, and the load that a 100\,N effort can lift.

\medskip
\textbf{MA:}\quad $2.0 \div 0.5 = \mathbf{4}$

\textbf{Load:}\quad $\text{load} = \text{MA} \times \text{effort} = 4 \times 100 = \mathbf{400\ \text{N}}$
\end{workedex}

\end{wedo}

\subsection{The Three Classes of Lever}\label{sub:9.4}

Levers are sorted into three classes by which part is in the \textbf{middle}. A memory aid: \textbf{F--L--E, 1--2--3}: the \textbf{F}ulcrum is in the middle of a Class 1 lever, the \textbf{L}oad in the middle of Class 2, and the \textbf{E}ffort in the middle of Class 3.

\diagLevers
\figcap[fig:levers]{The three classes of lever (F\,=\,Fulcrum, E\,=\,Effort, L\,=\,Load)}

\vspace{2pt}
\begin{ctab}
\begin{tabular}{|L{2.0cm}|L{3.6cm}|L{4.4cm}|L{5.0cm}|}
  \thead{Class} & \thead{In the middle} & \thead{What it does} & \thead{Examples} \\ \hline
  \talt Class 1 & Fulcrum & Can multiply force or speed & Seesaw, crowbar, scissors \\ \hline
        Class 2 & Load    & Always multiplies force (MA $>$ 1) & Wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener \\ \hline
  \talt Class 3 & Effort  & Always multiplies speed (MA $<$ 1) & Tweezers, your forearm, a fishing rod \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{ctab}

\subsection{The Trebuchet as a Class 1 Lever}\label{sub:9.5}

A trebuchet arm turns on a pivot between the counterweight and the projectile, so it is a \textbf{Class 1 lever}. The falling counterweight is the \textbf{effort}, on the \emph{short} arm. The projectile is the \textbf{load}, at the end of the \emph{long} arm. Its MA is therefore \textbf{less than 1}: it gives away force to gain speed. In the time the counterweight drops a short distance, the end of the long arm sweeps through a much longer arc --- so it moves much faster.

\diagTrebuchetLever
\figcap[fig:trebuchet-lever]{A trebuchet is a Class 1 lever with a short effort arm and a long load arm.}

\begin{workedex}{The trebuchet's mechanical advantage}
A trebuchet has a short arm of 0.4\,m and a long arm of 2.0\,m. Find its MA, and how many times faster the end of the long arm moves than the counterweight.

\medskip
\textbf{MA:}\quad effort arm $\div$ load arm $= 0.4 \div 2.0 = \mathbf{0.2}$ \quad (less than 1: force is given away)

\textbf{Speed:}\quad long arm $\div$ short arm $= 2.0 \div 0.4 = \mathbf{5}$ \quad so the arm tip moves \textbf{5 times} as fast as the counterweight.
\end{workedex}

\answerbox{1.6cm}
\pt{b}How many times faster does the end of the long arm move than the counterweight?
\answerbox{1.6cm}
\end{wedo}

\begin{readingbox}{Reading --- The Steelyard: Weighing with a Lever}
Long before electronic scales, market sellers weighed goods with a \emph{steelyard}. A steelyard is a lever: a long bar hanging from a hook near one end, which acts as the pivot. The goods hang from the short side, close to the pivot. A small, heavy weight slides along the long side, which is marked with a scale. The seller slides the weight out until the bar hangs level, then reads the weight of the goods off the scale.

How can a small weight balance a heavy load? The answer is distance. A lever balances when effort $\times$ effort arm equals load $\times$ load arm. Far from the pivot, a small weight turns the bar as strongly as a large weight close to it. A sliding weight ten times further from the pivot than the goods can balance goods ten times heavier than itself.

Steelyards are very old: bronze ones have been found at Pompeii, the Roman town buried by Vesuvius in AD~79. About three hundred years earlier, the Greek mathematician Archimedes had worked out the law of the lever that makes them work.

In this experiment you will do what a steelyard does: balance a heavy load near the pivot with a lighter mass further away, measure both arms, and work out your lever's mechanical advantage.
\end{readingbox}

\subsection{The Set-up}\label{sub:10.1}

A metre stick rests on a fulcrum at its 50\,cm mark, so the stick balances by itself. A 100\,g \textbf{load} stands on the 30\,cm mark, 20\,cm from the fulcrum, and stays there for the whole experiment. On the other side you place an \textbf{effort} mass and slide it until the stick balances level. The distance from the fulcrum to the effort is the \textbf{effort arm}.

\diagExpFourSetup
\expfigcap{The set-up. Measure each arm from the fulcrum to the \emph{centre} of the mass. Here a 50\,g effort balances the 100\,g load at an effort arm of 40\,cm.}

\subsection{Two Ways to Find the Mechanical Advantage}\label{sub:10.2}

\begin{infobox}{Mechanical advantage, two ways}
\textbf{From the arms:} \quad $\text{MA} = \text{effort arm} \div \text{load arm}$\\[2pt]
\textbf{From the forces:} \quad $\text{MA} = \text{load} \div \text{effort}$\\[4pt]
Weight is proportional to mass ($W = mg$), so you can compare the forces using the masses in grams. When the lever is balanced, the two answers should agree.
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Mechanical advantage of a balanced lever}
A 100\,g load stands 20\,cm from the fulcrum. A 50\,g effort balances it 40\,cm from the fulcrum. Find the MA from the arms and from the forces.

\medskip
\textbf{From the arms:}\quad $40 \div 20 = \mathbf{2}$

\textbf{From the forces:}\quad $100 \div 50 = \mathbf{2}$

\medskip
They agree. This is the law of the lever: $100 \times 20 = 50 \times 40 = 2000$ on both sides.
\end{workedex}

\subsection{Making it a Fair Test}\label{sub:10.3}

You will change the \textbf{effort mass} (50, 100, 150 and 200\,g) and measure the \textbf{effort arm} needed to balance the stick. From these you calculate the MA. Everything else stays the same: the 100\,g load, its place on the 30\,cm mark, the fulcrum at the 50\,cm mark, and the same metre stick.

\needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{scaffold}{Prediction --- before you begin}
As the effort mass increases from 50\,g to 200\,g, will the effort arm get longer or shorter? What will happen to the MA? Give a reason.
\answerbox{2cm}
\end{scaffold}

\subsubsection*{Equipment}
Metre stick \quad Triangular prism or thick pencil (fulcrum) \quad One 100\,g mass (load) \quad Four 50\,g slotted masses (effort) \quad Sticky tack

\subsubsection*{Method}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Rest the metre stick on the fulcrum at the 50\,cm mark and check that it balances level. If it balances a little to one side of 50\,cm, use that point as the fulcrum and measure every arm from there.
  \item Stand the 100\,g load on the 30\,cm mark, so the load arm is 20\,cm. Fix it in place with a little sticky tack. It stays there for the whole experiment.
  \item Stand one 50\,g mass on the other side of the fulcrum. Slide it until the stick balances level. Record the effort arm: the distance from the fulcrum to the centre of the mass.
  \item Take the effort mass off and repeat step 3 twice more, so you have 3 trials.
  \item Repeat steps 3--4 with an effort of 100\,g, 150\,g and 200\,g (stack the 50\,g masses).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Turning Effects}\label{sub:11.1}

A force that acts on a lever tries to turn it about the pivot. Its \textbf{turning effect} (also called its \emph{moment}) depends on the size of the force and how far from the pivot it acts.

\begin{infobox}{Turning effect}
\[ \text{turning effect} = \text{force} \times \text{distance from the pivot} \]
Force in newtons (N), distance in metres (m), turning effect in \textbf{newton metres (N\,m)}.\\[3pt]
A lever balances when the total turning effect one way equals the total turning effect the other way. With two or more loads on one side, \textbf{add} their turning effects.
\end{infobox}

The law of the lever from Lesson~9 is a special case: effort $\times$ effort arm and load $\times$ load arm are the two turning effects.

\begin{workedex}{Two children on one side}
On one side of a seesaw, a 300\,N child sits 2.0\,m from the pivot and a 200\,N child sits 1.0\,m from the pivot. Where must a 400\,N child sit on the other side to balance it?

\medskip
\textbf{Left side:}\quad $300 \times 2.0 + 200 \times 1.0 = 600 + 200 = 800$\,N\,m

\textbf{Right side:}\quad $400 \times d = 800$ \quad so \quad $d = 800 \div 400 = \mathbf{2.0\ \text{m}}$
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Two children on one side}
On one side of a seesaw, a 200\,N child sits 1.5\,m from the pivot and a 300\,N child sits 0.5\,m from the pivot. Where must a 450\,N adult sit on the other side to balance it?
\answerbox{3.0cm}
\end{wedo}

\subsection{Arcs and Tip Speed}\label{sub:11.2}

When a lever arm turns, every point on it turns through the \textbf{same angle in the same time}. A point far from the pivot travels around a bigger circle, so it covers a longer arc --- and moves faster.

\diagArmArcs
\figcap[fig:arm-arcs]{Both ends of the arm turn through the same angle in the same time, but the end of the long arm travels a much longer arc.}

\begin{infobox}{Arc length and speed}
\[ \text{arc length} = \frac{\text{angle}}{360} \times 2 \times \pi \times r \qquad\qquad \text{average speed} = \frac{\text{arc length}}{\text{time}} \]
$r$ = distance from the pivot (m) \quad Use $\pi \approx 3.14$.\\[3pt]
For two points on the same arm: \quad $\dfrac{\text{tip speed}}{\text{counterweight speed}} = \dfrac{\text{long arm}}{\text{short arm}}$
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Speed of the arm tip}
A trebuchet's long arm is 2.0\,m long. During a launch it sweeps through 90$^\circ$ in 0.25\,s. Find the arc travelled by the tip, and the tip's average speed.

\medskip
\textbf{Arc:}\quad $\tfrac{90}{360} \times 2 \times 3.14 \times 2.0 = 0.25 \times 12.56 = 3.14$\,m

\textbf{Speed:}\quad $3.14 \div 0.25 = \mathbf{12.6\ \text{m/s}}$
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Speed of the arm tip}

\end{wedo}

\begin{workedex}{Speed of the counterweight end}
The same trebuchet has a short arm of 0.4\,m. How fast does the counterweight end of the arm move?

\medskip
\textbf{Ratio:}\quad long arm $\div$ short arm $= 2.0 \div 0.4 = 5$, so the tip moves 5 times as fast.

\textbf{Speed:}\quad $12.6 \div 5 \approx \mathbf{2.5\ \text{m/s}}$
\end{workedex}

\subsection{Energy Sets the Limit}\label{sub:11.3}

The arms share out the speed, but the energy comes from the falling counterweight. The stone's kinetic energy can never be more than the gravitational potential energy the counterweight gives up --- and because some energy is always wasted, it is always less.

\begin{infobox}{From counterweight to launch speed}
$\text{GPE lost} = m \times g \times h$ \quad(counterweight mass and drop)\\[2pt]
$\text{KE of stone} = \text{efficiency} \times \text{GPE lost}$\\[2pt]
$\text{KE} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{2 \times \text{KE} \div m}$ \quad(stone mass)
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Launch speed from energy}
A 20\,kg counterweight falls 0.8\,m. The trebuchet is 25\% efficient and the stone has a mass of 0.2\,kg. Find the stone's launch speed. Use $g = 9.8$\,N/kg.

\medskip
\textbf{GPE lost:}\quad $20 \times 9.8 \times 0.8 = 156.8$\,J

\textbf{KE of stone:}\quad $0.25 \times 156.8 = 39.2$\,J

\textbf{Speed:}\quad $v^2 = 2 \times 39.2 \div 0.2 = 392$, \quad so \quad $v = \sqrt{392} \approx \mathbf{19.8\ \text{m/s}}$
\end{workedex}

\end{wedo}

\begin{readingbox}{Reading --- Galileo and the Two Motions}
How does a cannonball fly? In 1537 the Italian mathematician Niccolò Tartaglia published one of the first books to use mathematics to study gunnery. He described a cannonball leaving the barrel in a straight line, then curving around in an arc, and finally dropping straight down to the ground. Gunnery books repeated his ideas for many years.

About a hundred years later, Galileo Galilei worked out what really happens, and he published it in 1638. He realised that a projectile is doing \emph{two things at once}, and that the two motions do not affect each other. Sideways, once the ball is launched nothing pushes or pulls it (if we ignore the air), so it keeps moving sideways at a steady speed. Downwards, gravity pulls it, so it speeds up as it falls --- exactly as if it had simply been dropped. Put a steady sideways motion together with a speeding-up downwards motion and you get a smooth curve called a \emph{parabola}, with no straight sections at all.

The result that surprises people most is this. Launch one ball sideways and drop an identical ball from the same height at the same moment: they hit the ground at the same time. The launched ball lands far away, but its sideways speed makes no difference to how quickly gravity pulls it down.

That is why, in this unit, you can measure the strength of gravity just by timing how long a launched ball takes to fall. Galileo's idea replaced a model that had been trusted for a century --- a good example of science changing when someone finds better evidence and better reasoning.
\end{readingbox}

\subsection{What is a Projectile?}\label{sub:12.1}

A \textbf{projectile} is an object that has been launched and is then moving with only gravity acting on it. A thrown ball, water from a drinking fountain and a stone flung by a trebuchet are all projectiles. (We ignore air resistance, which is small for heavy, slow objects.) The curved path of a projectile is called its \textbf{trajectory}.

\subsection{Two Independent Motions}\label{sub:12.2}

\subsection{Time of Flight}\label{sub:12.3}

The time a projectile spends in the air depends only on the height it falls from, not on its sideways speed.

\begin{infobox}{Key equations --- launched sideways}
\[
  h = \tfrac{1}{2}g t^2
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}
  \qquad\qquad
  R = v \times t
  \qquad\qquad
  g = \frac{2h}{t^2}
\]
$h$ = drop height (m) \quad $t$ = time of flight (s) \quad $g = 9.8$\,m/s$^2$\\
$v$ = sideways launch speed (m/s) \quad $R$ = range (m)
\end{infobox}

\begin{workedex}{Time of flight}
A ball rolls off a table 1.25\,m high. How long does it take to land?

\medskip
\textbf{Step 1:}\quad $2h = 2 \times 1.25 = 2.5$

\textbf{Step 2:}\quad $2.5 \div 9.8 = 0.255$

\textbf{Step 3:}\quad $t = \sqrt{0.255} \approx \mathbf{0.51\ \text{s}}$
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Time of flight}

\answerbox{2.8cm}
\end{wedo}

\subsection{How Far Does It Go?}\label{sub:12.4}

While the ball falls, it keeps moving sideways at its launch speed. So the distance it travels sideways is its launch speed multiplied by its time in the air.

\begin{workedex}{Range}
The ball in Worked Example~\ref{we:12.1} leaves the table at 3.0\,m/s. How far from the table does it land?

\medskip
$R = v \times t = 3.0 \times 0.51 \approx \mathbf{1.5\ \text{m}}$
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Range}
A ball rolls off a table 0.60\,m high at 2.5\,m/s, and is in the air for 0.35\,s. How far from the table does it land?
\answerbox{2.4cm}
\end{wedo}

\subsection{Measuring $g$}\label{sub:12.5}

Rearranging $h = \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ gives $g = 2h \div t^2$. Measure the drop height and the time of flight, and you can calculate the strength of gravity. A slow-motion video gives the time:
\[ t = \text{number of frames} \div \text{frames per second} \]

\begin{workedex}{Finding $g$ from a video}
A ball rolls off a bench 0.80\,m high. A video filmed at 120 frames per second shows 48 frames between the ball leaving the bench and landing. Find $t$, then $g$.

\medskip
\textbf{Time:}\quad $t = 48 \div 120 = 0.40$\,s

\textbf{Gravity:}\quad $g = 2h \div t^2 = (2 \times 0.80) \div (0.40 \times 0.40) = 1.6 \div 0.16 = \mathbf{10\ \text{m/s}^2}$

\medskip
This is close to the accepted value of 9.8\,m/s$^2$.
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Finding $g$ from a video}
A ball rolls off a bench 0.44\,m high. A video filmed at 240 frames per second shows 72 frames between the ball leaving the bench and landing.
\pt{a}Find the time of the fall, $t$.
\answerbox{1.6cm}
\pt{b}Use $g = 2h \div t^2$ to find $g$.
\answerbox{1.8cm}
\end{wedo}

\lessonpart{A}{Reading and comprehension}{Teal}\label{read:13}

\begin{readingbox}{Reading --- Too Fast to Time}
Drop a ball from the height of a table and it hits the floor in less than half a second. That is far too quick to time with a stopwatch: by the time your thumb reacts, the fall is almost over. For most of history, nobody could measure a fall directly.

Galileo found a way around the problem in the early 1600s. Instead of dropping balls, he rolled them down long, gently sloping ramps. The slope slowed the motion down enough to time it, using a water clock: water dripped steadily into a cup while the ball rolled, and he weighed the water afterwards. From his ramps he showed that a falling object keeps speeding up at a steady rate.

Today you carry a better timer in your pocket. Many phones film in slow motion at 120 or 240 frames per second. Every frame is a snapshot taken a fixed, tiny time after the last one, so counting frames is like using a stopwatch that ticks 240 times a second.

In this experiment you will roll a ball off a table, film its fall in slow motion, count the frames, and use the time to measure the strength of gravity, $g$ --- then compare your answer with the accepted value of 9.8\,m/s$^2$.
\end{readingbox}

\begin{readingbox}{Reading --- Why Long Jumpers Don't Take Off at 45$^\circ$}
Ask a physics student how to throw something as far as possible and they will probably say ``at 45 degrees''. On flat ground, with no air, they are right: a ball launched at 45$^\circ$ goes further than at any other angle with the same speed. Launch it flatter and it hits the ground too soon. Launch it steeper and it spends its speed going up instead of along.

So why do the world's best long jumpers take off at only about 20$^\circ$? Because a jumper's speed is not the same at every angle. A sprinter can run at more than 10\,m/s, but nobody can spring \emph{upwards} that fast. To leave the board at 45$^\circ$, a jumper would have to slow right down. A flatter take-off keeps almost all the speed of the run-up, and that matters more than the angle.

Shot-putters also launch lower than 45$^\circ$, at about 35--40$^\circ$. They release the shot from above head height, so it has further to fall, and they can push harder at a flatter angle.

Medieval engineers had to solve the same problem with trebuchets. The stone leaves the sling high above the ground, and air resistance slows it the whole way. The best release angle for a real trebuchet is usually a little below 45$^\circ$ --- and the only way to find it is to test.
\end{readingbox}

\subsection{Launching at an Angle}\label{sub:14.1}

In Lesson~12 every ball was launched \emph{sideways}. Now the ball is launched upwards at an angle. Its speed is shared between two directions: part of it carries the ball \emph{up} (which decides how long it stays in the air) and part carries it \emph{along} (which decides how far it travels each second). A steep angle gives a long time in the air but little sideways speed; a flat angle gives plenty of sideways speed but a short time.

\diagRangeAngles
\figcap[fig:range-angles]{One launch speed, five launch angles. 45$^\circ$ goes furthest. 30$^\circ$ and 60$^\circ$ land in the same place, and so do 15$^\circ$ and 75$^\circ$.}

\subsection{The Best Angle}\label{sub:14.2}

\begin{infobox}{Range on flat ground (ignoring air resistance)}
\[ R = \frac{v^2 \times \sin(2\theta)}{g} \qquad\qquad R_{\max} = \frac{v^2}{g} \ \text{ at } \theta = 45^\circ \]
$R$ = range (m) \quad $v$ = launch speed (m/s) \quad $\theta$ = launch angle \quad $g = 9.8$\,m/s$^2$\\[2pt]
Two angles that add to 90$^\circ$ (\textbf{complementary angles}, such as 30$^\circ$ and 60$^\circ$) give the same range. You will be given any $\sin$ values you need.
\end{infobox}

\medskip
\textbf{Maximum range is at 45$^\circ$:}\quad $R_{\max} = v^2 \div g = (20 \times 20) \div 9.8 = 400 \div 9.8 \approx \mathbf{40.8\ \text{m}}$
\end{workedex}

\medskip
\textbf{Same range:}\quad the angles must add to 90$^\circ$, so $90 - 25 = \mathbf{65^\circ}$

\textbf{Longer in the air:}\quad the \textbf{65$^\circ$} launch --- it goes much higher, so it takes longer to come down.
\end{workedex}

\begin{wedo}{Complementary angles}
A ball launched at 35$^\circ$ lands 9.4\,m away.
\pt{a}Which other launch angle, at the same speed, lands in the same place?
\answerbox{1.4cm}
\pt{b}Which of the two launches keeps the ball in the air longer? Explain.
\answerbox{1.8cm}
\end{wedo}

\begin{workedex}{Using the range equation}
A ball is launched at 12\,m/s at 30$^\circ$. Find its range. Use $\sin 60^\circ = 0.87$.

\medskip
\textbf{Double the angle:}\quad $2 \times 30^\circ = 60^\circ$, so $\sin(2\theta) = 0.87$

\textbf{Range:}\quad $R = (12 \times 12 \times 0.87) \div 9.8 = 125.3 \div 9.8 \approx \mathbf{12.8\ \text{m}}$
\end{workedex}

\pt{a}Find $\sin(2\theta)$.
\answerbox{1.4cm}
\pt{b}Find the range.
\answerbox{1.8cm}
\end{wedo}

\subsection{Real Launches}\label{sub:14.3}

\begin{infobox}{Why real launches are set lower than 45$^\circ$}
\textbf{Launch height:} a stone released high above the ground has extra time to fall, so a flatter launch, with more sideways speed, goes further.\\[2pt]
\textbf{Air resistance:} the air slows the stone the whole way. A steep, high path spends longer in the air, so it loses more.\\[2pt]
\textbf{Speed:} for people (and some machines) the launch speed is higher at a flatter angle.
\end{infobox}
