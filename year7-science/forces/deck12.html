Galileo and the Two Motions

Before you read

In the 1500s, people thought a cannonball flew in a straight line, then curved, then fell straight down. Do you think that is right?

Read Galileo and the Two Motions in your booklet. Then answer questions C1–C8.

Check after reading

A ball launched sideways and a ball dropped from the same height land at the same time. Why?

AnswerThe two motions are independent. Gravity pulls both balls down in exactly the same way; the launched ball’s sideways speed makes no difference to how quickly it falls.

Booklet p.137 · §12.1

Part B · Theory

What is a projectile?

Projectile

An object that has been launched and is then moving with only gravity acting on it (we ignore air resistance). Its curved path is its trajectory.

Projectile or not?

(a) a netball after it leaves the shooter’s hands  (b) a plane flying level  (c) a marble rolling off a table  (d) a firework rocket while it is still burning

Answer(a) yes  (b) no — its engines keep pushing  (c) yes, once it leaves the edge  (d) no — a burning rocket is still being pushed. Only gravity may act on a projectile.

Put a steady sideways motion together with a speeding-up downwards motion and you get a smooth curve: a parabola.

At every moment the launched ball is at the same height as the dropped ball — so they land together.

Booklet p.138 · §12.3 · Worked Example 12.1

Part B · Theory

I do Time of flight

The time in the air depends only on the drop height, not on the sideways speed.

Key equations: launched sideways

\(h = \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2h}{g}}\)

\(R = v \times t \qquad g = \dfrac{2h}{t^2}\)

\(g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\)

We do

A ball rolls off a table 1.0 m high. How long does it take to land?

Answer\(2h = 2.0\);  \(2.0 \div 9.8 = 0.204\);  \(t = \sqrt{0.204} \approx 0.45\ \text{s}\)
Worked Example 12.1

A ball rolls off a table 1.25 m high. How long does it take to land?

  1. 1 \(2h = 2 \times 1.25 = 2.5\)
  2. 2 \(2.5 \div 9.8 = 0.255\)
  3. ✓ \(t = \sqrt{0.255} \approx 0.51\ \text{s}\)

I do How far does it go?

While the ball falls, it keeps moving sideways at its launch speed. So the sideways distance is

Range

\(R = v \times t\)   (launch speed × time in the air)

We do

The ball from the last slide (1.0 m table, 0.45 s in the air) leaves the table at 2.5 m/s. How far from the table does it land?

Answer\(R = 2.5 \times 0.45 \approx 1.1\ \text{m}\)
Worked Example 12.2

The ball in Worked Example 12.1 leaves the table at 3.0 m/s. How far from the table does it land?

  1. 1 Time in the air: \(t = 0.51\ \text{s}\)
  2. ✓ \(R = v \times t = 3.0 \times 0.51 \approx 1.5\ \text{m}\)

Booklet p.139 · §12.5 · Worked Example 12.3

Part B · Theory

I do Measuring g

Measure the drop height and the time of flight, and you can calculate the strength of gravity. A slow-motion video gives the time:

From a video

\(t = \text{number of frames} \div \text{frames per second}\)

\(g = 2h \div t^2\)

We do

A ball rolls off a 1.0 m bench. A video at 240 frames per second shows the fall lasting 108 frames. Find \(t\), then \(g\).

Answer\(t = 108 \div 240 = 0.45\ \text{s}\);  \(g = 2 \times 1.0 \div 0.45^2 = 2.0 \div 0.2025 \approx 9.9\ \text{m/s}^2\)
Worked Example 12.3

A ball rolls off a bench 0.80 m high. A video at 120 frames per second shows 48 frames between leaving the bench and landing. Find \(t\), then \(g\).

  1. 1 Time: \(t = 48 \div 120 = 0.40\ \text{s}\)
  2. 2 \(g = 2h \div t^2 = (2 \times 0.80) \div (0.40 \times 0.40) = 1.6 \div 0.16\)
  3. ✓ \(g = 10\ \text{m/s}^2\) — close to the accepted 9.8 m/s²

Part D · Above level

We do Change the gravity, change the height

These go beyond the Year 7 standard: change the gravity and compare heights.

On Mars, g = 3.7 m/s². A ball rolls off a 1.0 m ledge at 2 m/s. Find its time of flight and how far it travels. Compare with Earth (0.45 s).

\(t = \sqrt{2 \times 1.0 \div 3.7} = \sqrt{0.541} \approx 0.74\ \text{s}\)

\(R = 2 \times 0.74 \approx 1.5\ \text{m}\)

Weaker gravity: it falls more slowly, stays in the air longer, and lands further away.

Ball A rolls off a 0.2 m step; ball B rolls off a 1.8 m wall. B falls 9 times as far. How many times as long does it take?

A: \(\sqrt{0.4 \div 9.8} \approx 0.20\ \text{s}\);  B: \(\sqrt{3.6 \div 9.8} \approx 0.61\ \text{s}\)

\(0.61 \div 0.20 \approx 3\) — and \(\sqrt{9} = 3\)

3 times as long: the time goes with the square root of the height.

Part D · Above level

We do How good is the measurement?

Evaluate the method you will use in Experiment 5.

A group rolls a ball off a 1.2 m bench and measures g = 9.1 m/s². Find the percentage error, and suggest two ways to make the result more accurate.

\(|9.1 - 9.8| \div 9.8 \times 100\% = 0.7 \div 9.8 \times 100\% \approx 7.1\%\)

Count frames from the exact frame the ball leaves the edge to the frame it lands, at a higher frame rate.

About 7%. Also: measure the height carefully, and repeat several drops and average.

A fall from 0.8 m lasts 0.40 s: 12 frames at 30 frames per second, or 96 frames at 240. Miscount by one frame (13, or 97). What value of g does each give?

30 fps: \(t = 13 \div 30 = 0.433\ \text{s}\), so \(g = 1.6 \div 0.188 \approx 8.5\ \text{m/s}^2\)

240 fps: \(t = 97 \div 240 = 0.404\ \text{s}\), so \(g = 1.6 \div 0.163 \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\)

One frame barely matters at a high frame rate — so film at 120 frames per second or more.

Lesson 12 · Recap

What we covered

  • A projectile moves with only gravity acting on it; its path is a parabola
  • Sideways: no force, so a steady speed. Downwards: gravity, so it speeds up
  • The two motions are independent — a dropped ball and a ball launched sideways land together
  • Time of flight \(t = \sqrt{2h \div g}\) depends only on the height; range \(R = v \times t\)
  • Measure g with \(g = 2h \div t^2\) and a slow-motion video — that is Experiment 5
Exit question

A ball rolls off a 0.45 m step at 3 m/s. How long is it in the air, and how far from the step does it land?

Answer\(t = \sqrt{2 \times 0.45 \div 9.8} = \sqrt{0.0918} \approx 0.30\ \text{s}\).  \(R = 3 \times 0.30 \approx 0.9\ \text{m}\).
