What we’re learning today

We are learning to…
  • plan a fair test that measures the strength of gravity, g
  • time a fall by counting the frames of a slow-motion video
  • calculate g for each trial and find a mean
  • judge how accurate a result is with percentage error
I can…

  • use \(g = 2h \div t^2\) to calculate g

Starter

Try to time a coin falling off your desk with a stopwatch. How long does it take? Do you trust your answer?

What we’re listening forAnswers will vary wildly: a desk-height fall takes under half a second, about the same as your reaction time. Today we use slow-motion video to get a time we can trust.

Why is counting video frames like using a very fast stopwatch?

AnswerEach frame is taken a fixed, tiny time after the last one — at 240 fps, every 1/240 of a second. Counting frames counts those tiny, equal steps of time.

The set-up

  • Release the ball from the same mark every time — never push it.
  • Measure the table height \(h\) once, in metres.
  • Film side-on, in slow motion, with the whole fall in the picture.
  • Count frames from the last frame on the table to the first frame on the floor.

Booklet p.147 · §13.2 · Worked Example 13.1

Part B · Planning the experiment

I do From frames to g

Two steps

Time: \(t = \text{frames} \div \text{frame rate}\)

Gravity: \(g = 2h \div t^2\)   (square \(t\) first)

Worked Example 13.1

A table is 0.78 m high. Filmed at 240 fps, the ball takes 96 frames to fall. Find \(t\), then \(g\).

  1. 1 Time: \(t = 96 \div 240 = 0.40\ \text{s}\)
  2. 2 Square it: \(t^2 = 0.40 \times 0.40 = 0.16\)
  3. ✓ \(g = (2 \times 0.78) \div 0.16 = 1.56 \div 0.16 = 9.75\ \text{m/s}^2\)

Booklet p.148 · We do 13.1

Part B · Work through together

We do Find g from the frames

We do 13.1

A table is 0.72 m high. Filmed at 120 fps, the ball takes 46 frames to fall from the edge to the floor.

(a) Find the time of the fall, \(t\), to two decimal places.

(b) Use \(g = 2h \div t^2\) to find \(g\).

Write your working in the We do 13.1 box in your booklet.

  1. 1 (a) \(t = 46 \div 120 \approx 0.38\ \text{s}\)
  2. 2 (b) \(t^2 = 0.38 \times 0.38 = 0.1444\), and \(2h = 2 \times 0.72 = 1.44\)
  3. ✓ \(g = 1.44 \div 0.1444 \approx 10.0\ \text{m/s}^2\)

Booklet p.148 · §13.3 · Worked Example 13.2

Part B · Planning the experiment

I do How close did you get?

Percentage error

\[\text{percentage error} = \frac{\text{your value} - \text{accepted value}}{\text{accepted value}} \times 100\%\]

Ignore any minus sign. For g, the accepted value is \(9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\). Smaller is more accurate.

Worked Example 13.2

A group’s mean value of g is \(9.2\ \text{m/s}^2\). Find the percentage error.

  1. 1 Difference: \(9.8 - 9.2 = 0.6\)
  2. 2 Divide by the accepted value: \(0.6 \div 9.8 \times 100\%\)
  3. ✓ Percentage error \(\approx 6\%\)

Booklet p.148 · §13.4

Part B · Planning the experiment

Making it a fair test

Keep it the same
  • the same ball, ramp and release mark
  • the same table, so the same height \(h\)
  • the same camera position and frame rate

Do five trials. A trial that is clearly different is an anomaly — mark it and leave it out of the mean.

Predict

Will your g be exactly \(9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\), a little more, or a little less? Write your prediction and a reason in your booklet.

Booklet p.147 · Part B · Equipment and method

Part B · Planning the experiment

Method and safety

Method
  1. Measure the table height \(h\) in metres.
  2. Tape the ramp about 20 cm from the edge; mark a release point.
  3. Film side-on in slow motion; record the frame rate.
  4. Release the ball — don’t push. Film the fall.
  5. Count the frames of the fall.
  6. Repeat for five trials.
Safety

Catch the ball, or put a box on the floor to stop it rolling away — a ball bearing underfoot is easy to slip on. Keep the phone and tripod clear of walkways.

Booklet p.152 · Part D · Extension 13.8

Part D · Above level

We do Why film at 240 frames per second?

Extension 13.8

A camera films at 30 fps; a slow-motion camera films at 240 fps.

(a) How much time passes between frames for each camera?

(b) A fall lasts about 0.4 s. Explain why the 30 fps camera would give a much less precise value of g.

  1. 1 (a) 30 fps: \(1 \div 30 \approx 0.033\ \text{s}\). 240 fps: \(1 \div 240 \approx 0.004\ \text{s}\).
  2. 2 (b) At 30 fps the whole fall is only about 12 frames, so being one frame out changes \(t\) by about 8%.
  3. ✓ Because \(t\) is squared in \(g = 2h \div t^2\), that becomes about a 16% error in g — at 240 fps one frame is only about 1%.

Lesson 13 · Recap

What we covered

  • A fall from a table is too quick for a stopwatch — slow-motion video gives the time
  • \(t = \text{frames} \div \text{frame rate}\), then \(g = 2h \div t^2\)
  • Repeat trials, leave out anomalies, and find the mean
  • Percentage error compares your result with the accepted value of \(9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\)
  • This is the same method you will use on the trebuchet
Exit question

A 0.80 m fall takes 100 frames at 240 fps. Find g.

Answer\(t = 100 \div 240 \approx 0.417\ \text{s}\), \(t^2 \approx 0.174\).  \(g = 1.6 \div 0.174 \approx 9.2\ \text{m/s}^2\).
