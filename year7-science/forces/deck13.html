What we’re learning today

We are learning to…
  • plan a fair test that measures the strength of gravity, g
  • use a motion sensor’s velocity–time graph to find an acceleration
  • test whether the mass of a falling object changes its acceleration
  • judge how accurate a result is with percentage error

  • use \(\text{acceleration} = \text{change in speed} \div \text{time}\)

Starter

Drop a heavy book and a flat sheet of paper from the same height at the same moment. Which lands first? Now scrunch the paper into a tight ball and try again.

What we’re listening forThe flat sheet drifts down slowly: air resistance is large for its size. Scrunched up, it lands at almost the same moment as the book. Without air in the way, heavy and light objects fall together. Today we test that with a motion sensor.

Booklet p.141 · §13.2

Part B · Planning the experiment

Reading the graph

While the object falls, its speed goes up steadily, so the line is straight. Read three things off it:

  • the starting velocity — the moment it is let go
  • the final velocity — just before it lands
  • the time of fall between them
From the graph to g

\(\text{change in speed} = \text{final velocity} - \text{starting velocity}\)

\(\text{acceleration} = \text{change in speed} \div \text{time of fall}\)

Check it: the software’s slope of the straight part should be close.

Booklet p.142 · Worked Example 13.1

Part B · Planning the experiment

I do Acceleration from a graph

Worked Example 13.1

The graph for a falling container shows a starting velocity of 0.2 m/s, a final velocity of 4.1 m/s and a time of fall of 0.40 s. The software gives a slope of 9.7 m/s\(^2\). Find the acceleration, and check it.

  1. 1 Change in speed: \(4.1 - 0.2 = 3.9\ \text{m/s}\)
  2. 2 Acceleration: \(3.9 \div 0.40 = 9.75\ \text{m/s}^2\)
  3. ✓ The software’s slope, 9.7 m/s², is very close — the graph was read well.

Booklet p.142 · §13.3 · Worked Example 13.2

Part B · Planning the experiment

I do How close did you get?

Percentage error

\[\text{percentage error} = \frac{\text{your value} - \text{accepted value}}{\text{accepted value}} \times 100\%\]

Ignore any minus sign. For g, the accepted value is \(9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\). Smaller is more accurate.

Worked Example 13.2

A group’s mean value of g is \(9.2\ \text{m/s}^2\). Find the percentage error.

  1. 1 Difference: \(9.8 - 9.2 = 0.6\)
  2. 2 Divide by the accepted value: \(0.6 \div 9.8 \times 100\%\)
  3. ✓ Percentage error \(\approx 6\%\)

Booklet p.143 · §13.4

Part B · Planning the experiment

Making it a fair test

The variables

Change: the mass — three identical containers, one empty, one half full of sand, one full.

Measure: the acceleration of each one.

Keep the same: the size and shape, the sensor settings, the starting point 40 cm below the sensor, letting go without pushing, and the cushion.

Do three trials for each container. A trial that is clearly different is an anomaly — mark it and leave it out of the mean.

Predict

Which container will have the greatest acceleration: the lightest, the middle one or the heaviest? Or will they all be about the same? Write your prediction and a reason in your booklet.

Booklet pp.143–144 · Part B · Equipment and method

Part B · Planning the experiment

Method and safety

Method
  1. Fill the containers (empty, half full, full), tape the lids shut, and record each mass in kg.
  2. Clamp the sensor high on the stand, pointing straight down. Cushion underneath.
  3. Long range, 50 readings per second, graph of velocity against time.
  4. Hold the container 40 cm below the sensor, hand at the side.
  5. Start recording, let go — don’t push. Stop once it lands.
  6. Read the starting velocity, final velocity, time of fall and slope.
  7. Three trials for each container.
Safety

Stand the base well onto the bench so it can’t tip. Tape the lids on firmly — spilt sand makes the floor slippery. Keep feet and heads out from under the falling object, and never stand on a chair or desk to reach the sensor.

Booklet p.148 · Part D · Extension 13.9

Part D · Above level

We do Why 50 readings a second?

Extension 13.9

The motion sensor takes 50 readings every second.

(a) How much time passes between one reading and the next?

(b) A fall lasts about 0.45 s. About how many readings does the sensor take during the fall?

(c) Explain why a sensor that took only 5 readings a second would give a much less precise value of g.

  1. 1 (a) \(1 \div 50 = 0.02\ \text{s}\) between readings.
  2. 2 (b) \(0.45 \div 0.02 \approx 22\) readings during the fall.
  3. ✓ (c) At 5 a second there is a reading only every 0.2 s — about 2 in the whole fall, too few to find where the fall starts and ends, or a reliable slope.

Lesson 13 · Recap

What we covered

  • A fall is too quick for a stopwatch — a motion sensor measures the speed 50 times a second
  • While an object falls, its velocity–time graph is a straight line sloping up
  • \(\text{acceleration} = \text{change in speed} \div \text{time of fall}\), checked against the software’s slope
  • Objects of different mass fall with about the same acceleration, \(g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\)
  • Repeat trials, leave out anomalies, find the mean, and compare with 9.8 m/s² using percentage error
Exit question

A container speeds up from 0.2 m/s to 4.0 m/s in 0.41 s. Find its acceleration, then the percentage error.

AnswerChange in speed \(= 4.0 - 0.2 = 3.8\ \text{m/s}\), so acceleration \(= 3.8 \div 0.41 \approx 9.3\ \text{m/s}^2\).  Percentage error \(= 0.5 \div 9.8 \times 100\% \approx 5\%\).
