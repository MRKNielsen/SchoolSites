Starter

Booklet pp.66–67 · §6.1–6.2

Part B · Planning the experiment

The set-up

The pulley

It just turns the pull from down to sideways. It doesn’t add any force.

Forces on the trolley

Up and down: weight and the normal force are balanced. Sideways: the pull of the string is unbalanced (friction is tiny) — so the trolley accelerates.

Booklet p.67 · §6.3 · Worked Example 6.1

Part B · Planning the experiment

I do Calculating the pulling force

Pulling force = weight of the hanging mass

Use \(W = mg\) with \(g = 9.8\ \text{N/kg}\). The mass must be in kilograms: divide grams by 1000.

Quick check

What is 50 g in kilograms? What pulling force does a 50 g hanging mass give?

Answer\(50 \div 1000 = 0.050\ \text{kg}\),  so \(W = 0.050 \times 9.8 = 0.49\ \text{N}\).
Worked Example 6.1

A 150 g mass hangs on the end of the string. Find the pulling force on the trolley.

  1. 1 Convert to kg: \(150 \div 1000 = 0.150\ \text{kg}\)
  2. ✓ \(W = mg = 0.150 \times 9.8 = 1.47\ \text{N}\)

Booklet p.67 · §6.4 · Method

Part B · Planning the experiment

Making it a fair test

Why move the masses?

We start with three 50 g masses on the trolley. For each new trial, we move one from the trolley to the hanger. The total mass being pulled stays the same — only the force changes.

Predict first

What will happen to the acceleration as the hanging mass goes from 50 g up to 200 g? Use \(F = ma\) to give a reason. Write it in your booklet.

We do — spot the variables. A student tests how the number of rubber bands on a catapult affects how far a ball flies. Name the independent variable, the dependent variable and two controlled variables.

Independent: the number of rubber bands

Dependent: how far the ball flies

Controlled: the same ball, the same launch angle, pulling back the same distance

Booklet p.66 · Part B · Equipment and method

Part B · Planning the experiment

Method

Method
  1. Find the trolley’s mass with the balance.
  2. Clamp the pulley to the bench. Tie the string from the trolley, over the pulley, to the hanger.
  3. Hang 50 g. Put the other three 50 g masses on the trolley.
  4. Hold the trolley at the start line, then let go — no push. Measure the acceleration.
  5. Do 3 trials. Then move one mass to the hanger (100 g) and repeat. Then 150 g and 200 g.
Equipment

Trolley · runway · pulley and string · hanger with 4 × 50 g masses · motion sensor or ticker-tape timer · balance · buffer block and catch box

Safety

Put a buffer block at the end of the runway and catch the trolley before it hits the pulley. Keep your feet clear of the hanging mass, over a catch box. Never overload the hanger.

Directly proportional?

Two things are directly proportional if doubling one doubles the other. When the force doubled (50 g → 100 g), did the acceleration double?

AnswerYes — \(0.40 \times 2 = 0.80\), and they measured 0.81 m/s². The points also lie close to a straight line through (0, 0). That is exactly what \(F = ma\) predicts.

A group measured 0.42, 0.61 and 0.40 m/s² for 50 g. Which value looks wrong? Find the mean of the other two.

0.61 is much bigger than the other two, so it looks wrong.

\((0.42 + 0.40) \div 2 = 0.82 \div 2 = 0.41\)

0.41 m/s²

One group added an extra 50 g to the hanger without taking one off the trolley. Why does that make the test unfair?

Now two things have changed: the pulling force and the total mass.

You can’t tell which change caused the change in acceleration.

Part D · Above level

We do Going further

These go beyond the Year 7 standard: interpret results with a formula.

Another group found: 0.5 N → 0.4 m/s², and 1.0 N → 0.8 m/s². Calculate \(F \div a\) for each. What does this number tell you?

\(0.5 \div 0.4 = 1.25\)  and  \(1.0 \div 0.8 = 1.25\)

Since \(F = ma\), we get \(F \div a = m\).

It is the total mass being accelerated: about 1.25 kg.

The total mass being pulled is 1.5 kg. A 120 g mass hangs on the string. Predict the acceleration.

Pulling force: \(0.120 \times 9.8 = 1.176\ \text{N}\)

\(a = F \div m = 1.176 \div 1.5 \approx 0.78\ \text{m/s}^2\)

about 0.78 m/s² — friction would make the measured value a little smaller

Lesson 6 · Recap

What we covered

  • The weight of the hanging mass is the pulling force: \(W = mg\), with the mass in kg
  • Moving masses from the trolley to the hanger changes the force but keeps the total mass the same
  • Repeat trials, take the mean, and leave out results that clearly don’t fit
  • Double the force → about double the acceleration — just as \(F = ma\) predicts
