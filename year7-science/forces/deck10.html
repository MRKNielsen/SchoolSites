Starter

A small child and an adult want to balance on a seesaw. Where should the adult sit?

What we’re listening forListen for: closer to the pivot than the child. Push for a reason: the heavier person needs a shorter distance, so both sides turn the seesaw equally. Today we measure that trade-off with a metre stick.

How can a small sliding weight balance goods that are much heavier than it?

AnswerDistance. Far from the pivot, a small weight turns the bar as strongly as a heavy weight close to the pivot. A weight ten times further from the pivot can balance goods ten times heavier.

Part B · Planning the experiment

I do Two ways to find the MA

Mechanical advantage, two ways

From the arms:  \(\text{MA} = \text{effort arm} \div \text{load arm}\)

From the forces:  \(\text{MA} = \text{load} \div \text{effort}\)

Weight is proportional to mass, so we can compare the forces using the masses in grams. When the lever balances, both answers should agree.

We do

A 200 g effort balances the 100 g load (load arm 20 cm) when the effort arm is 10 cm. Find the MA both ways.

AnswerFrom the arms: \(10 \div 20 = 0.5\).  From the forces: \(100 \div 200 = 0.5\). They agree — and an MA below 1 means this lever does not multiply force.
Worked Example 10.1

A 100 g load stands 20 cm from the fulcrum. A 50 g effort balances it 40 cm from the fulcrum. Find the MA from the arms and from the forces.

  1. 1 From the arms: \(40 \div 20 = 2\)
  2. 2 From the forces: \(100 \div 50 = 2\)
  3. ✓ They agree. This is the law of the lever: \(100 \times 20 = 50 \times 40 = 2000\)

We do Find the MA two ways

We do 10.1

A 150 g load stands 10 cm from the fulcrum. A 50 g effort balances it 30 cm from the fulcrum.

(a) Find the MA from the arms.

(b) Find the MA from the forces.

(c) Show that the law of the lever works for this lever.

Write your working in the We do 10.1 box in your booklet.

  1. 1 (a) From the arms: effort arm \(\div\) load arm \(= 30 \div 10 = 3\)
  2. 2 (b) From the forces: load \(\div\) effort \(= 150 \div 50 = 3\)
  3. ✓ (c) \(150 \times 10 = 1500\) and \(50 \times 30 = 1500\) — equal on both sides, so the lever balances.

Booklet p.111 · Part B · Equipment and method

Part B · Planning the experiment

Method

Method
  1. Rest the metre stick on the fulcrum at the 50 cm mark and check it balances level.
  2. Stand the 100 g load on the 30 cm mark (load arm 20 cm). Fix it with a little sticky tack.
  3. Stand a 50 g mass on the other side and slide it until the stick balances. Record the effort arm.
  4. Take it off and repeat, so you have 3 trials.
  5. Repeat with 100, 150 and 200 g (stack the 50 g masses).
Getting a good reading

Measure from the fulcrum to the centre of the mass. If the bare stick balances a little away from 50 cm, use that point as the fulcrum and measure every arm from there.

Safety

Keep the masses low on the bench, and hold the stick while you add or move a mass — an unbalanced stick can tip and drop them. Keep feet clear of the bench edge.

I do From three trials to one MA

Example

With a 50 g effort, three trials balanced the stick at effort arms of 39 cm, 41 cm and 40 cm. The load arm is 20 cm. Find the mean effort arm and the MA both ways.

  1. 1 Mean: \((39 + 41 + 40) \div 3 = 120 \div 3 = 40\ \text{cm}\)
  2. 2 MA from the arms: \(40 \div 20 = 2\)
  3. ✓ MA from the forces: \(100 \div 50 = 2\) — the two agree

Part D · Above level

We do Going further

These go beyond the Year 7 standard: test the law of the lever and look for what could spoil the results.

Another group’s results: 50 g → 41 cm, 100 g → 20 cm, 200 g → 10.5 cm. Calculate effort mass × effort arm for each. What do you notice, and what should it equal?

\(50 \times 41 = 2050\),  \(100 \times 20 = 2000\),  \(200 \times 10.5 = 2100\)

They are all close to the same number.

They should all equal load × load arm \(= 100 \times 20 = 2000\) — the law of the lever.

A student moves the fulcrum to the 45 cm mark and leaves the load and effort where they were. Why won’t her results fit the law of the lever?

The stick’s own weight acts at its centre, the 50 cm mark.

With the fulcrum at 50 cm that weight has no turning effect. At 45 cm it sits 5 cm away, on the effort side.

It adds an extra turning effect that helps the effort, so her effort arms come out too short.

Part D · Above level

We do What if the load moves?

Investigate

Keep the effort at 100 g. Move the load to a load arm of 10, 20, 30 and 40 cm, and balance the stick each time. How does the load arm change the effort arm you need?

AnswerThe effort arm goes up in step with the load arm: 10 → 10 cm, 20 → 20 cm, 30 → 30 cm, 40 → 40 cm. A bigger load arm gives the load a bigger turning effect, so the effort must sit further out to match it.

Lesson 10 · Recap

What we covered

  • Balance the load with an effort, and measure both arms from the fulcrum to the centre of each mass
  • The heavier the effort, the shorter the effort arm needed — double the mass, half the arm
  • MA = effort arm ÷ load arm = load ÷ effort; the two should nearly agree
  • Every balance obeys the law of the lever: effort × effort arm = load × load arm
  • Repeat trials, take the mean, and spot results that don’t fit
Exit question

A 100 g load sits 20 cm from the fulcrum. Where must a 250 g effort go to balance it, and what is the MA?

Answer\(100 \times 20 = 2000\), so the effort arm is \(2000 \div 250 = 8\ \text{cm}\). MA \(= 8 \div 20 = 0.4\) (and \(100 \div 250 = 0.4\)).
