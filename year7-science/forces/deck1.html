Part A · Reading and comprehension

The Forces You Cannot See

Before you read

Astronauts on the Space Station float around. Is that because there is no gravity in space? Make a prediction, then read to find out.

Read The Forces You Cannot See in your booklet. Then answer questions C1–C8.

Check after reading

Gravity on the Space Station is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. So why do the astronauts seem to float?

AnswerThey are falling around the Earth, and the whole station is falling with them. Everything falls together, so nothing pushes on them — there is no floor pushing up, so they feel weightless.

Part B · Theory

Every force is a push or a pull

A magnet pulls a paperclip. A hand pushes a door. The Earth pulls you down. There is no third option — every force is a push or a pull.

A force can…
  1. make a still object start moving
  2. make a moving object stop
  3. make it speed up or slow down
  4. change the direction it moves
  5. change its shape
Quick check

A footballer heads the ball back the way it came. Which of the five effects is the force having? Hint: there is more than one.

Answer4 — it changes the ball’s direction. 3 — it changes the ball’s speed (it stops for an instant, then speeds up again). 5 — the ball squashes a little while it touches the head.

Part B · Theory

I do Measuring force — the newton

Force is measured in newtons (N) with a spring scale. Holding up a small apple takes about 1 N; a 1 kg bag of sugar takes about 10 N.

Reading a spring scale

Check the biggest number. Work out what one small division is worth. Read at eye level. Always write the unit.

Worked Example 1.1

This scale goes from 0 to 10 N with 20 small divisions. The pointer is on the 7th division. What is the reading?

  1. 1 One division: \(10 \div 20 = 0.5\ \text{N}\).
  2. ✓ Reading: \(7 \times 0.5 = 3.5\ \text{N}\).

Booklet p.7 · §1.4 · Worked Example 1.2

Part B · Theory

I do Mass and weight are not the same thing

  • Mass is how much matter (stuff) is in an object. It is measured in kilograms (kg) and is the same everywhere.
  • Weight is the force of gravity pulling on that mass. It is measured in newtons (N) and changes if gravity changes.
The link

\(W = mg\)   where \(g = 9.8\ \text{N/kg}\) on Earth and \(g = 1.6\ \text{N/kg}\) on the Moon.

Worked Example 1.2

A student has a mass of 50 kg. Find their weight on Earth and on the Moon.

  1. 1 Earth: \(W = mg = 50 \times 9.8 = 490\ \text{N}\).
  2. 2 Moon: \(W = mg = 50 \times 1.6 = 80\ \text{N}\).
  3. ✓ Their mass is still 50 kg in both places — only the weight changes.

Booklet p.8 · We do 1.2

Part B · Work through together

We do Weight on Earth and on the Moon

We do 1.2

A robot has a mass of 20 kg. Use \(g = 9.8\ \text{N/kg}\) on Earth and \(g = 1.6\ \text{N/kg}\) on the Moon. (a) Find its weight on Earth. (b) Find its weight on the Moon. (c) Has its mass changed?

Write your working in the We do 1.2 box in your booklet.

  1. 1 (a) Earth: \(W = mg = 20 \times 9.8 = 196\ \text{N}\)
  2. 2 (b) Moon: \(W = mg = 20 \times 1.6 = 32\ \text{N}\)
  3. ✓ (c) No — the robot is still 20 kg of matter. Only its weight changes.

Booklet pp.8–9 · §1.5 · Worked Example 1.3

Part B · Theory

I do Contact and non-contact forces

Contact forces only act when two objects touch. Non-contact forces act across a gap.

Worked Example 1.3

A fridge magnet holds a shopping list on the fridge door without sliding down. Name two forces acting on the magnet. Is each one contact or non-contact?

  1. 1 Weight pulls the magnet down. Gravity acts across a gap, so it is non-contact.
  2. 2 Friction between the magnet and the door stops it sliding. The surfaces must touch, so it is contact.
  3. 3 The magnetic pull towards the door (non-contact) presses the magnet on hard enough for friction to hold it.
  4. ✓ Weight is non-contact; friction is contact.

Booklet p.9 · We do 1.3

Part B · Work through together

We do Contact or non-contact?

We do 1.3

A book is pushed across a desk. After you let go, it slides and slows down. Name two forces acting on the book while it slides, and say whether each one is contact or non-contact.

Write your working in the We do 1.3 box in your booklet.

  1. 1 Weight pulls the book down. Gravity acts across a gap, so it is non-contact.
  2. 2 Friction from the desk slows the book. The surfaces touch, so it is contact.
  3. ✓ Weight is non-contact; friction is contact. (The normal force from the desk, pushing up, is also contact.)

Part D · Above level

We do Going further — gravity on other worlds

These go beyond the Year 7 standard. You will need to rearrange \(W = mg\).

A box weighs 294 N on Earth. (a) Find its mass. (b) Find its weight on the Moon.

(a) Rearrange \(W = mg\): \(m = W \div g = 294 \div 9.8 = 30\ \text{kg}\)

(b) \(W = 30 \times 1.6 = 48\ \text{N}\)

30 kg; 48 N on the Moon

On Mars, g = 3.7 N/kg. A student has a mass of 40 kg. Find her weight on Mars and on Earth. Where could she jump higher?

Mars: \(40 \times 3.7 = 148\ \text{N}\)

Earth: \(40 \times 9.8 = 392\ \text{N}\)

Mars — gravity pulls on her with much less force, so the same push from her legs lifts her higher.

Lesson 1 · Recap

What we covered

  • A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons (N)
  • A force can start, stop, speed up, slow down, turn or squash an object
  • To read a spring scale, first work out what one division is worth
  • Mass (kg) is not weight (N): \(W = mg\)
  • Contact forces need touching; non-contact forces act across a gap
Exit question

Your mass is 40 kg. What is your weight on Earth? Would it be different on the Moon?

AnswerEarth: \(40 \times 9.8 = 392\ \text{N}\). Moon: \(40 \times 1.6 = 64\ \text{N}\) — yes, your weight is smaller on the Moon, but your mass is still 40 kg.
