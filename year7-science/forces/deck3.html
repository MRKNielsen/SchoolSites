Booklet p.24 · §3.1 · Worked Example 3.1

Part B · Planning the experiment

I do Making it a fair test

Fair test

Change one thing, measure one thing, keep everything else the same.

Worked Example 3.1

A student rolls a toy car down a ramp at different heights and measures how far it travels across the floor. Identify the variables.

  1. 1 Independent — what the student changes: the height of the ramp.
  2. 2 Dependent — what the student measures: the distance travelled.
  3. ✓ Controlled — kept the same: the same car, the same floor, releasing without a push.

Booklet p.24 · Part B · Prediction, equipment and method

Part B · Planning the experiment

Investigating friction

Predict first

Which surface will produce the most friction? Write your prediction and a reason in the booklet before you start.

Equipment

Wooden block · spring scale · 3+ surfaces (bench, sandpaper, fabric, carpet) · ruler

Method
  1. Place the block on the first surface.
  2. Attach the spring scale and pull slowly and steadily.
  3. Read the scale while the block moves at a steady speed.
  4. Repeat 3 times, then repeat for each surface.
Safety

Pull gently and keep the scale in line with the block — an overstretched scale can snap back. Keep sandpaper away from eyes and skin.

Booklet p.26 · Part C · Results and analysis

Part C · Results and analysis

I do Calculating a mean

Example

Three trials on the bench gave 1.2 N, 1.4 N and 1.3 N. Find the mean.

  1. 1 Add the trials: \(1.2 + 1.4 + 1.3 = 3.9\).
  2. 2 Divide by the number of trials: \(3.9 \div 3\).
  3. ✓ Mean friction on the bench = 1.3 N.

Lesson 3 · Recap

What we covered

  • A fair test changes one variable, measures one, and controls the rest
  • At a steady speed, the spring scale reading equals the friction force
  • Rougher surfaces produce more friction
  • Repeat trials and take the mean to make results more reliable
