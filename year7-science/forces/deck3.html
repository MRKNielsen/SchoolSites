Lesson 3 · Overview

What we’re learning today

We are learning to…
  • observe five everyday forces at five workstations
  • describe which direction each force acts in
  • tell contact forces from non-contact forces
  • write an inference and a generalisation from what I observe
I can…
  • work safely at each workstation and answer its questions
  • say what each force did and which way it acted
  • name which forces acted without touching
  • explain the difference between an observation and an inference
Starter

Think of three forces you have felt or used today. For each one: was it a push or a pull, and did the objects have to touch?

What we’re listening forListen for: gravity pulling them down (no touching), pushing a door or a bag (touching), friction from the floor. Today you test five everyday forces — and decide which ones act without touching.

Experiment 1 · Before the workstations

I do Observation, inference, generalisation

WordWhat it isExample
Observationwhat you see, hear or feel“The magnets pushed my hands apart.”
Inferencean explanation of the observation, using what you know“A force pushed the magnets apart without them touching.”
Generalisationa pattern that is true for every example you tested“Like poles always repel.”
Example

A ball rolled across the floor, slowed down and stopped. Write an inference.

  1. 1 Observe: the ball slowed down and stopped.
  2. 2 What do I know? Friction acts against an object’s motion.
  3. ✓ Inference: friction from the floor pushed against the ball’s motion and slowed it down.

Safety at the workstations

Straws and balls (A, E)
  • Use only your own straw, and never blow towards anyone’s face.
  • Put your straw in the bin as soon as you finish, then wash your hands.
  • Never throw a ball — a golf ball can hurt someone.
  • Pick up a dropped ball straight away, so no one slips on it.
  • Allergic to latex (rubber)? Tell your teacher before you start.
Everything else (B–E)
  • Use the scissors and the plastic rod only for the task.
  • Keep magnets away from laptops and phones.
  • Don’t carry a full tub of water. Wipe up spills straight away.
  • Paper and Styrofoam burn easily: keep them away from flames.

Booklet p.32 · §3.1 · Workstation A

Experiment 1 · Workstation A

Workstation A: Pushing forces

At this workstation

Part 1 — roll the table tennis ball and blow on it through a straw while it is moving:

  • towards you
  • away from you
  • across in front of you.

Part 2 — play blowball: two teams of two, a straw each, and two goals. Who scores the most?

Safety

Your own straw only. Never blow towards anyone’s face. Bin the straw as soon as you finish.

After the workstation

What does a push do to a moving ball? Does it depend on which way you push?

AnswerBlowing pushes the ball. Moving towards you, it slows down (and may roll back). Moving away, it speeds up. Moving across, it changes direction. A push can change an object’s speed and its direction.

Booklet p.33 · §3.2 · Workstation B

Experiment 1 · Workstation B

Workstation B: Gravitational forces

At this workstation
  1. Cut out a piece of paper the size of the coin.
  2. Hold the paper and the coin at the same height. Drop them both at the same time.
  3. Roll the paper into a ball and drop it and the coin again.
Safety

Use the scissors only to cut the paper. Carry them with the points down.

After the workstation

Why did the coin land first? Why did the paper fall faster once it was rolled into a ball?

AnswerGravity pulls both of them down, and air resistance pushes up on both. The flat paper has a big surface for its tiny weight, so air resistance slows it a lot. As a ball it meets less air resistance, so it falls almost as fast as the coin. Gravity didn’t change — the air resistance did.

Booklet p.34 · §3.3 · Workstation C

Experiment 1 · Workstation C

Workstation C: Electrostatic forces

At this workstation
  1. Tear a piece of paper into small bits.
  2. Rub the plastic rod briskly on wool (a woollen jumper works well).
  3. Bring the rod near the pieces of paper.
  4. Rub the rod again and hold it near a weak stream of running water.
Safety

Use the rod only for the task. Wipe up any water you spill.

After the workstation

Does the rod need to touch the paper? What happens to the stream of water?

AnswerThe bits of paper jump up to the rod before it touches them, and the stream of water bends towards the rod. Rubbing gives the rod an electrostatic charge. The electrostatic force acts over a distance: it is a non-contact force.

Booklet p.35 · §3.4 · Workstation D

Experiment 1 · Workstation D

Workstation D: Magnetic forces

At this workstation
  1. Hold one magnet in each hand.
  2. Slowly bring the end of one magnet close to the end of the other. What do you feel? Draw the magnets.
  3. Repeat with the other end of one magnet.
  4. Write a generalisation about the forces between the magnets.
Safety

Keep the magnets away from laptops, phones and other electronic devices.

After the workstation

What generalisation can you write about the two magnets?

AnswerLike poles (N and N, or S and S) push apart: they repel. Unlike poles (N and S) pull together: they attract. The force acts without the magnets touching, and it gets stronger as they get closer.

Booklet p.36 · §3.5 · Workstation E

Experiment 1 · Workstation E

Workstation E: Buoyancy forces

At this workstation
  1. Push a table tennis ball to the bottom of the water and let it go.
  2. Do the same with the other balls: golf, tennis, rubber and Styrofoam.
  3. What forces act on a ball in water, and which way?
  4. Write a generalisation about what happens to different balls.
Safety

Never throw a ball. Don’t carry the tub when it is full. Tell your teacher if you are allergic to latex.

After the workstation

What forces act on a ball in water, and in which direction?

AnswerGravity (its weight) pulls the ball down. The water pushes it up with a buoyancy force. The table tennis ball shoots up because the buoyancy force is much bigger than its weight. A golf ball sinks: its weight is bigger. Generalisation: balls that are light for their size float.

After the workstations

We do Which way do the forces act?

For each object, predict the forces and their directions. Press → to show the arrows — a longer arrow is a bigger force.

Think

The flat paper and the paper ball have the same weight. Why does the paper ball fall faster?

AnswerIts weight is the same, but rolled into a ball it pushes through much less air, so the air resistance on it is smaller.

After the workstations

You do Contact or non-contact?

For each force you met today, decide: did the objects have to touch? Then check.

Remember (Lesson 1)

A contact force acts only when the objects touch. A non-contact force acts over a distance.

Lesson 3 · Recap

What we covered

  • A push can speed an object up, slow it down or change its direction
  • Gravity pulls everything down; air resistance pushes up on a falling object
  • Electrostatic and magnetic forces act over a distance — they are non-contact forces
  • Like magnetic poles repel and unlike poles attract
  • Water pushes up on an object with a buoyancy force
  • An inference explains an observation; a generalisation describes a pattern
Exit question

Name one non-contact force you saw today, and say which way it acted.

AnswerFor example: gravity pulled the coin down; the electrostatic force pulled the paper up to the rod; the magnetic force pushed like poles apart.
