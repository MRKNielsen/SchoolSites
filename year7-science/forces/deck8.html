Check after reading

Why can a ball never bounce back up to the height it was dropped from?

AnswerWhen it hits the ground, some of its energy becomes thermal energy inside the rubber and some becomes sound. Less energy comes out than went in, so it can’t rise as high as it fell.

Booklet p.90 · §8.1

Part B · Planning the experiment

Measuring a bounce

When the ball is held still at the drop height, its energy is gravitational potential energy. At the top of the bounce it is still again, so its energy is all GPE once more.

Because \(\text{GPE} = mgh\), and \(m\) and \(g\) don’t change, comparing the two heights tells us how much energy was kept.

Bounce efficiency

\(\text{Efficiency} = \dfrac{\text{bounce height } h_2}{\text{drop height } h_1} \times 100\%\)

Booklet p.90 · §8.1 · Worked Example 8.1

Part B · Planning the experiment

I do Calculating bounce efficiency

Worked Example 8.1

A ball is dropped from 100 cm and bounces back up to 62 cm. Find the efficiency of the bounce, and the percentage of energy wasted.

  1. 1 Efficiency: \(62 \div 100 \times 100\% = 62\%\)
  2. ✓ Wasted: \(100\% - 62\% = 38\%\) — as thermal energy and sound
We do

A ball dropped from 200 cm bounces to 130 cm. What is the efficiency of the bounce?

Answer\(130 \div 200 \times 100\% = 65\%\) — so 35% of the energy was wasted.

Booklet p.90 · Part B · Equipment and method

Part B · Planning the experiment

Method

Method
  1. Tape two metre rulers upright to the wall, with 0 cm touching the floor.
  2. Hold the ball so its bottom is level with 40 cm.
  3. Let go — don’t push or throw it.
  4. Read the height of the bottom of the ball at the top of its first bounce.
  5. Do 3 trials. Then repeat for 80, 120 and 160 cm.
Getting a good reading

The top of the bounce lasts a split second. Crouch so your eyes are level with where the ball will stop — or film it in slow motion and pause at the highest point. One person drops; one person reads.

Safety

Keep every drop height within reach while standing on the floor — no standing on chairs or desks. Clear bags and chairs out of the way.

Booklet p.92 · Part C · Results and analysis

Part C · Results and analysis

I do From three trials to one efficiency

Example

From a drop height of 40 cm, three bounces reached 23 cm, 21 cm and 22 cm. Find the mean bounce height and the efficiency.

  1. 1 Mean: \((23 + 21 + 22) \div 3 = 66 \div 3 = 22\ \text{cm}\)
  2. ✓ Efficiency: \(22 \div 40 \times 100\% = 55\%\)

Part D · Above level

We do Going further

These go beyond the Year 7 standard: link heights to energy in joules, and predict.

A 0.5 kg ball is dropped from 1.6 m and bounces to 1.2 m. Use g = 9.8 N/kg. Find (a) the GPE before and after (b) the efficiency (c) the energy wasted.

(a) Before: \(0.5 \times 9.8 \times 1.6 = 7.84\ \text{J}\); after: \(0.5 \times 9.8 \times 1.2 = 5.88\ \text{J}\)

(b) \(5.88 \div 7.84 \times 100\% = 75\%\)

(c) \(7.84 - 5.88 = 1.96\ \text{J}\), as thermal energy and sound

Would a ball bounce more efficiently on concrete or on sand? Explain using energy words.

On sand, the sand moves and squashes and doesn’t spring back, so much more of the kinetic energy becomes thermal energy (and sound).

Concrete — more energy is stored as elastic potential energy in the ball and given back.

Lesson 8 · Recap

What we covered

  • At the drop height and at the top of the bounce, all the useful energy is GPE
  • So \(\text{efficiency} = \text{bounce height} \div \text{drop height} \times 100\%\)
  • A bounce wastes energy as thermal energy and sound — it is never destroyed
  • A higher drop gives a higher bounce, but the efficiency stays about the same
  • Measure to the bottom of the ball, repeat trials and take the mean
Exit question

A ball dropped from 50 cm bounces to 30 cm. What is the efficiency of the bounce?

Answer\(30 \div 50 \times 100\% = 60\%\).
