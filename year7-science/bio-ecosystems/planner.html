Year 7 Science · Unit planning document

Unit: Biodiversity & Ecosystems

11 lessons over 7 weeks · Victorian Curriculum F–10 v2.0 · Year 7 core with Year 8 extension

Core activities link to our own unit pages unless marked Stile. Every lesson carries a Caring for Country / First Nations perspectives slide with a matching booklet page — these are not optional extras and are listed under Core activities in the deck itself.

Week1
  • Living & non-living
  • Characteristics of life (MRS GREN)
  • Classification — levels
  • The Linnaean system
  • Lesson 1 — Name and explain the seven life processes; distinguish living, dead and non-living things.
  • I can classify an object as living, dead or non-living and give a reason. I can explain why fire is not alive.
  • Lesson 2 — Order the seven Linnaean levels; explain how features place an organism in a group.
  • I can list Kingdom → Species in order and place an Australian animal into at least three levels.
  • Lesson 1 · Living or Non-living? — MRS GREN, dead vs non-living, Victorian examples
  • Worksheet 1 · 40 marks
  • Lesson 2 · Classification — Levels — opens with the 5-minute spaced retrieval warm-up
  • Worksheet 2 · 34 marks
  • Issue the Species Profile — Eastern Barred Bandicoot and the Research Portfolio. Stages 01–02 due.
  • Student Booklet §1–§2
  • Support: booklet scaffold boxes; Stile Glossary: Classification and biodiversity; glossary flashcards; Handwriting Practice — Biodiversity & Ecosystems.
  • Extend: worksheet questions marked Year 8 extension; the Above The Level prompt in each portfolio stage.
  • Stile: Lesson: Living or non-living?
  • Stile: 2. Classification — a bee or not a bee?
  • Stile: 3.1 Levels of classification
  • Hands-on icebreaker sorting cards; kingdoms sorting worksheet
  • MRS GREN introductory story
  • Formative: Worksheets 1–2 (74 marks); Lesson 1 retrieval quiz inside Lesson 2.
  • Optional diagnostic: pre-unit check on classification; exit pass — living / non-living.
Week2
  • Scientific names
  • Binomial nomenclature and the six kingdoms
  • Dichotomous keys
  • Lesson 3 — Explain what binomial nomenclature is and why it is used; name and describe the six kingdoms.
  • I can write a scientific name correctly and classify an organism into a kingdom with a reason.
  • Lesson 4 — Use table-style and flowchart-style keys; construct a key of my own.
  • I can construct a simple dichotomous key for 4–6 Australian organisms.
  • Lesson 3 · Scientific Names & The Six Kingdoms
  • Worksheet 3 · 37 marks
  • Lesson 4 · Dichotomous Keys — worked key, then students build one
  • Worksheet 4 · 35 marks
  • Portfolio Stages 03–04 (feed CAT Parts A3, A5)
  • Student Booklet §3–§4
  • Support: Stile Using dichotomous keys; two-step keys before four-step; the worked key example stays on screen while students work.
  • Extend: students build a key for a set of organisms they choose; portfolio Stage 04 Above The Level.
  • Stile: Lesson: Scientific names
  • Stile: 3.2 The six kingdoms
  • Stile: 3. Dichotomous keys — how can we tell bees apart?
  • Fantastic Beasts dichotomous key worksheet, or the Aliens key
  • Classifying plants prac + results sheet
  • Formative: Worksheets 3–4; retrieval quiz (Lessons 1–3).
  • Stile Check-in: Classification and dichotomous keys.
  • Optional exit pass — dichotomous keys.
Week3
  • Ecosystems & biodiversity
  • Biotic and abiotic factors
  • Food webs & energy flow
  • Photosynthesis and respiration
  • Lesson 5 — Define ecosystem, biotic and abiotic; explain what biodiversity means and why it matters.
  • I can explain why high biodiversity is generally more stable, and describe two Victorian ecosystems.
  • Lesson 6 — Classify producers, consumers and decomposers; build food webs; predict the effect of removing a species.
  • I can draw a food web of at least six species with the arrows the right way round.
  • Lesson 5 · Australian Ecosystems & Biodiversity
  • Worksheet 5 · 36 marks
  • Lesson 6 · Food Webs & Energy Flow — Victorian woodland species
  • Worksheet 6 · 39 marks
  • Practical: testing a leaf for starch (photosynthesis), with practical sheet and rubric; respiration as the counterpart reaction — Student Booklet §6
  • Portfolio Stages 05–06 (feed CAT Parts B–E — the food-web evidence)
  • Support: the three things students get wrong box in Booklet §6 (respiration is not breathing); a pre-drawn food-web frame to fill in.
  • Extend: energy loss between levels; write the full impact chain when a top predator is removed.
  • Stile: 5. Biodiversity and food — how do bees impact biodiversity?
  • Kahoot! photosynthesis and respiration
  • Schoolyard quadrat / biodiversity survey
  • Cultural burning and food webs — extended Caring for Country discussion
  • Formative: Worksheets 5–6; retrieval quiz (Lessons 1–5); practical write-up against the prac rubric.
  • Stile Check-in: Biodiversity in ecosystems.
  • Stile check-in: photosynthesis and respiration.
Week4
  • Ecosystem services
  • Provisioning, regulating, cultural, supporting
  • Adaptations
  • Structural, behavioural, physiological
  • Lesson 7 — Describe the four categories of ecosystem services and give Victorian examples; explain why they are threatened by biodiversity loss.
  • I can sort examples into the four categories and give a specific Victorian example for at least two.
  • Lesson 8 — Define adaptation and its three types; link adaptations to habitat and niche.
  • I can explain HOW an adaptation helps an organism survive — function, not just description.
  • Lesson 7 · Ecosystem Services — native bee pollination, Caring for Country
  • Worksheet 7 · 37 marks
  • Lesson 8 · Adaptations — Australian examples across ecosystems
  • Worksheet 8 · 37 marks
  • Portfolio Stages 07–08 (Stage 08 feeds CAT Part G)
  • Student Booklet §7–§8
  • Support: sort-the-cards for the four service categories before writing; sentence stem — this adaptation helps the animal … because …
  • Extend: which service category is hardest to put a price on, and why; overlapping niches and competition.
  • Stile: 1. Do we need to save the bees?
  • Stile: 4. Ecosystem services — why are bees important?
  • Adaptation dice game + rules
  • Adaptations Kahoot!
  • ClickView Madagascar: lost worlds with worksheet and solutions
  • Mini animal presentation
  • Formative: Worksheets 7–8; retrieval quiz (Lessons 1–7).
  • Exit pass — classify three adaptations by type and state the function of one.
Week5
  • Introduced species
  • Native, introduced, invasive
  • Human impacts
  • Habitat loss, pollution, climate change
  • Lesson 9 — Distinguish native, introduced and invasive; explain the mechanisms of harm; evaluate management strategies.
  • I can describe THREE ways an introduced species harms native species, and discuss one management strategy and its limitations.
  • Lesson 10 — Explain habitat loss and fragmentation, the edge effect, eutrophication and climate change.
  • I can explain why fragmented habitat is more harmful than an equivalent area of cleared land.
  • Lesson 9 · Introduced Species — red fox and European carp impact chains
  • Worksheet 9 · 37 marks
  • Lesson 10 · Human Impacts on Ecosystems — how threats compound
  • Worksheet 10 · 37 marks
  • Portfolio Stages 09–10 (feed CAT Parts D4, E2–E3), worked from the threats section of the Species Profile
  • Support: the fox and carp impact chains are built one link at a time on screen — students complete the final link only.
  • Extend: which threat multiplies the others, and what that means for where conservation money goes.
  • Stile: 7. Human impacts — are we a threat to bees?
  • Stile: 8. Ecosystem dynamics — how are we impacting biodiversity?
  • Local case study: fragmentation in the Merri Creek corridor
  • Eutrophication demo or ClickView clip
  • Formative: Worksheets 9–10; retrieval quiz (Lessons 1–9).
  • Stile Check-in: Human impacts on ecosystems.
Week6
  • Conservation & action
  • In-situ, ex-situ, legislation
  • Consolidation
  • Unit review and portfolio completion
  • Lesson 11 — Describe in-situ and ex-situ conservation; evaluate strategies using Victorian examples; identify individual, community and government action.
  • I can evaluate a conservation strategy by stating advantages AND limitations, and give a Victorian success story with evidence.
  • Review — Retrieve and apply everything from Lessons 1–11 under test conditions.
  • Lesson 11 · Conservation & Action — habitat corridors, EPBC Act, Victorian success stories
  • Worksheet 11 · 37 marks
  • Portfolio Stage 11 (feeds CAT Part F — the key criterion)
  • 60-mark unit review under test conditions
  • Revision from the Worksheet Booklet (all 11 worksheets, 406 marks) and the Retrieval Quiz (200 questions, filter by topic)
  • Support: reprint the two worksheets the class scored lowest on; run the retrieval quiz filtered to those topics.
  • Extend: Above The Level portfolio responses; argue for how a fixed conservation budget should be split and defend it.
  • Zoos Victoria Fighting Extinction materials, or a Landcare Victoria clip on a local revegetation project
  • Caring for Country as the world's oldest conservation system — extended discussion
  • Class debate: fenced sanctuaries vs landscape-scale corridors
  • Formative: 60-mark unit review, marked against the Solution Set; worksheet booklet answers in red available as a marking copy.
  • Completed Research Portfolio checked — it is unmarked by design, it is the CAT preparation.
Week7
  • Common Assessment Task
  • Ecosystem Investigation
  • Year 7 core + Year 8 extension
  • CAT — Apply the method practised across all eleven portfolio stages to a species I have not studied.
  • I can classify, map a food web, identify threats and evaluate conservation strategies for an unfamiliar species using a supplied species booklet.
  • CAT: Ecosystem Investigation — Parts A–G, issued with a Species Booklet laid out in the same five sections as the Species Profile: Profile, Ecology, Food-web evidence, Threats, Conservation.
  • Reading and planning session, then the writing session.
  • The species is different from the bandicoot on purpose — what transfers is the method, not the animal.
  • Support: the stage → CAT part map printed in the front of the Research Portfolio goes into the reading session with the student; if a CAT question is hard, they go back to the stage beside it.
  • Extend: the Year 8 extension parts, banded separately by the rubric tool.
  • Peer feedback round on Part F before submission
  • Reflection: which portfolio stage helped most, and why
  • Summative: CAT — Ecosystem Investigation, Parts A–G.
  • Banded with the Rubric & Report Generator — it bands every strand, drafts report comments, sorts teaching groups and prints a filled rubric per student.
