Arrow rule

Arrows in a food chain show the direction of energy flow — they point FROM the food source TOWARD the consumer (the organism doing the eating).

Booklet p.21 · Q6.3

Worked example — what if rabbits disappeared?

✍️ Predict the effects of removing one species

Rabbits were removed from a Victorian woodland food web (e.g. by disease or predators). Rabbits eat native shrubs and are eaten by eastern quolls and wedge-tailed eagles. Predict the effects.

1Effect on prey: Native shrubs would experience less grazing pressure — shrub populations would likely increase.
2Effect on predators: Eastern quolls and eagles lose a food source. Quoll and eagle populations may decline unless they switch to other prey (e.g. crimson rosellas, ringtail possums).
3Wider effects: If quolls decline, grasshoppers (a quoll food source) may increase, leading to more grass being eaten.
Conclusion

Removing one species sends ripple effects throughout the web. Complex webs are more resilient — there are alternative food sources. Simple chains collapse entirely.

Now you try

✍️ Predict the effects of losing the wedge-tailed eagle

The wedge-tailed eagle is the apex predator in our woodland food web. If eagle populations collapsed due to habitat loss, predict TWO effects on other species in the web.

1Effect 1: Secondary consumers (brown falcon, eastern quoll, lace monitor) experience less predation from above — their numbers may increase.
2Effect 2: As secondary consumers increase, primary consumers (rabbits, kangaroos, rosellas) face more predation pressure — their numbers may decline.
Conclusion

The eagle acts as a keystone species — controlling populations below it. Its loss causes a cascade of changes (a "trophic cascade") through the entire web.

First Nations perspectives

Booklet p.21

Caring for Country — fire and food webs

Wurundjeri-Woiwurrung people used cultural burning — careful, seasonal, low-intensity burns — to manage the landscape on Country for thousands of years.

These burns were not random. They were timed using ecological knowledge: which animals were nesting, which plants were seeding, which areas needed renewal. The result was a mosaic of different vegetation ages across Country.

🌎 Effect on food webs A mosaic landscape — patches of fresh growth alongside older bush — supports a greater variety of species at different trophic levels. Fresh grass attracts grazing animals; dense scrub provides cover for prey and nesting sites; open areas allow raptors to hunt. Cultural burning actively increased food web complexity and biodiversity.

Year 8 Extension

Booklet p.21 · Year 8 extension

Trophic levels and energy pyramids

Each "feeding level" in a food chain is called a trophic level.

  • Level 1: Producers (plants, algae)
  • Level 2: Primary consumers (herbivores)
  • Level 3: Secondary consumers
  • Level 4+: Tertiary / apex consumers

Energy is lost at each level — as heat, movement, and waste. Only about 10% of energy is transferred from one level to the next.

Work through with the class

Booklet p.21 · Year 8 extension

Worked example — following the energy

✍️ Use the 10% rule to calculate energy at each trophic level

Grasses and shrubs in a Victorian woodland capture 10 000 kJ of energy per square metre each year. Work out how much energy reaches each level above them, then estimate how much plant matter it takes to support one 4 kg wedge-tailed eagle.

1Level 1 → Level 2 (grass → rabbits, kangaroos): 10 000 × 0.10 = 1 000 kJ. The other 9 000 kJ is lost as heat, movement and undigested waste.
2Level 2 → Level 3 (rabbits → quolls, brown falcons): 1 000 × 0.10 = 100 kJ.
3Level 3 → Level 4 (quolls → wedge-tailed eagle): 100 × 0.10 = 10 kJ. Only 0.1% of the original energy reaches the apex predator.
4Now in biomass: the same 10% rule means roughly 10 kg of food → 1 kg of consumer. So a 4 kg eagle needs about 40 kg of quolls, which needed 400 kg of rabbits, which needed 4 000 kg of plants.
5How many rabbits? If one rabbit has a mass of about 2 kg: 400 ÷ 2 = 200 rabbits — and 4 tonnes of vegetation — to support a single eagle.
Conclusion

Energy pyramids are wide at the bottom and narrow at the top because energy runs out. That is why apex predators are always rare, need huge territories, and are the first to disappear when habitat is lost.

Your turn

Booklet p.21 · Year 8 extension

Now you try — grassland energy budget

✍️ A Victorian grassland captures 50 000 kJ per m² each year

(a) How much energy reaches the primary consumers (kangaroos)?  (b) How much reaches the secondary consumers (lace monitors)?  (c) How much energy is lost between Level 1 and Level 2?  (d) Using the 10 kg → 1 kg rule, how much plant matter is needed to grow one 6 kg lace monitor?

1(a) 50 000 × 0.10 = 5 000 kJ reaches the kangaroos.
2(b) 5 000 × 0.10 = 500 kJ reaches the lace monitors.
3(c) 50 000 − 5 000 = 45 000 kJ lost — that is 90%, as heat, movement and waste.
4(d) 6 kg monitor → 60 kg of herbivores → 600 kg of plants.
Challenge

a food chain rarely has more than 4 or 5 levels. Why not? There simply isn't enough energy left — a fifth level would receive under 1 kJ from the original 50 000, not enough to keep an animal alive.

Year 8 Extension

Booklet p.21 · Year 8 extension

Keystone species — disproportionate importance

A keystone species has an outsized effect on its ecosystem relative to its abundance. Remove a keystone species and the ecosystem changes dramatically.

Australian examples:

  • Sea urchin (removed by otters in the Pacific) — when urchins explode in number, they destroy kelp forests
  • Bettongs — small marsupials that dig up fungal spores and aerate soil, supporting plant diversity across Australian woodlands
  • Wedge-tailed eagle — apex predator controlling herbivore populations
Trophic cascade

When a keystone species (usually an apex predator) is removed, the effects cascade down through all trophic levels — herbivores increase, plants decrease, other species change. This is called a trophic cascade.

💬 Extension challenge

In Yellowstone, USA, reintroducing wolves caused rivers to change course — because deer stopped overgrazing riverbanks. How is this an example of a trophic cascade?

Section 6 · Recap

What we covered

  • Producers make food from sunlight; consumers eat other organisms; decomposers break down dead matter
  • Arrows in food chains/webs point in the direction of energy flow (from food to consumer)
  • Food webs are more realistic than food chains — most organisms eat and are eaten by multiple species
  • Removing one species can send ripple effects through the whole web
  • Wurundjeri cultural burning created complex mosaic landscapes that supported richer, more stable food webs
