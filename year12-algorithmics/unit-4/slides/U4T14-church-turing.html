The Church–Turing Thesis

Unit 4 AOS3 · Topic 14 of 17 — what "computable" means, and what the limits do not say

VCE Algorithmics (HESS) · Northcote High School

The thesis

Church–Turing thesis

Any function that is computable by an effective procedure — any algorithm at all, carried out by any means — is computable by a Turing machine.

  • It identifies an informal idea ("there is a step-by-step method") with a precise mathematical one ("a Turing machine exists").
  • Church reached the same conclusion via lambda calculus; Turing via his machines; Gödel via recursive functions. All three formalisms turned out to be exactly equivalent.
  • That convergence, from three unrelated starting points, is the main evidence for the thesis.
Its practical use

Because of the thesis, proving something impossible for Turing machines proves it impossible for any algorithm — in any language, on any hardware, now or in future.

Why "thesis" and not "theorem"

  1. 1 A theorem is proved from definitions. Both sides of the statement must be formally defined.
  2. 2 One side here is informal. "Effective procedure" / "algorithm" is an intuitive notion, not a mathematical object.
  3. 3 So no proof is possible — you cannot prove a statement half of which is not formally defined.
  4. 4 The evidence is empirical: every model of computation ever proposed — lambda calculus, recursive functions, register machines, cellular automata, every programming language — computes exactly the same class of functions.
  5. ✓ Universally accepted; not proved, and not provable in its current form.
How to answer this in the exam

Two components: what it claims, and why the informality of "algorithm" prevents proof. Answers that only restate the claim earn half.

What the limits rule out — and what they don't

ClaimVerdict
No algorithm decides the Halting Problem for all inputstrue — proved
A faster computer could decide itfalse — speed is irrelevant to existence
A quantum computer could decide itfalse — quantum machines compute the same class, sometimes faster
A cleverer programming language could decide itfalse — all Turing-complete languages are equivalent
Turing machines can only run simple algorithmsfalse — they compute everything computable
The distinction to keep straight

Undecidability is about existence, not about resources. No amount of time, memory or hardware creates an algorithm that was proved not to exist.

Three separate questions

  • Computable? Does any algorithm exist? — Turing machines, decidability.
  • Feasible? Does a polynomial-time algorithm exist? — P, NP, tractability.
  • Practical? Does it run fast enough on my data, on my machine? — engineering.
They are answered in that order

A problem must be computable before feasibility means anything, and feasible before practicality is worth discussing.

Exam focus

Explain why the student is incorrect

Adapted from VCAA Algorithmics (HESS) Written Examination 2022, Section B Q13b

Question (4 marks)

Two students are discussing what they have learned.

Student 1: "Turing showed Hilbert's programme was impossible by proving there are some things Turing machines cannot do."

Student 2: "Yes — Turing showed that Turing machines can only run very simple algorithms, such as those that terminate in polynomial time."

Explain why Student 2's statement is incorrect. Use the connection between Hilbert's programme and Turing's work to support your answer.

Two errors are packed in there

One about the power of Turing machines, one confusing two different axes. Find both before reading on.

Working it through

  1. 1 Error one — power. Turing machines are not limited to simple algorithms. By the Church–Turing thesis they compute everything that is computable by any effective procedure; a universal Turing machine can simulate any other machine.
  2. 2 Error two — the wrong axis. "Terminate in polynomial time" is a statement about complexity. Turing's result is about computability — whether an algorithm exists at all. Turing machines happily run exponential-time algorithms.
  3. 3 The Hilbert connection. Hilbert asked for a definite procedure deciding the provability of any mathematical statement. To answer, Turing had to define "definite procedure" — hence the machine.
  4. 4 What Turing actually proved. The Halting Problem is undecidable, so no such procedure exists. Hilbert's programme fails not because machines are weak, but because some problems admit no algorithm at all.
  5. ✓ The limitation is in the problems, not in the machine.

Elaboration — the sentence that fixes the misconception

Say this

"Turing machines are not weak — they compute everything that is computable. What Turing proved is that some well-posed problems have no algorithm at all, and that limitation applies equally to every computer ever built."

  • The misconception is comforting: it suggests a better machine might do better. The thesis closes that door.
  • It also explains why the result has lasted. A theorem about 1936 hardware would be a curiosity; a theorem about all possible algorithms is permanent.
Where the 4 marks sit

Roughly: one for identifying the power error, one for the computability-vs-complexity confusion, one for stating Hilbert's question, one for what Turing proved and its implication.

Check yourself

State the Church–Turing thesis, and explain why it is a thesis rather than a theorem.

Anything computable by an effective procedure is computable by a Turing machine. It cannot be a theorem because "effective procedure" is an informal notion — one side of the claim is not mathematically defined, so no proof is possible. Its support is the equivalence of every computational model ever proposed.

Would a quantum computer be able to solve the Halting Problem?

No. Quantum computers compute the same class of functions as Turing machines; they may offer speed-ups for some problems, which is a complexity matter. Undecidability concerns whether an algorithm exists at all, and no change of hardware creates one.

Distinguish the three questions: computable, feasible, practical.

Computable — does any algorithm exist (decidability)? Feasible — does a polynomial-time algorithm exist (tractability)? Practical — does it run fast enough on this data and hardware (engineering)? Each only makes sense once the previous is answered yes.

How did Turing's work settle Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem?

Hilbert sought a definite procedure deciding provability for any mathematical statement. Turing formalised "definite procedure" as a Turing machine, then proved the Halting Problem undecidable. Since that problem can be encoded as a question about provability, no such procedure exists — answering Hilbert negatively.

One sentence to remember

The Church–Turing thesis makes "no Turing machine can do this" mean "no algorithm can do this" — which is why undecidability is permanent, not a hardware problem.
