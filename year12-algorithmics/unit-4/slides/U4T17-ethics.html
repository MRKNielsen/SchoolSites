Ethics of Data-Driven Algorithms

Unit 4 AOS3 · Topic 17 of 17 — when we delegate the decision

VCE Algorithmics (HESS) · Northcote High School

Why this sits in an algorithms course

Not as a moral coda. Every claim you make here is a claim about a mechanism.

  • Training data, objective functions and model opacity are technical objects — and they are where the harm originates.
  • So ethics questions in this subject are answered with the analysis you already have from Topic 16.
  • The examinable content is four dimensions: transparency, accountability, bias, and machine ethics.
The distinction that organises everything

A designed algorithm can be traced line by line and its author is identifiable. A trained one has behaviour that came from data, lives in thousands of weights, and has no single author. Both facts create ethical problems the other does not.

1 · Transparency

Definition

The extent to which a system's internal workings can be understood and explained to those affected by its decisions.

  • The black box problem: a deep network produces an output with no human-readable reason — opaque even to the people who trained it.
  • Why it matters: an affected person cannot contest what they cannot see; a regulator cannot verify compliance; a developer cannot locate the fault.
  • Responses: interpretable models (decision trees, linear models — understandable by construction), post-hoc explanation tools, explainable-AI techniques.
  • The trade-off: the most interpretable model is often not the most accurate. Choosing between them is itself an ethical decision made by a person.

2 · Accountability

Definition

The assignment of responsibility for a system's decisions and their consequences.

  • The accountability gap: automating a decision does not automate responsibility for it — but it does break the human chain that used to carry it.
  • Candidates: the developer, the data curator, the deploying organisation, the operator who accepted the output.
  • Responses: audit trails and decision logs, human-in-the-loop requirements for high-stakes decisions, statutory frameworks.
Don't conflate these two

Transparency asks can we understand the decision?  Accountability asks who answers for the outcome? A system can be perfectly transparent and still leave nobody accountable.

Common mistake

Algorithmic bias does not require anyone to be prejudiced. It arises from data and objectives, and appears reliably in systems built by people trying to be fair.

Fairness is not one property

Two reasonable definitions of a fair classifier:

  • Calibration: among everyone the model scores as high-risk, the same proportion genuinely is — regardless of group.
  • Equal error rates: the model wrongly flags the same proportion of negatives in each group.
Impossibility result

If the base rate genuinely differs between two groups and the classifier is not perfect, no model can satisfy both at once. This is a theorem, not an engineering shortfall — more data will not fix it.

Why this belongs in this subject

Compare intractability. The response to an NP-hard problem was to choose a trade-off openly and justify it. Same move here: fairness is a choice among incompatible definitions, and the choice must be argued rather than assumed.

4 · Machine ethics

Definition

How to design systems that act in morally acceptable ways — and whether ethical principles can be encoded in an algorithm at all.

The standard questions

  • In an unavoidable crash, does the vehicle protect the passenger or the pedestrian?
  • May an autonomous weapon decide to use lethal force?
  • How should a triage model allocate scarce ICU beds?
FrameworkEncoded as
Utilitarianmaximise total welfare — may sacrifice a minority
Deontologicalhard rules; some actions always forbidden
Virtuebehave as a fair, honest agent would
The finding that matters

MIT's Moral Machine gathered millions of responses to crash dilemmas worldwide and found preferences vary systematically by culture — which makes "just program in the right values" an empty instruction.

Three case studies

Case 1 · Robodebt (Australia, 2015–2019)

  • The mechanism: annual income reported to the ATO was averaged across 26 fortnights and compared with what a recipient had declared. Any gap was raised as a debt.
  • Why the maths fails: averaging assumes income arrives evenly. Casual and seasonal workers — precisely the welfare population — earn unevenly, so the average manufactures a discrepancy where none exists.
  • The reversal: the scheme shifted the onus of proof onto the recipient, who had to disprove a debt the department had never established.
  • Scale: around $746 million wrongly recovered from roughly 381,000 people; a Royal Commission found the method unlawful.
The lesson

No neural network. No opacity — the rule was arithmetic and fully traceable. The failure was an invalid modelling assumption deployed at scale with no route of appeal. Transparency alone guarantees nothing.

Case 2 · COMPAS recidivism scoring (US)

  • The system: a proprietary risk score informing bail and sentencing decisions.
  • The 2016 finding: among defendants who did not reoffend, Black defendants were wrongly flagged high-risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants.
  • The vendor's reply: the score was calibrated — a given score meant the same reoffending likelihood for either group. Both parties were reading true numbers.
  • The resolution: the impossibility result from earlier. Different base rates made the two fairness criteria mathematically incompatible.
  • Compounding factor: the model was a trade secret, so defendants could not inspect the basis of a decision about their liberty.
The lesson

"Is this algorithm fair?" is under-specified. Ask fair by which criterion, at whose expense, and who chose.

Case 3 · Facial analysis — Gender Shades (2018)

  • An audit of three commercial gender-classification products.
  • Error rate for lighter-skinned men: as low as 0.8%. For darker-skinned women: up to 34.7%.
  • Mechanism: representation bias — benchmark datasets were overwhelmingly light-skinned, so aggregate accuracy looked excellent while a subgroup failed badly.
  • Intersectionality: reporting by skin tone alone, or gender alone, hides the worst cell. The disparity appears only when you cross them.
The lesson

An aggregate accuracy figure is a choice about who counts. Always ask what the disaggregated numbers look like.

Exam focus

Describe one technical and one ethical risk

Adapted from VCAA Algorithmics (HESS) Written Examination 2023, Section B Q8b

Question (2 marks)

A community proposes that at least 1000 local residents contribute data on soil type, temperature and crop yield, which will be used to train a neural network. They hope the model will predict and explain successful crop production, and intend to sell it to other communities for use in their areas.

Describe one technical risk and one ethical risk with this approach.

The detail that carries both answers

Read the last clause again — sell it to other communities for use in their areas. Both risks follow from it.

Working it through

  1. 1 Technical risk — the data is local. The model is trained entirely on one region's soil, climate and practices. Applied elsewhere it is operating outside the distribution it learned from, so its predictions may be badly wrong while still appearing confident.
  2. 2 Also technical: a neural network is a black box, so the hope that it will explain successful production is unlikely to be met — it can predict, but not supply reasons.
  3. 3 Ethical risk — consent and benefit. Residents contribute data for their community's benefit; it is then commercialised. Did they consent to that use, and do they share in the proceeds?
  4. ✓ Also ethical: buyers may make costly planting decisions on advice that is unreliable for their region, with no accountability for the resulting loss.
Keep the two categories distinct

Technical = will it work as intended? Ethical = who could be harmed, and is that acceptable? Giving two technical risks scores one mark.

The four-move paragraph

Ethics answers lose marks for being opinion. This frame keeps them analytical.

  1. 1 Stakeholder — name who is affected. Not "society"; the specific group bearing the cost.
  2. 2 Harm — state what they lose, concretely.
  3. 3 Mechanism — the technical cause: which data, which objective, which opacity. This is what separates Algorithmics from a general essay.
  4. 4 Mitigation — a specific change, plus what it costs. "Remove the bias" is not a mitigation.
Naming the cost

Stating what your own mitigation sacrifices is what makes it an analysis rather than a wish.

Worked exemplar

Question

A bank deploys a neural network to screen loan applications. Discuss one ethical issue this raises. (4 marks)

Response

[Stakeholder] Applicants from postcodes historically under-served by the bank are the group at risk. [Harm] They face systematically higher rejection rates and cannot obtain a reason for the decision, so they cannot correct or contest it. [Mechanism] The model is trained on the bank's own past approvals, so historical bias is reproduced; postcode acts as a measurement proxy for ethnicity even though ethnicity is never an input; and the network's weights give no traceable justification for an individual refusal. [Mitigation] Test approval rates disaggregated by postcode and demographic group before deployment, and require an interpretable model for refusals so a reason can be issued — accepting a likely reduction in overall predictive accuracy as the cost.

Check yourself

Distinguish transparency from accountability, with an example of one without the other.

Transparency = the workings can be understood and explained to those affected; accountability = a party is answerable for the outcome. Robodebt was fully transparent — the averaging rule was simple arithmetic — yet nobody was answerable and recipients had no effective appeal.

A model has 94% overall accuracy. Why is that insufficient to judge it fair?

Aggregate accuracy is dominated by the largest group in the test set, so a minority subgroup can fail badly while the headline number stays high — as in Gender Shades, where an acceptable average concealed a 34.7% error rate for darker-skinned women. Fairness requires disaggregated performance.

Two audits of one classifier reach opposite conclusions and neither has erred. How?

They apply different fairness criteria. If group base rates differ and the classifier is imperfect, calibration and equal false-positive rates cannot both hold — an impossibility result. One audit finds it calibrated and fair; the other finds unequal error rates and unfair. Both are correct; the criterion must be chosen and justified.

Why might a developer deliberately choose a less accurate model?

To gain interpretability. A decision tree or linear model can supply a human-readable reason for each decision, letting affected people contest it and regulators verify compliance. In a high-stakes setting the ability to explain and appeal can outweigh a small accuracy loss.
