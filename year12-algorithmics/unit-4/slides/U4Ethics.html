Why this sits in an algorithms course

Not as a moral coda. Every claim you'll make here is a claim about a mechanism — training data, an objective function, a model's opacity. Ethics questions in this subject are answered with the analysis you already have.

The four dimensions

1 · Transparency

Definition

The extent to which the internal workings of a system can be understood and explained to those affected by its decisions.

  • The black box problem: a deep network produces an output with no human-readable reason — opaque even to the people who trained it.
  • Why it matters: an affected person cannot challenge what they cannot see; a regulator cannot check compliance; a developer cannot find the fault.
  • Responses: interpretable models (decision trees, linear models — understandable by construction), post-hoc explanation tools, and explainable AI techniques generally.
  • The trade-off: the most interpretable model is often not the most accurate one. That choice is itself an ethical decision, made by a person.

2 · Accountability

Definition

The assignment of responsibility for a system's decisions and their consequences.

  • The accountability gap: automating a decision does not automate responsibility for it — but it does break the human chain that used to carry it.
  • Candidates: the developer, the data curator, the organisation that deployed it, the operator who accepted the output.
  • Responses: audit trails and decision logs, human-in-the-loop requirements for high-stakes decisions, statutory frameworks (e.g. the EU AI Act).
Don't conflate these two

Transparency asks can we understand the decision?  Accountability asks who answers for the outcome? A system can be perfectly transparent and still have nobody accountable.

Common mistake

Algorithmic bias does not require anyone to be prejudiced. It arises from data and objectives, and appears reliably in systems built by people trying to be fair.

Fairness is not one property

Two reasonable definitions of a fair classifier:

  • Calibration: among everyone the model scores as high-risk, the same proportion actually reoffends — regardless of group.
  • Equal error rates: the model wrongly flags the same proportion of non-reoffenders in each group.
Impossibility result

If the base rate \(p\) genuinely differs between two groups, and the classifier is not perfect, no model can satisfy both conditions at once. This is a theorem, not an engineering shortfall — you cannot buy your way out with more data or a bigger network.

Why this belongs in this subject

Compare intractability. The response to an NP-hard problem was to choose a trade-off openly and justify it. Same move here: fairness is a choice among incompatible definitions, and the choice must be argued, not assumed.

4 · Machine ethics

Definition

How to design systems that act in morally acceptable ways — and whether ethical principles can be encoded into an algorithm at all.

The standard questions

  • An unavoidable crash: does the vehicle protect the passenger or the pedestrian?
  • May an autonomous weapon decide to use lethal force?
  • How should a triage model allocate scarce ICU beds?
FrameworkEncoded as
Utilitarianmaximise total welfare — may sacrifice a minority
Deontologicalhard rules; some actions always forbidden
Virtuebehave as a fair, honest agent would
The finding that matters

MIT's Moral Machine collected millions of responses to crash dilemmas worldwide and found the preferences vary systematically by culture. There may be no single machine ethics acceptable everywhere — which makes "just program in the right values" an empty instruction.

Three case studies

Case 1 · Robodebt (Australia, 2015–2019)

  • The mechanism: annual income reported to the ATO was averaged across 26 fortnights and compared to what a recipient had declared. Any gap was raised as a debt.
  • Why the maths fails: averaging assumes income arrives evenly. Casual and seasonal workers — precisely the welfare population — earn unevenly, so the average manufactures a discrepancy where none exists.
  • The reversal: the scheme shifted the onus of proof onto the recipient, who had to disprove a debt the department had never established.
  • Scale: around $746 million wrongly recovered from roughly 381,000 people; a Royal Commission found the averaging method unlawful. The human cost included deaths by suicide.
The lesson

No neural network. No opacity — the rule was arithmetic and fully traceable. The failure was an invalid modelling assumption deployed at scale with no route of appeal. Transparency alone guarantees nothing.

Case 2 · COMPAS recidivism scoring (US)

  • The system: a proprietary risk score used to inform bail and sentencing decisions.
  • ProPublica's 2016 finding: among defendants who did not go on to reoffend, Black defendants were wrongly flagged high-risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants.
  • The vendor's reply: the score was calibrated — a given score meant the same reoffending likelihood for either group. Both parties were reading true numbers.
  • The resolution: the previous slide's theorem. Different base rates made the two fairness criteria mathematically incompatible; the dispute was never really empirical.
  • Compounding factor: the model was a trade secret, so defendants could not inspect the basis of a decision about their liberty.
The lesson

"Is this algorithm fair?" is under-specified. Ask fair by which criterion, at whose expense, and who chose.

Case 3 · Facial analysis — Gender Shades (2018)

  • Buolamwini and Gebru audited three commercial gender-classification products.
  • Error rate for lighter-skinned men: as low as 0.8%. For darker-skinned women: up to 34.7%.
  • Mechanism: representation bias — benchmark datasets were overwhelmingly light-skinned, so aggregate accuracy looked excellent while a subgroup failed badly.
  • Intersectionality: reporting accuracy by skin tone alone, or by gender alone, hides the worst cell. The disparity only appears when you cross them.
The lesson

An aggregate accuracy figure is a political choice about who counts. Always ask what the disaggregated numbers look like.

Writing about ethics

The four-move paragraph

Ethics answers lose marks for being opinion. This frame keeps them analytical — four moves, one paragraph.

  1. 1 Stakeholder — name who is affected. Not "society"; the specific group bearing the cost.
  2. 2 Harm — state what they lose, concretely.
  3. 3 Mechanism — the technical cause: which data, which objective, which opacity. This is the mark that separates Algorithmics from a general essay.
  4. 4 Mitigation — a specific change, plus what it costs. "Remove the bias" is not a mitigation.

Worked exemplar

Question

A bank deploys a neural network to screen loan applications. Discuss one ethical issue this raises. (4 marks)

Response

[Stakeholder] Applicants from postcodes historically under-served by the bank are the group at risk. [Harm] They face systematically higher rejection rates and cannot obtain a reason for the decision, so they cannot correct or contest it. [Mechanism] The model is trained on the bank's own past approvals, so historical bias is reproduced; postcode functions as a measurement proxy for ethnicity even though ethnicity is never an input, and the network's weights give no traceable justification for an individual refusal. [Mitigation] Test approval rates disaggregated by postcode and demographic group before deployment, and require an interpretable model for refusals so a reason can be issued — accepting a likely reduction in overall predictive accuracy as the cost.

Note the last clause

Naming the cost of your own mitigation is what makes it an analysis rather than a wish.

Practice

Distinguish transparency from accountability, with an example where a system has one and not the other. (3 marks)

Transparency = the workings can be understood and explained to those affected; accountability = a party is answerable for the outcome. Robodebt was fully transparent — the averaging rule was simple arithmetic — yet no one was answerable, and recipients had no effective appeal. So transparency does not entail accountability.

A model has 94% overall accuracy. Explain why this figure alone is insufficient to judge it fair. (3 marks)

Aggregate accuracy is dominated by the largest group in the test set, so a minority subgroup can fail badly while the headline number stays high — as in Gender Shades, where an acceptable average concealed a 34.7% error rate for darker-skinned women. Fairness requires disaggregated performance across the affected groups.

Two audits of one classifier reach opposite conclusions about its fairness, and neither has made an error. Explain how. (3 marks)

They are applying different fairness criteria. If group base rates differ and the classifier is imperfect, calibration and equal false-positive rates cannot both hold — an impossibility result, not a bug. One audit finds it calibrated and fair; the other finds unequal error rates and unfair. Both are correct; the criterion must be chosen and justified. (This was the COMPAS dispute.)

Why might a developer choose a less accurate model deliberately? (2 marks)

To gain interpretability. A decision tree or linear model can supply a human-readable reason for each decision, enabling affected people to contest it and regulators to verify compliance. In a high-stakes setting the ability to explain and appeal can outweigh a small accuracy loss.

Exit ticket

Four questions to carry

  1. Can we understand the decision?
  2. Who answers for it?
  3. Fair to whom, and by which criterion?
  4. How should it decide when the ethical frameworks disagree?
