The reason to use one

Some tasks resist specification. Nobody can write the rules that distinguish a cat from a dog in pixel values — but we can supply thousands of labelled examples and let the parameters be fitted.

Three kinds of learning

Supervised

Training examples come with correct labels. The model learns a mapping from input to label. Classifying photographs, predicting crop success, marking email as spam.

Unsupervised

The data has no labels. The model finds structure by itself — grouping similar items together. Clustering customers by buying habits, finding topics in documents.

Reinforcement

No labels, but a reward signal. The model acts, receives feedback, and adjusts to earn more reward over time. Game playing, robot control.

Which one this course focuses on

Everything in this topic — SVMs, neural network classifiers — is supervised. The labelled examples are what make training possible, and their quality is what the ethics topic will hold to account.

Features — how data becomes numbers

Feature

A single measurable property of an example, expressed as a number. A set of features turns each example into a point in space.

  • A crop sample described by rainfall alone is a point on a line — one dimension.
  • Add soil pH and it becomes a point on a plane — two dimensions.
  • A 100×100 greyscale photograph is a point in 10 000 dimensions, one per pixel.
Why this framing matters

Once examples are points, classification becomes geometry: draw a boundary through the space so the classes fall on opposite sides. Everything that follows is a different way of choosing that boundary.

Learning from data

How a data-driven algorithm learns

  1. 1 Collect labelled examples — inputs paired with known correct outputs.
  2. 2 Choose a model with adjustable parameters. In a network these are the weights and biases; they start at random values.
  3. 3 Measure the error — run the model over the training data and compare its outputs with the correct labels.
  4. 4 Adjust the parameters slightly, in the direction that reduces the error.
  5. 5 Repeat steps 3–4 until the error stops improving. Each full pass over the data is an epoch.
  6. ✓ Nothing about the structure changes. Only the numbers do.

What "adjust the parameters" means

  • The error function takes the current parameters and returns a single number: how wrong the model is over the training data.
  • Changing a weight changes that number. So the error is a surface over the space of possible parameter settings.
  • Training walks downhill on that surface, one small step at a time. The step size is the learning rate.
Learning rate matters

Too large and the steps overshoot the minimum and bounce. Too small and training takes forever. Exactly the exploration/exploitation trade-off from Topic 11.

Local optima are a real problem in training too, and the responses rhyme: random restarts become random initialisation, and accepting the occasional worse step becomes momentum and noisy gradient steps.

Useful in an exam

Asked why training might not find the best possible model, "the search can settle in a local optimum of the error surface" is a complete and correct answer.

Support vector machines

The widest street

  • Binary classification by a line in 2-D, a plane in 3-D, a hyperplane in general.
  • Many boundaries separate the training data. The SVM's objective is to choose the one that maximises the margin — the distance to the nearest point of each class.
  • A wide margin means new points near the training data still land on the correct side, so the model generalises.
Say it as an objective

"Maximise the distance from the boundary to the nearest training point of either class." That sentence is the answer to "state the SVM's optimisation objective".

Find the widest street yourself

What to do

Rotate the boundary and watch how much room there is between the classes at each angle. Then widen the street until it touches a point. The angle whose street is widest is the SVM's answer — the ringed points are its support vectors.

Only the support vectors matter

  1. 1 The support vectors are the training points sitting exactly on the margin — the nearest ones to the boundary.
  2. 2 Move any other point, however far, and the boundary does not change — as long as it stays on its own side of the margin.
  3. 3 Move a support vector and the boundary shifts immediately.
  4. 4 So the model is defined by a handful of points, not the whole dataset. A trained SVM can discard the rest.
  5. ✓ That is where the name comes from: these are the vectors that support the boundary.
Check yourself

Is this a strength or a weakness? (Both. It makes the model compact and robust to distant outliers — but a single mislabelled point placed near the boundary can move it badly.)

The same idea in one dimension

Strip it down to a single feature — relative rainfall — and the geometry becomes obvious.

What generalises

In 1-D the "hyperplane" is a single point, the margin is a gap, and the support vectors are the nearest failure (−2.0) and the nearest success (+1.5). The SVM puts the boundary at the midpoint, −0.25, because that maximises the gap on both sides.

Not separable? Add a dimension

  1. 1 The problem. Points inside a circle versus outside cannot be split by any straight line in 2-D.
  2. 2 The trick. Invent a new feature: \(z = x^2 + y^2\), the squared distance from the origin.
  3. 3 In \((x,y,z)\)-space the inside points sit low and the outside points high — a flat plane separates them perfectly.
  4. 4 Generalise. Map the data into a higher-dimensional space where a linear boundary works, classify linearly there, and the boundary appears curved when projected back.
  5. ✓ The classifier is still linear. The space changed, not the method.
Try it

XOR data — \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\) in class A; \((0,1)\) and \((1,0)\) in class B — is not linearly separable. Find a third feature that separates it. (Hint: consider \(x \times y\).)

Neural networks

The multilayer perceptron

  • Neuron: takes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and passes the result through an activation function.
  • Layers: input (the features) → one or more hidden layers → output (the classification).
  • Every connection carries a weight. These, plus the biases, are the parameters training adjusts.
  • Hidden layers construct intermediate features automatically — the learned version of the "add a dimension" trick.

Activation functions

  • Step — outputs 0 or 1 depending on whether the sum exceeds a threshold. Simple, and what exam questions use.
  • Sigmoid — squashes any input into \((0,1)\), smoothly. Useful when you want a confidence rather than a hard decision.
  • ReLU — outputs the sum if positive, otherwise 0. The workhorse of modern deep networks.
Why any activation at all?

Without one, every neuron is a weighted sum — and a weighted sum of weighted sums is still just a weighted sum. A network of any depth would collapse to a single linear boundary. The non-linearity is what lets layers build on each other.

Forward propagation — worked

Setup

Inputs \(x_1 = 1\), \(x_2 = 0\). Hidden neuron \(h\): weights \(0.5\) and \(-0.3\), bias \(0.1\), step activation (fires if the sum is \(> 0\)).

  1. 1 Weighted sum at \(h\): \(0.5(1) + (-0.3)(0) + 0.1 = 0.6\).
  2. 2 Activation: \(0.6 > 0\), so \(h\) outputs 1.
  3. 3 Output neuron: weight from \(h\) is \(0.8\), bias \(-0.5\), so \(0.8(1) - 0.5 = 0.3\).
  4. ✓ \(0.3 > 0\), so the network classifies 1.
Show the sums

Weighted sum, add bias, apply activation, layer by layer, left to right. The marks are for the arithmetic shown, not the final label.

Worked example — with a sigmoid

Setup

A neuron with weights \(0.5\) and \(1.5\), bias \(0.5\), and a sigmoid activation \(\sigma(z) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\). Inputs \(x_1 = 2\), \(x_2 = -1\).

  1. 1 Weighted sum: \(0.5(2) + 1.5(-1) + 0.5 = 1 - 1.5 + 0.5 = 0\).
  2. 2 Apply the sigmoid: \(\sigma(0) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{0}} = \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.5\).
  3. 3 Interpret it. The output is a number in \((0,1)\), read as a confidence. \(0.5\) sits exactly on the fence — the neuron is maximally unsure.
  4. ✓ With a step activation the same sum would give a flat 0 or 1 and that uncertainty would be invisible.
You will not be asked to evaluate \(e^{-z}\)

Know the shape: large positive \(z\) → near 1, large negative \(z\) → near 0, \(z = 0\) → exactly 0.5. That is enough to reason about any sigmoid question.

What hidden layers buy you — XOR

XOR cannot be separated by any single straight line. Two hidden neurons solve it.

The network (step activation)

\(h_1\) = OR: weights \(1, 1\), bias \(-0.5\)
\(h_2\) = AND: weights \(1, 1\), bias \(-1.5\)
output: weight \(+1\) from \(h_1\), \(-1\) from \(h_2\), bias \(-0.5\)

Read it in words

"At least one input is on, and not both." Each hidden neuron draws one line; the output layer combines them into a region no single line could describe.

Backpropagation, conceptually

Forward propagation computes an answer. Backpropagation is how the network learns from getting it wrong.

  1. 1 Forward: run an example through the network and get an output.
  2. 2 Compare: measure the error against the known correct label.
  3. 3 Assign blame backwards: work out how much each weight in the final layer contributed to the error, then each weight in the layer before, and so on back to the input.
  4. 4 Nudge every weight a little in the direction that would have reduced the error.
  5. ✓ Repeat over many examples and many epochs. The name is literal: the error propagates backwards through the network.
Depth of treatment

You are not expected to compute a backpropagation update by hand. You are expected to describe what it does — and that it is what makes the weights meaningful rather than random.

Evaluating a model

Split the data before you train

  • Training set — the model fits its weights on this, and only this.
  • Validation set — used to choose between models and settings (how many layers, what learning rate) without touching the test set.
  • Test set — held back untouched until the very end. Used once, to estimate performance on genuinely unseen data.
The cardinal rule

Never evaluate on data the model was trained on. Any accuracy figure produced that way measures memorisation, not learning — and it will be far higher than real-world performance.

Fitting a curve — three attempts at the same data

The same 26 noisy points, fitted with polynomials of degree 1, 2 and 14. Errors are root-mean-square, measured on the training points and on a large sample of fresh data.

Read the two numbers, not the picture

The underfit is bad on both — it never captured the pattern. The overfit has the lowest training error of the three and the worst new-data error. Low training error is not evidence of a good model.

The same three failures, as decision boundaries

  • Under-fitting — a straight line cannot follow a curved separation, so it misclassifies whole regions no matter how much data you add.
  • Appropriate — follows the real shape, and tolerates the two stray crosses as noise rather than chasing them.
  • Over-fitting — contorts to capture those two strays. Perfect on the training set, and those detours are pure invention.
The judgement being made

Every fit decides which variation is signal and which is noise. Overfitting is the mistake of treating noise as signal — and you cannot tell them apart from the training data alone.

Read the gap

Both axes matter: error up the side, model complexity along the bottom. The two curves separating is the signature of overfitting — training error keeps falling while new-data error turns back upward, and it is the second curve you care about.

Learning curves — diagnosing which problem you have

  • Plot both errors against the amount of training data. Training error rises as data is added (harder to fit more points); test error falls.
  • High bias: the curves meet, but above the target. The model is too simple — gathering more data changes nothing. Add capacity or better features.
  • High variance: a wide gap persists. The model is memorising. More data, or a simpler model, will close it.
The diagnostic value

The shape tells you what to do next. Converged-but-poor and wide-gap look identical if you only quote a single accuracy figure — and they call for opposite fixes.

Why accuracy alone misleads

A cautionary sum

A disease affects 1 in 1000 people. A model that simply answers "no" to every patient achieves 99.9% accuracy — and detects nothing whatsoever.

  • When one class is far more common than the other, accuracy is dominated by the common class and says almost nothing about the rare one.
  • Ask instead: of the cases the model flagged, how many were real? Of the real cases, how many did it find?
  • And always ask for accuracy broken down by group, not just the headline figure.

Exam focus

Find the weight

Adapted from VCAA Algorithmics (HESS) Written Examination 2023, Section A Q15

Setup

A single output node \(a\) receives three inputs \(i_0, i_1, i_2\). The connection from \(i_0\) has unknown weight \(X\); from \(i_1\) the weight is \(1\); from \(i_2\) it is \(-2\). The inputs are set to \(i_0 = 0.5\), \(i_1 = 0.5\), \(i_2 = 1\).

What value of \(X\) makes the activation of \(a_0\) equal to 0? — set up the weighted sum

A\(-2\)
B\(0\)
C\(1\)
D\(3\)

Working it through

  1. 1 Write the weighted sum — each input times its weight, added together. \[ 0.5 \times X \;+\; 0.5 \times 1 \;+\; 1 \times (-2) \]
  2. 2 Simplify: \(0.5X + 0.5 - 2 = 0.5X - 1.5\).
  3. 3 Set the activation to zero: \(0.5X - 1.5 = 0\).
  4. 4 Solve: \(0.5X = 1.5\), so \(X = 3\).
  5. ✓ Answer: D.
Where errors creep in

Forgetting that \(i_0\) is \(0.5\), not \(1\) — which gives \(X = 1.5\). That is not among the options, so the mistake is invisible until you notice nothing matches. If your answer isn't on the list, recheck the multiplications first.

Explain how an SVM could classify this data

Adapted from VCAA Algorithmics (HESS) Written Examination 2023, Section B Q8a

Question (2 marks)

A community records whether each tomato crop succeeded or failed, alongside the relative rainfall for that crop, measured on a scale from −10 to +10. The successes cluster at higher rainfall, the failures at lower.

Explain how a support vector machine could be used as a classifier for this data.

Response

Each crop is a point on a single axis — relative rainfall — labelled success or failure. Because there is only one feature, the separating hyperplane is a single threshold value on that axis.

The SVM chooses the threshold that maximises the margin: it finds the highest-rainfall failure and the lowest-rainfall success — the support vectors — and places the boundary midway between them, giving the largest possible gap on either side. A new crop is then classified by whether its rainfall falls above or below that threshold.

Two marks, two ideas

One for identifying the boundary as a threshold on the single feature; one for saying it is positioned to maximise the margin between the two nearest opposing points.

What is wrong with this training approach?

Adapted from VCAA Algorithmics (HESS) Written Examination 2021, Section B Q10

Question (4 marks)

A student builds a neural network to classify photographs as showing him or not showing him.

a. Describe a motivation for using a neural network rather than a traditional algorithm. (2 marks)

b. He trains it on a set of 100 photographs containing both. He then tests accuracy by renaming those same photographs "Photo 1", "Photo 2" and so on, and running the model on the renamed files. Describe two problems with this approach. (2 marks)

Working it through

a — motivation

There is no practical way to write explicit rules that identify a face from pixel values — the variation in lighting, angle and expression is far too great to specify by hand. A neural network instead learns the distinguishing features from labelled examples, so the developer supplies data rather than rules.

  1. 1 Problem one — testing on the training set. Renaming the files changes nothing about their content; the model is evaluated on the very photographs it learned from. It may simply have memorised them, so the accuracy figure says nothing about performance on new photographs. A separate held-out test set is required, split off before training.
  2. 2 Problem two — the dataset is far too small and probably unrepresentative. 100 photographs cannot cover the range of lighting, angles, backgrounds and ages the model will meet, so it will overfit to incidental features of those particular images.
  3. ✓ Both point the same way: the reported accuracy will be far higher than real-world performance.

Elaboration — why the renaming is a red herring

The renaming detail is placed deliberately. It invites you to think something has been anonymised and therefore made fair.

  • The network never sees filenames. It sees pixels — identical before and after renaming.
  • What matters for a valid test is not whether the tester knows which photo is which, but whether the model has seen that image during training.
  • The correct procedure: split the data before training into training, validation and test sets; train only on the first; evaluate only on the last.
The general principle

Never evaluate on data the model was trained on. Any accuracy figure produced that way measures memorisation, not learning.

Check yourself

State the SVM's optimisation objective and why it aids generalisation.

Among all hyperplanes separating the training data, choose the one maximising the margin — the distance to the nearest point of each class. A wide margin means a new point near the training data still falls on the correct side, so small variations do not flip the classification.

Why must a neural network have non-linear activation functions?

Without them each neuron computes a weighted sum, and a weighted sum of weighted sums is still a weighted sum — so a network of any depth would collapse to a single linear boundary. The non-linearity is what allows hidden layers to build genuinely new features.

Compute: inputs 1 and 2, weights 0.4 and −0.1, bias 0.3, step activation.

Weighted sum \(= 0.4(1) + (-0.1)(2) + 0.3 = 0.4 - 0.2 + 0.3 = 0.5\). Since \(0.5 > 0\), the neuron fires, outputting 1. Always show the sum — that is where the marks are.

A model scores 99% on training data and 62% on new data. Diagnose and fix.

Overfitting — it has memorised the training set including its noise. Fixes: gather more and more varied training data, reduce model capacity, or use a validation set to stop training before memorisation sets in.

Distinguish the training, validation and test sets.

Training — the model fits its weights on this. Validation — used to choose between models and settings without touching the test data. Test — held back untouched and used once at the end, to estimate performance on genuinely unseen data.

A classifier reports 99.9% accuracy on a disease affecting 1 in 1000. Is it good?

Not necessarily. Always answering "no" achieves exactly that figure while detecting nothing. When one class dominates, accuracy is driven by the common class. Ask how many of the flagged cases were real, how many real cases were found, and how the figures look broken down by group.
