Formula board

Combinations

\(E(aX + bY) = aE(X) + bE(Y)\)

\(\text{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2\text{Var}(X) + b^2\text{Var}(Y)\)

\(\text{sd} = \sqrt{\text{Var}}\) — convert at the last step

Sample mean

\(E(\bar{X}) = \mu\), \(\text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)

Normal population → exact; otherwise CLT for large \(n\)

Continuous \(X\)

\(\int f = 1\); \(E(X) = \int x f(x)\,dx\); \(E(X^2) = \int x^2 f(x)\,dx\)

\(\Pr(Y \le y) = \Pr\!\left(X \le \tfrac{y-b}{a}\right)\) for \(Y = aX+b,\ a > 0\)

Inference

CI: \(\bar{x} \pm z\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)   (90/95/99%: \(z = 1.6449,\, 1.9600,\, 2.5758\))

Margin of error \(M = z\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\); width \(= 2M\); \(\dfrac{n_2}{n_1} = \left(\dfrac{W_1}{W_2}\right)^2\)

Test: \(z = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\)

two-tail: \(p = 2P(Z \geq |z|) = \Pr\big(|\bar{X}-\mu| \ge |\bar{x}-\mu|\big)\)

Binomial → normal

\(\text{Bi}(n,p) \mathrel{\dot\sim} N\big(np,\, np(1-p)\big)\), large \(n\)

SAC-style question 1: combinations

Question

A gift box holds 4 chocolates and 2 truffles. Chocolate masses are \(N(20,\, 1.5^2)\) g; truffle masses are \(N(35,\, 2^2)\) g; the empty box is exactly 50 g. Find the distribution of the total mass, and \(P(\text{total} > 205)\) g.

  • \(T = C_1 + \cdots + C_4 + R_1 + R_2 + 50\) — six independent copies, not \(4C + 2R\).
  • \(E(T) = 4(20) + 2(35) + 50 = 200\);   \(\text{Var}(T) = 4(1.5^2) + 2(2^2) = 17\) (the 50 adds no variance).
  • \(T \sim N(200,\, 17)\); \(P(T > 205) = P\!\left(Z > \tfrac{5}{\sqrt{17}}\right) = P(Z > 1.213) \approx 0.113\).
Traps dodged

Six independent copies — not \(4C + 2R\); constants shift means only.

SAC-style question 2: sample mean

Question

A café's espresso shots have mean 30 mL and sd 3 mL (any shape of distribution). For a day of \(n = 81\) shots, estimate the probability the day's average shot exceeds 30.5 mL.

  • Population not stated normal — but \(n = 81\) is large, so by the CLT \(\bar{X} \mathrel{\dot\sim} N\!\left(30,\, \left(\tfrac{3}{9}\right)^2\right) = N(30,\, \tfrac{1}{9})\).
  • \(P(\bar{X} > 30.5) \approx P\!\left(Z > \dfrac{0.5}{1/3}\right) = P(Z > 1.5) \approx 0.0668\).
  • Cite the CLT explicitly — naming the theorem is part of the answer.

SAC-style question 3: full hypothesis test

Question

Bags of coffee should average 250 g with \(\sigma = 4\) g. A random sample of 20 bags (population normal) gives \(\bar{x} = 247.8\) g. Test at the 5% level whether the mean has changed, and state the corresponding 95% CI.

  • \(H_0: \mu = 250\), \(H_1: \mu \neq 250\) ("changed" → two-tail).
  • \(z = \dfrac{247.8 - 250}{4/\sqrt{20}} = \dfrac{-2.2}{0.894} \approx -2.46\); \(p = 2P(Z \geq 2.46) \approx 0.0139\).
  • \(p < 0.05\) → reject \(H_0\): evidence the filling mean has changed.
  • CI: \(247.8 \pm 1.96(0.894) = (246.0,\, 249.6)\) — and indeed 250 lies outside it. Consistent.
Lesson 3 · Revision

Mark-losers and rapid fire

  • The four classic traps, named and disarmed.
  • Rapid-fire drill: ten quick answers under time.

The four classic mark-losers

  • Variance vs sd — combining standard deviations directly. Variances add; standard deviations do not. Writing \(\text{sd}(X+Y) = \text{sd}(X) + \text{sd}(Y)\) gives \(1.5 + 2 = 3.5\) where the truth is \(\sqrt{1.5^2+2^2} = 2.5\), and nothing in the answer looks wrong. It is tempting because every other rule this term is additive, and because the question usually hands you standard deviations. The fix is procedural: square them the moment you write them down, do the whole calculation in variances, and take one square root at the very end.
  • \(2X\) vs \(X_1 + X_2\) — "twice one item" against "two random items". Variances \(4\sigma^2\) and \(2\sigma^2\); the means are identical, so nothing in the mean will ever warn you. It is tempting because the arithmetic \(2 \times\) is right there in the wording. The fix: ask how many times something random actually happened. Two boxes chosen means two independent values; one box, doubled, means one.
  • Tail choice — decided by the wording, before you see the data. "Changed" or "differs" is two-tail; "reduced", "increased", "overfills" is one. It is tempting to settle this after computing \(\bar{x}\), because by then the direction is obvious — but that is precisely what makes it illegitimate, and it halves the \(p\)-value. The fix: write \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) as line one, before touching the CAS.
  • Conclusion language. "Do not reject \(H_0\)", never "accept \(H_0\)" — failing to find evidence is not the same as establishing the null, exactly as "not guilty" is not "innocent". Then a final sentence naming the actual quantity and its units. It is tempting to stop at the \(p\)-value, since that is where the mathematics ends. The fix: treat the sentence as part of the answer, not a flourish — it is separately marked, and it is the step most often lost when time runs short.
What these four have in common

None of them is a hard calculation, and none of them produces an answer that looks wrong. Each is a decision made in the first ten seconds of a question — which quantity to combine, how many random draws there were, which tail, what the question actually asked — that then propagates silently through correct arithmetic to a wrong result. That is why the remedy in every case is a habit at the start rather than a check at the end.

Self-test

Can you write, from memory, the distribution of \(\bar{X}\) for a sample of \(n\) from \(N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) — and say what changes if the population isn't normal?

Rapid fire — 6 in 6 minutes

Try it! (1 min each)
  • 1. \(X\): mean 8, sd 3. Mean and sd of \(5 - 2X\)?
  • 2. \(\text{Var}(X - Y)\) for independent \(X, Y\) with variances 5 and 7?
  • 3. sd of \(\bar{X}\), sample of 25 from sd-10 population?
  • 4. 95% CI: \(n = 100\), \(\bar{x} = 52\), \(\sigma = 5\)?
  • 5. "Has the mean increased?" — which tail?
  • 6. \(P(\text{Type I error})\) at significance level 0.05?
  • 1. mean \(5 - 16 = -11\); sd \(|-2| \times 3 = 6\).   2. \(5 + 7 = 12\).   3. \(10/5 = 2\).
  • 4. \(52 \pm 0.98 = (51.02,\, 52.98)\).   5. upper one-tail (\(H_1: \mu > \mu_0\)).   6. \(0.05 = \alpha\), by definition.

Time strategy

Skim the whole paper first; bank the pipeline-stage-1 marks early; leave the interpretation sentences time at the end — they are quick marks if you've left space for them.

Final checklist

  • Can I state the distribution of any linear combination of independent normals, unprompted? Given any \(aX + bY + c\), can you write down its mean, its variance and the fact that it is normal without being asked for each separately? That single sentence is the gateway to most of Chapter 15, and it is worth being able to produce it cold rather than reconstructing it each time.
  • Do I check the wording for \(2X\) vs \(X_1 + X_2\), and for the tail structure, before computing? Both are decisions, not calculations, and both are invisible in the final answer once made wrongly. If your habit is to read the question once and start writing, these are the two things to slow down for.
  • Does every test I write have all five lines? Hypotheses, distribution under \(H_0\), test statistic, \(p\)-value, conclusion in context. They are marked separately, so a correct \(p\)-value with no stated hypotheses still loses the setup marks — and a correct calculation with no closing sentence loses the interpretation mark, which is the cheapest one on the page.
  • Can I tell which justification of normality a question needs? Population stated normal → exact, cite that. Population unknown or given by a density, with \(n\) large → approximate, cite the central limit theorem. Naming the wrong one is a marked error even when every number that follows is right.
  • Is my bound reference organised so I can find the formula board in five seconds? Under time pressure the difference between knowing where something is and knowing roughly where something is costs several minutes across a paper. Tab it before the day, not on it.
