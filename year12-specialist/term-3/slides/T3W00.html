Part 1

The language of probability

  • Sample space, event, complement — the vocabulary the later chapters use without explaining.
  • Addition and multiplication rules.
  • Independence — the single most important word in Chapter 15.

The words, precisely

WordNotationMeaning
Sample space\(\varepsilon\)every outcome that could happen. Probabilities across it total 1.
Event\(A\)a set of outcomes you care about, e.g. "the die shows an even number".
Complement\(A'\)everything except \(A\). \(P(A') = 1 - P(A)\).
Union\(A \cup B\)\(A\) or \(B\) (or both).
Intersection\(A \cap B\)\(A\) and \(B\) together.
Mutually exclusive\(A \cap B = \varnothing\)they cannot both happen. \(P(A \cap B) = 0\).
Reading tip

In a worded problem, "or" almost always means \(\cup\) and "and" means \(\cap\). Translate the sentence before reaching for a formula.

The three rules you'll actually use

\[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\]

\[P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \qquad\qquad P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)\]

  • The addition rule subtracts the overlap so it isn't counted twice. If \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive the overlap is 0 and the rule simplifies.
  • \(P(A \mid B)\) reads "the probability of \(A\) given \(B\) has happened" — you have shrunk the sample space down to \(B\).
Quick example

\(P(A) = 0.5\), \(P(B) = 0.4\), \(P(A \cap B) = 0.2\).

\(P(A \cup B) = 0.5 + 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.7\) and \(P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{0.2}{0.4} = 0.5\).

Independence — the word that carries Chapter 15

Definition

\(A\) and \(B\) are independent when knowing one happened tells you nothing about the other:

\(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\), equivalently \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\).

In the example above \(P(A \mid B) = 0.5 = P(A)\), so those two events are independent.

Why it matters later

Every variance rule in Chapter 15 — \(\text{Var}(X+Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)\) and everything built on it — is only valid for independent variables. The expectation rules need no such condition. When a question says "randomly selected", "chosen at random and independently", or "a random sample", it is granting you that permission.

Common confusion

Mutually exclusive is not the same as independent — in fact it's close to the opposite. If \(A\) and \(B\) can't both happen, then knowing \(A\) happened tells you \(B\) definitely didn't. That's maximal dependence.

Part 2

Discrete random variables, \(E(X)\) and \(\text{Var}(X)\)

  • What a random variable is, and what its distribution table shows.
  • \(E(X)\) — the long-run average.
  • \(\text{Var}(X)\) and \(\text{sd}(X)\) — the spread, and the formula you'll use every time.

What a random variable is

Definition

A random variable \(X\) attaches a number to each outcome of a random process. Discrete means the values are separate and countable — 0, 1, 2, … A probability distribution lists each value with its probability.

Toss two coins and let \(X\) be the number of heads:

\(x\)012
\(P(X = x)\)0.250.50.25
  • Every probability sits in \([0,1]\), and they must total exactly 1 — that's the check on any table.
  • Capital \(X\) is the variable; lower-case \(x\) is a particular value it might take. Ch 15–16 relies on that distinction constantly.

Expected value: the balance point

\[E(X) = \mu = \sum x\,P(X = x)\]

  • Multiply each value by its probability and add. It is a weighted average, not an ordinary one.
  • \(E(X)\) need not be a value \(X\) can take — the mean of one die roll is 3.5.
  • Read it as "the average over very many repetitions", not "what will happen".
Quick example

Two coins, \(X\) = number of heads:

\(E(X) = 0(0.25) + 1(0.5) + 2(0.25) = 1\) head, as symmetry suggests.

Carry this forward

\(E\) is linear: it passes straight through sums and multiples. That single property is the whole of 15A–B on the expectation side.

Variance and standard deviation: the spread

\[\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 = E(X^2) - \big[E(X)\big]^2 \qquad\qquad \text{sd}(X) = \sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}\]

  • \(E(X^2)\) means square each value and weight by its probability — \(\sum x^2 P(X=x)\). It is not \(\big[E(X)\big]^2\), and the gap between the two is the variance.
  • Variance is in squared units; the standard deviation brings you back to the units of the problem, which is why answers are usually quoted with \(\sigma\).
  • Variance is never negative. A negative answer means \(E(X^2)\) and \(\big[E(X)\big]^2\) were swapped.
Quick example

Same two coins: \(E(X^2) = 0(0.25) + 1(0.5) + 4(0.25) = 1.5\).

\(\text{Var}(X) = 1.5 - 1^2 = 0.5\), so \(\sigma = \sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.707\) heads.

Part 2 formula card

Keep these

\[\sum P(X=x) = 1 \qquad E(X) = \sum x\,P(X=x) \qquad \text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - \big[E(X)\big]^2\]

The habit worth building

When a distribution table appears, compute \(E(X)\) and \(\text{Var}(X)\) immediately, before reading the rest of the question. Nearly every Ch 15 question needs both, and having them written down turns a multi-part problem into substitution.

Part 3

The normal distribution and \(z\)-scores

  • What \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) says, and the 68–95–99.7 rule.
  • Standardising: \(z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\).
  • Probabilities as areas — and finding them on CAS, forwards and backwards.

Shape and notation

Notation

\(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) reads "\(X\) is normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\)".

  • Symmetric, single peak at \(\mu\); \(\mu\) is simultaneously the mean, median and mode.
  • \(\mu\) slides the curve left and right; \(\sigma\) makes it narrow and tall or wide and flat.
  • Total area under the curve is 1, because it accounts for every possible outcome.
  • It is continuous, so \(P(X = 5) = 0\) — only intervals have probability. This also means \(P(X < 5)\) and \(P(X \le 5)\) are equal, unlike in the discrete case.
Watch the second slot

\(N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) carries the variance, but CAS asks for the standard deviation. \(N(100, 25)\) means \(\sigma = 5\), not 25.

Standardising: turning any normal into one normal

\[z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \qquad\qquad Z \sim N(0, 1)\]

  • A \(z\)-score answers one question: how many standard deviations from the mean is this value? Negative means below the mean.
  • Standardising strips out the units, so scores from different distributions become comparable.
  • The transformation is itself a linear function of \(X\) — which is exactly why Chapter 15 opens with linear functions.
Quick example

Heights are \(N(170, 8^2)\) cm. A person 182 cm tall has \(z = \dfrac{182 - 170}{8} = 1.5\) — one and a half standard deviations above average.

The three you should know cold

\(z = 1.645\) leaves 5% in one tail · \(z = 1.96\) leaves 2.5% in each tail (the 95% interval) · \(z = 2.576\) leaves 0.5% in each tail (99%). Chapter 16 uses these constantly.

Probabilities are areas — and CAS finds them both ways

Forwards: value → probability

normCdf(lower, upper, \(\mu\), \(\sigma\))

For "less than 60" use a lower bound of \(-10^{99}\); for "more than 60" an upper bound of \(10^{99}\).

Backwards: probability → value

invNorm(area, \(\mu\), \(\sigma\)) returns the \(x\) with that much area to its left.

For a top-10% cut-off, ask for area 0.9.

Sanity checks
  • A probability must land in \([0,1]\). If not, a bound is wrong.
  • Above the mean, the answer is under 0.5; below it, over 0.5.
  • Sketch the curve and shade before computing — it catches almost every tail error.
Symmetry facts

\(P(Z > a) = P(Z < -a)\) and \(P(Z > a) = 1 - P(Z < a)\).

Part 4

The binomial distribution

  • The four conditions that make a situation binomial.
  • \(\Pr(X = x)\), and the mean and variance you'll quote from memory.
  • Why 15F approximates it with a normal.

When is a situation binomial?

All four must hold
  • Fixed number of trials, \(n\), decided before you start.
  • Each trial has exactly two outcomes — success or failure.
  • The probability of success, \(p\), is the same every trial.
  • Trials are independent.

Write it as \(X \sim \text{Bi}(n, p)\), where \(X\) counts the successes.

Is it binomial?
  • Yes — 20 free throws at a fixed 70% success rate; \(X\) = number made.
  • No — drawing 5 cards without replacement: \(p\) changes each draw, so the trials aren't independent.
  • No — throwing until the first six: \(n\) isn't fixed.

The formulas

\[\Pr(X = x) = \binom{n}{x} p^{x}(1-p)^{n-x}, \qquad x = 0, 1, \ldots, n\]

\[E(X) = np \qquad\qquad \text{Var}(X) = np(1-p) \qquad\qquad \text{sd}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}\]

  • \(\binom{n}{x}\) counts the orders in which \(x\) successes can occur; the rest is the probability of any one such order.
  • On CAS: binomPdf(\(n\), \(p\), \(x\)) for exactly \(x\); binomCdf(\(n\), \(p\), lower, upper) for a range.
  • \(E(X) = np\) is worth internalising — 20 throws at 70% averages 14 made.
The usual slip

"At least 3" is \(\Pr(X \ge 3) = 1 - \Pr(X \le 2)\) — note the 2. Writing \(1 - \Pr(X \le 3)\) is the most common lost mark in the topic.

A binomial with large \(n\) has so many possible values that its bar chart starts to trace out a normal curve. Section 15F makes that precise:

Looking ahead — 15F

For large \(n\), \(X \sim \text{Bi}(n,p)\) is approximately \(N\big(np,\; np(1-p)\big)\).

Both parameters come straight from the two formulas on the previous slide — which is the whole reason they're worth memorising now.

Also worth knowing

A binomial counts successes, so a sample proportion \(\hat{p} = X/n\) is a binomial divided by a constant — a linear function of a random variable. That is exactly the machinery of 15A, applied to real data.
