Part 1

The language of probability

  • Sample space, event, complement — the vocabulary the later chapters use without explaining.
  • Addition and multiplication rules.
  • Independence — the single most important word in Chapter 15.

The words, precisely

WordNotationMeaning
Sample space\(\varepsilon\)every outcome that could happen. Probabilities across it total 1.
Event\(A\)a set of outcomes you care about, e.g. "the die shows an even number".
Complement\(A'\)everything except \(A\). \(P(A') = 1 - P(A)\).
Union\(A \cup B\)\(A\) or \(B\) (or both).
Intersection\(A \cap B\)\(A\) and \(B\) together.
Mutually exclusive\(A \cap B = \varnothing\)they cannot both happen. \(P(A \cap B) = 0\).
Reading tip

In a worded problem, "or" almost always means \(\cup\) and "and" means \(\cap\). Translate the sentence before reaching for a formula.

The three rules you'll actually use

\[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\]

\[P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \qquad\qquad P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)\]

  • The addition rule subtracts the overlap so it isn't counted twice. Add \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\) and any outcome lying in both events has been included in each total — so it appears twice in the sum. Subtracting \(P(A \cap B)\) removes exactly that duplicate, no more and no less. When \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive there is no overlap to remove, \(P(A \cap B) = 0\), and the rule collapses to \(P(A) + P(B)\). That is why "or" questions feel easier when the events cannot happen together: the correction term vanishes.
  • \(P(A \mid B)\) shrinks the sample space down to \(B\). Read it as "the probability of \(A\) given that \(B\) has happened". Once you know \(B\) occurred, every outcome outside \(B\) is off the table, so the denominator is no longer 1 but \(P(B)\), and the numerator counts only the part of \(A\) that survives inside \(B\) — the intersection. The formula is nothing more than that: a restricted numerator over a restricted denominator.
  • The multiplication rule is the same statement rearranged. Multiply both sides of the conditional formula by \(P(B)\) and you get \(P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)\). This is exactly what you are doing when you multiply along the branches of a tree diagram — each step after the first is conditional on the steps that came before it, so the probabilities you multiply are conditional ones.
Quick example

\(P(A) = 0.5\), \(P(B) = 0.4\), \(P(A \cap B) = 0.2\).

\(P(A \cup B) = 0.5 + 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.7\) and \(P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{0.2}{0.4} = 0.5\).

Independence — the word that carries Chapter 15

Definition

\(A\) and \(B\) are independent when knowing one happened tells you nothing about the other:

\(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\), equivalently \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\).

In the example above \(P(A \mid B) = 0.5 = P(A)\), so those two events are independent.

Why it matters later

Every variance rule in Chapter 15 — \(\text{Var}(X+Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)\) and everything built on it — is only valid for independent variables. The expectation rules need no such condition.

The reason is worth understanding rather than memorising. If two quantities tend to be large together, their high values coincide and their low values coincide, so the total swings further than either one does alone and the true variance of the sum exceeds \(\text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)\). If they tend to move in opposite directions, each one partly cancels the other and the total is steadier than the sum of variances predicts. Independence is precisely the case where neither happens, so the variances simply add. Means never have this problem: an average is unaffected by whether the quantities move together, which is why \(E(X+Y) = E(X) + E(Y)\) always holds.

When a question says "randomly selected", "chosen at random and independently", or "a random sample", it is granting you that permission — and you should say so in your working, because it is the condition that makes the next line legal.

Common confusion

Mutually exclusive is not the same as independent — in fact it's close to the opposite. If \(A\) and \(B\) can't both happen, then knowing \(A\) happened tells you \(B\) definitely didn't. That's maximal dependence.

Part 2

Random variables, \(E(X)\) and \(\text{Var}(X)\)

  • What a random variable is, and what its distribution table shows.
  • \(E(X)\) — the long-run average.
  • \(\text{Var}(X)\) and \(\text{sd}(X)\) — the spread, and the formula you'll use every time.
  • The continuous case: probability density functions, and the same two numbers by integration.

What a random variable is

Definition

A random variable \(X\) attaches a number to each outcome of a random process. Discrete means the values are separate and countable — 0, 1, 2, … A probability distribution lists each value with its probability.

Toss two coins and let \(X\) be the number of heads:

\(x\)012
\(P(X = x)\)0.250.50.25
  • Every probability sits in \([0,1]\), and they must total exactly 1. This is the check to run on any table before you use it, and it is also how "find \(k\)" questions are solved: if a table contains an unknown, the condition that the probabilities sum to 1 is the equation that determines it. The total is 1 because the listed values are every value \(X\) can take, so one of them is certain to occur.
  • Capital \(X\) is the variable; lower-case \(x\) is a particular value it might take. \(X\) is the whole random mechanism — the thing that has a distribution, a mean and a variance. \(x\) is one number that came out of it. Chapters 15–16 lean on this distinction constantly: \(\bar{X}\) is a random variable with its own distribution, while \(\bar{x}\) is the single number your sample produced. Confusing the two is what makes the phrase "the distribution of the sample mean" sound like nonsense, and getting it straight is most of what makes Chapter 16 click.

Expected value: the balance point

\[E(X) = \mu = \sum x\,P(X = x)\]

  • Multiply each value by its probability and add — it is a weighted average, not an ordinary one. An ordinary average would treat every listed value as equally likely. The weighting by \(P(X = x)\) is what makes common values pull the answer towards themselves and rare values barely move it. If the values happen to be equally likely the weights are all equal and the formula does reduce to the ordinary average, which is the special case people are used to.
  • \(E(X)\) need not be a value \(X\) can take. The mean of one die roll is 3.5, and no die shows 3.5. This is not a defect: \(E(X)\) is the balance point of the distribution, the place a ruler carrying those weights would balance, and a balance point need not sit on top of one of the weights.
  • Read it as "the average over very many repetitions", not "what will happen". Expected value is a long-run statement. It says nothing about the next single outcome, which is why an expected value of 3.5 is entirely compatible with never once observing 3.5. Whenever an exam question asks you to "interpret" an expected value in context, this long-run phrasing is what earns the mark.
Quick example

Two coins, \(X\) = number of heads:

\(E(X) = 0(0.25) + 1(0.5) + 2(0.25) = 1\) head, as symmetry suggests.

Carry this forward

\(E\) is linear: it passes straight through sums and multiples, so \(E(aX + b) = aE(X) + b\) and \(E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y)\). That single property is the whole of 15A–B on the expectation side — every expectation result next week is this one fact applied to a longer expression.

It is worth noticing how cheaply this comes. \(E(X)\) is a sum, and sums can be split apart and have constants pulled out of them; that is all linearity is. Variance is not a plain sum — it involves squaring — which is exactly why the variance rules next week look so different and carry conditions that the expectation rules do not.

Variance and standard deviation: the spread

\[\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 = E(X^2) - \big[E(X)\big]^2 \qquad\qquad \text{sd}(X) = \sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}\]

  • \(E(X^2)\) means square each value, then weight by its probability — \(\sum x^2 P(X=x)\). Square first, average second. \(\big[E(X)\big]^2\) does the opposite: average first, square second. These are different numbers, and the gap between them is the variance. Order of operations is the entire content of this formula, and reversing it is the single most common way to lose the mark.
  • Variance is in squared units; the standard deviation brings you back to the units of the problem. If \(X\) is a mass in grams then \(\text{Var}(X)\) is in grams-squared, which is not a quantity anyone can picture. Taking the square root returns you to grams, which is why final answers are almost always quoted as \(\sigma\). The working, though, is nearly always done in variances — they are the things that combine cleanly — and you convert once at the end. That pattern recurs all term.
  • Variance is never negative, because it measures a spread and \(E(X^2) \ge \big[E(X)\big]^2\) always. So a negative answer is not a hard question producing a strange result; it is a reliable signal that the two terms were subtracted the wrong way round. Check it every time — it costs a second and catches the error before it propagates through the rest of a multi-part question.
Quick example

Same two coins: \(E(X^2) = 0(0.25) + 1(0.5) + 4(0.25) = 1.5\).

\(\text{Var}(X) = 1.5 - 1^2 = 0.5\), so \(\sigma = \sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.707\) heads.

Continuous random variables: the density function

Not every random variable takes separate, countable values. A weight, a time, a length can take any value in an interval. Such an \(X\) is described not by a table but by a probability density function \(f\).

Definition

\(f\) is a probability density function for \(X\) when

\(f(x) \ge 0\) for all \(x\),  and  \(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1\).

Probability is area under the curve: \(\displaystyle \Pr(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\).

  • These are the discrete rules with \(\sum\) replaced by \(\int\) — nothing else changes. "Probabilities are non-negative" becomes "\(f(x) \ge 0\)"; "the probabilities total 1" becomes "the total area is 1"; "add the probabilities of the outcomes you want" becomes "integrate over the interval you want". If you can see the discrete statement behind each continuous one, there is genuinely no new idea here, only new notation.
  • \(f(x)\) is a density, not a probability, and it may exceed 1. A density of 3 on an interval of width \(0.1\) contributes only \(0.3\) of probability. This is why you can never read a probability off the vertical axis — probability is an area, so it always involves a width as well as a height. Nothing has probability until you integrate.
  • \(\Pr(X = c) = 0\) for every single value \(c\), because an interval of zero width encloses zero area. So \(<\) and \(\le\) may be swapped freely without changing an answer — a licence you do not have in the discrete case, where \(\Pr(X \le 2)\) and \(\Pr(X < 2)\) differ by the whole of \(\Pr(X = 2)\). This is also why the normal distribution in Part 3 only ever answers questions about ranges.
Where this bites

Sections 15A, 15B and 15F all set questions that open "\(X\) has probability density function \(f(x) = \ldots\)". Everything else in Chapter 15 then proceeds identically — but you have to get \(E(X)\) and \(\text{Var}(X)\) out of an integral first.

\(E(X)\) and \(\text{Var}(X)\) by integration

\[E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,dx \qquad\quad E(X^2) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 f(x)\,dx \qquad\quad \text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - \big[E(X)\big]^2\]

  • Same three lines as the discrete case. \(\sum x\,P(X=x)\) becomes \(\int x f(x)\,dx\), and the variance formula is unchanged.
  • Integrate only over the interval where \(f\) is non-zero — elsewhere the integrand is 0.
Worked example

\(f(x) = 1.5(1 - x^2)\) for \(0 \le x \le 1\), and \(0\) elsewhere. Find \(\Pr(X \le 0.5)\), \(E(X)\) and \(\text{sd}(X)\).

  • Check it is a density: \(\int_0^1 1.5(1-x^2)\,dx = 1.5\left[x - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = 1.5 \times \tfrac{2}{3} = 1\) ✓
  • \(\Pr(X \le 0.5) = 1.5\left[x - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^{0.5} = 1.5(0.5 - 0.0417) = 0.6875\).
  • \(E(X) = \int_0^1 1.5x(1-x^2)\,dx = 1.5\left(\tfrac12 - \tfrac14\right) = 0.375\).
  • \(E(X^2) = \int_0^1 1.5x^2(1-x^2)\,dx = 1.5\left(\tfrac13 - \tfrac15\right) = 0.2\), so \(\text{Var}(X) = 0.2 - 0.375^2 = 0.059375\) and \(\text{sd}(X) \approx 0.244\).
On CAS

Define \(f(x)\) once, then ask for \(\int f\), \(\int x f\) and \(\int x^2 f\). Three lines, no algebra by hand — and the same three lines every time.

Part 2 formula card

Discrete

\[\sum P(X=x) = 1 \qquad E(X) = \sum x\,P(X=x) \qquad \text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - \big[E(X)\big]^2\]

Continuous — same statements, \(\sum \to \int\)

\[\int f(x)\,dx = 1 \qquad E(X) = \int x\,f(x)\,dx \qquad \text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - \big[E(X)\big]^2\]

The habit worth building

When a distribution table appears, compute \(E(X)\) and \(\text{Var}(X)\) immediately, before reading the rest of the question. Nearly every Ch 15 question needs both, and having them written down turns a multi-part problem into substitution.

Part 3

The normal distribution and \(z\)-scores

  • What \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) says, and the 68–95–99.7 rule.
  • Standardising: \(z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\).
  • Probabilities as areas — and finding them on CAS, forwards and backwards.

Shape and notation

Notation

\(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) reads "\(X\) is normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\)".

  • Symmetric, single peak at \(\mu\). Because it is symmetric about its peak, \(\mu\) is simultaneously the mean, the median and the mode — so half the area lies either side of \(\mu\), and \(P(X > \mu) = 0.5\) exactly. That symmetry is what lets you convert \(P(Z > a)\) into \(P(Z < -a)\) whenever it is convenient, a move used constantly in Chapter 16.
  • \(\mu\) slides the curve; \(\sigma\) sets its width. Changing \(\mu\) moves the whole shape left or right without altering it. Changing \(\sigma\) makes it narrow and tall or wide and flat — and it must trade height for width, because the total area is pinned at 1. A larger \(\sigma\) therefore does not just spread the curve; it necessarily lowers the peak.
  • Total area is 1, because the curve accounts for every possible outcome. Every probability you ever compute from it is a fraction of that single unit of area, which is why no normal probability can exceed 1 and why "area to the left" plus "area to the right" is always exactly 1.
  • It is continuous, so \(P(X = 5) = 0\) and only intervals carry probability. \(P(X < 5)\) and \(P(X \le 5)\) are therefore equal — unlike the discrete case, where they differ by the whole of \(P(X = 5)\). Carrying a discrete habit into a normal question is harmless; carrying a normal habit into a binomial question is not.
Watch the second slot

\(N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) carries the variance, but every CAS command asks for the standard deviation. So \(N(100, 25)\) means \(\sigma = 5\), not 25.

The notation is not being awkward for its own sake: variance is the quantity that combines additively in Chapter 15, so it is the natural thing to carry in the distribution's name. Get into the habit of writing the variance in the brackets and the standard deviation into the calculator, and of pausing at every \(N(\ ,\ )\) you meet to ask which one is on the page.

Standardising: turning any normal into one normal

\[z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \qquad\qquad Z \sim N(0, 1)\]

  • A \(z\)-score answers one question: how many standard deviations from the mean is this value? Read the formula that way and it stops being something to memorise — the numerator is the distance from the mean, and dividing by \(\sigma\) converts that distance into "number of standard deviations". A negative \(z\) simply means the value sits below the mean.
  • Standardising strips out the units, so different distributions become comparable. A mass in grams and a time in seconds cannot be compared directly, but once each is expressed as "so many standard deviations from its own mean" they are on a common footing. It is also why one table of \(z\)-values serves every normal distribution there is: convert to \(z\), and there is only ever one curve to look things up on.
  • The transformation is itself a linear function of \(X\) — namely \(Z = \tfrac{1}{\sigma}X - \tfrac{\mu}{\sigma}\), which is \(aX + b\) with \(a = 1/\sigma\) and \(b = -\mu/\sigma\). This is not a coincidence, and it is exactly why Chapter 15 opens with linear functions: applying next week's rules to that \(a\) and \(b\) gives \(E(Z) = 0\) and \(\text{Var}(Z) = 1\), which is the whole content of "\(Z \sim N(0,1)\)". Standardising is the first linear transformation you ever met; Chapter 15 just makes the machinery explicit.
Quick example

Heights are \(N(170, 8^2)\) cm. A person 182 cm tall has \(z = \dfrac{182 - 170}{8} = 1.5\) — one and a half standard deviations above average.

The three you should know cold

\(z = 1.645\) leaves 5% in one tail · \(z = 1.96\) leaves 2.5% in each tail (the 95% interval) · \(z = 2.576\) leaves 0.5% in each tail (99%). Chapter 16 uses these constantly.

Probabilities are areas — and CAS finds them both ways

Forwards: value → probability

normCdf(lower, upper, \(\mu\), \(\sigma\))

For "less than 60" use a lower bound of \(-10^{99}\); for "more than 60" an upper bound of \(10^{99}\).

Backwards: probability → value

invNorm(area, \(\mu\), \(\sigma\)) returns the \(x\) with that much area to its left.

For a top-10% cut-off, ask for area 0.9.

Sanity checks
  • A probability must land in \([0,1]\). If it doesn't, a bound is wrong — usually a missing \(\pm 10^{99}\), which leaves the calculator integrating over the wrong interval entirely.
  • Above the mean, the answer is under 0.5; below it, over 0.5. Half the area sits either side of \(\mu\), so this one comparison catches a reversed inequality instantly, before it costs you the rest of the question.
  • Sketch the curve and shade before computing. Nearly every lost mark on normal probability is a tail error rather than an arithmetic one, and a five-second sketch makes the correct bounds obvious in a way that staring at the wording does not.
Symmetry facts

\(P(Z > a) = P(Z < -a)\)  and  \(P(Z > a) = 1 - P(Z < a)\).

The first holds because the curve is a mirror image about 0, so the upper tail beyond \(a\) has exactly the same area as the lower tail below \(-a\). The second holds because the two regions together are the whole curve, whose area is 1. Between them these let you rewrite any tail as any other, which is what makes a single \(z\)-table — or a single CAS call — enough for every question. Section 16D leans on the first one heavily.

Part 4

The binomial distribution

  • The four conditions that make a situation binomial.
  • \(\Pr(X = x)\), and the mean and variance you'll quote from memory.
  • Why 15F approximates it with a normal.

When is a situation binomial?

All four must hold
  • Fixed number of trials, \(n\), decided before you start. If the experiment runs until something happens, \(n\) is itself random and the distribution is not binomial.
  • Each trial has exactly two outcomes — success or failure. "Success" is just a label for the outcome you are counting; it need not be a good thing.
  • The probability of success, \(p\), is the same every trial. Anything that changes the odds part-way through — fatigue, sampling without replacement, a machine drifting — breaks this.
  • Trials are independent. The result of one must not shift the probabilities for the next.

The last two conditions are the ones that actually fail in practice, and they usually fail together: sampling without replacement changes \(p\) because earlier draws inform later ones.

Write it as \(X \sim \text{Bi}(n, p)\), where \(X\) counts the successes.

Is it binomial?
  • Yes — 20 free throws at a fixed 70% success rate; \(X\) = number made.
  • No — drawing 5 cards without replacement: \(p\) changes each draw, so the trials aren't independent.
  • No — throwing until the first six: \(n\) isn't fixed.

The formulas

\[\Pr(X = x) = \binom{n}{x} p^{x}(1-p)^{n-x}, \qquad x = 0, 1, \ldots, n\]

\[E(X) = np \qquad\qquad \text{Var}(X) = np(1-p) \qquad\qquad \text{sd}(X) = \sqrt{np(1-p)}\]

  • The formula is a count multiplied by a probability. \(p^x(1-p)^{n-x}\) is the probability of one particular sequence with \(x\) successes — multiply along the trials, which is legal precisely because they are independent. Every such sequence has that same probability, whatever order the successes come in, and \(\binom{n}{x}\) counts how many orders there are. Multiply the two and you have the probability of \(x\) successes in any order.
  • On CAS: binomPdf(\(n\), \(p\), \(x\)) for exactly \(x\), and binomCdf(\(n\), \(p\), lower, upper) for a range. Cdf is the one you want far more often, because most questions ask about "at least", "at most" or "between" rather than an exact count.
  • \(E(X) = np\) is worth internalising — 20 throws at 70% averages 14 made, which is the answer common sense would give. It is also worth noticing that \(\text{Var}(X) = np(1-p)\) is largest when \(p = 0.5\) and shrinks towards 0 as \(p\) approaches 0 or 1: a near-certain event produces very little variability, because almost every trial comes out the same way.
The usual slip

"At least 3" is \(\Pr(X \ge 3) = 1 - \Pr(X \le 2)\) — note the 2. Writing \(1 - \Pr(X \le 3)\) is the most common lost mark in the topic.

The reason is that \(X\) is discrete, so the complement of "3 or more" is "2 or fewer", not "3 or fewer" — the value 3 must land on exactly one side of the split. It is the same instinct that is harmless for the normal distribution, where \(\Pr(X = 3) = 0\) and the endpoint costs nothing, and that is exactly why it slips through: the habit is safe in one topic and wrong in the other. When you meet a "\(\ge\)" on a discrete variable, write the complement out in words before reaching for the calculator.

Why Chapter 15 comes back to this

A binomial with large \(n\) has so many possible values that its bar chart starts to trace out a normal curve. Section 15F makes that precise:

Looking ahead — 15F

For large \(n\), \(X \sim \text{Bi}(n,p)\) is approximately \(N\big(np,\; np(1-p)\big)\).

Both parameters come straight from the two formulas on the previous slide — which is the whole reason they're worth memorising now.

Also worth knowing

A binomial counts successes, so a sample proportion \(\hat{p} = X/n\) is a binomial divided by a constant — a linear function of a random variable. That is exactly the machinery of 15A, applied to real data.
