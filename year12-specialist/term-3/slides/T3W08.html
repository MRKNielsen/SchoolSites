Key idea

A proof is an argument that leaves no escape route. The technique you pick — direct, contrapositive, contradiction — is just a choice of which direction to close the exits from.

Lesson 1 · 2A

Conditionals and direct proof

  • \(P \Rightarrow Q\), its converse and its contrapositive — which are equivalent?
  • "Iff" means two proofs.
  • Direct proof: name the form, do the algebra, conclude.

Conditional statements and their relatives

StatementFormTied to original?
Conditional\(P \Rightarrow Q\)—
Converse\(Q \Rightarrow P\)No — independent
Contrapositive\(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\)Yes — equivalent
  • \(P \Leftrightarrow Q\) ("if and only if") requires proving both directions.
  • "\(n\) even \(\Rightarrow n^2\) even" is true; its converse also happens to be true — but that needed its own proof.

Direct proof — worked examples

Example 1A

Prove that the sum of two odd integers is even.

  1. 1 Name the form: let the integers be \(2m+1\) and \(2n+1\) for integers \(m, n\) — this is what "odd" means.
  2. 2 Algebra: \((2m+1) + (2n+1) = 2m + 2n + 2 = 2(m+n+1)\).
  3. ✓ \(m+n+1\) is an integer, so the sum is twice an integer — even. ∎
Example 1B

Prove that the product of any two consecutive integers is even.

  1. 1 Let the integers be \(n\) and \(n+1\). One of them must be even — but say why rather than asserting it.
  2. 2 Case 1, \(n\) even: \(n = 2k\), so \(n(n+1) = 2k(n+1)\) — twice an integer.
  3. 3 Case 2, \(n\) odd: \(n = 2k+1\), so \(n + 1 = 2k+2 = 2(k+1)\) and \(n(n+1) = 2n(k+1)\) — twice an integer.
  4. ✓ Every integer is even or odd, so the cases are exhaustive. The product is even in both. ∎
Worth noticing

"Obviously one of them is even" is the claim you were asked to prove. Splitting into cases is how you earn it.

Example 1C

Prove that if \(n\) is an odd integer, then \(n^2 - 1\) is divisible by 8.

  1. 1 \(n\) odd means \(n = 2k+1\) for some integer \(k\).
  2. 2 \(n^2 - 1 = (2k+1)^2 - 1 = 4k^2 + 4k = 4k(k+1)\).
  3. 3 \(k(k+1)\) is a product of consecutive integers, so it is even (Example 1B) — write \(k(k+1) = 2t\).
  4. ✓ Then \(n^2 - 1 = 4(2t) = 8t\), divisible by 8. ∎
Proofs stack

Example 1B was used here as a lemma. Quoting a result you have already proved is legitimate and saves work — name it when you do.

Example 1D

Prove that the sum of any three consecutive integers is divisible by 3.

  1. 1 Choose the symbol well. Call the middle one \(n\), so the three are \(n-1\), \(n\), \(n+1\).
  2. 2 Sum \(= (n-1) + n + (n+1) = 3n\).
  3. ✓ \(n\) is an integer, so the sum is three times an integer. ∎
Why the middle one

Starting at \(n\) gives \(3n + 3\) — still fine, but a line longer. Centring the algebra on the middle term makes the cancellation immediate. Choosing the variable well is part of the craft.

Example 1E

Prove that if \(a \mid b\) and \(b \mid c\), then \(a \mid c\). (Here \(a \mid b\) means "\(a\) divides \(b\)".)

  1. 1 Unpack both definitions. \(a \mid b\) means \(b = ak\) for some integer \(k\); \(b \mid c\) means \(c = b\ell\) for some integer \(\ell\).
  2. 2 Substitute the first into the second: \(c = (ak)\ell = a(k\ell)\).
  3. ✓ \(k\ell\) is an integer, so \(c\) is a multiple of \(a\), i.e. \(a \mid c\). ∎
The whole proof is the definitions

Nothing here is clever. Every step is "write down what the symbol means", and that is genuinely most of direct proof — the marks are in the translation, not the algebra.

The rhythm of direct proof

Translate the hypothesis into algebraic form → manipulate → recognise the conclusion's form. Every definition you write down is a mark.

  • (b) State the converse and the contrapositive of: "if it is raining, the ground is wet." Which is logically equivalent to the original?

  • (b) Converse: "if the ground is wet, it is raining" (not equivalent — sprinklers exist). Contrapositive: "if the ground is not wet, it is not raining" — equivalent.
Lesson 2 · 2A

Proof by contrapositive

  • When the hypothesis is hard to grip, prove \(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\) instead.
  • Splitting into exhaustive cases is legitimate — say why they cover everything.

Proof by contrapositive — worked examples

Example 2A

Prove: if \(n^2\) is even, then \(n\) is even.

  1. 1 Direct attack is awkward — "\(n^2\) even" is hard to use. Contrapositive: if \(n\) is odd, then \(n^2\) is odd.
  2. 2 \(n = 2k+1 \Rightarrow n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1\) — odd.
  3. ✓ The contrapositive is equivalent to the original, so the original is proved. ∎
When to reach for it

When the hypothesis is a "negative-flavoured" or hard-to-manipulate condition and its negation is concrete. "Not even" becomes "odd = \(2k+1\)" — algebra you can touch.

Example 2B

Prove: if \(3\) divides \(n^2\), then \(3\) divides \(n\).

  1. 1 Contrapositive: if \(3 \nmid n\), then \(3 \nmid n^2\). Now "not divisible" is concrete: \(n = 3k+1\) or \(n = 3k+2\).
  2. 2 Case 1, \(n = 3k+1\): \(n^2 = 9k^2 + 6k + 1 = 3(3k^2 + 2k) + 1\).
  3. 3 Case 2, \(n = 3k+2\): \(n^2 = 9k^2 + 12k + 4 = 3(3k^2 + 4k + 1) + 1\).
  4. ✓ Remainder 1 both times, so \(3 \nmid n^2\) either way. Exhaustive cases are legitimate — just say why they cover everything. ∎
Where this goes

This is the engine inside the proof that \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational — you will meet it again in Lesson 3.

Example 2C

Prove: if \(n^3 + 5\) is odd, then \(n\) is even.

  1. 1 "\(n^3 + 5\) is odd" gives you very little to hold. Contrapositive: if \(n\) is odd, then \(n^3 + 5\) is even.
  2. 2 \(n = 2k+1 \Rightarrow n^3 = 8k^3 + 12k^2 + 6k + 1 = 2(4k^3 + 6k^2 + 3k) + 1\) — odd.
  3. 3 So \(n^3 + 5\) is odd \(+\) odd \(=\) even. Writing it out: \(n^3 + 5 = 2(4k^3 + 6k^2 + 3k + 3)\).
  4. ✓ The contrapositive holds, so the original does. ∎
The tell

When the hypothesis is a statement about the output of some algebra and the conclusion is about the input, the contrapositive turns the problem the right way round — you get to start from the thing you can substitute for.

Example 2D

Prove: for integers \(a, b\), if \(ab\) is odd then \(a\) and \(b\) are both odd.

  1. 1 Negate the conclusion carefully. The negation of "\(a\) is odd and \(b\) is odd" is "\(a\) is even or \(b\) is even" — the "and" flips to "or".
  2. 2 Contrapositive: if \(a\) is even or \(b\) is even, then \(ab\) is even. An "or" hypothesis means two cases.
  3. 3 Case 1, \(a = 2k\): \(ab = 2(kb)\) — even. Case 2, \(b = 2k\): \(ab = 2(ak)\) — even.
  4. ✓ Even in both cases, so the contrapositive holds and the original is proved. ∎
De Morgan, doing real work

\(\neg(P \wedge Q) \equiv \neg P \vee \neg Q\). Negating "both" into "at least one" is where this proof is won or lost — and the cases are allowed to overlap, since "or" is inclusive.

Example 2E

Prove: for real \(x, y\), if \(x + y \geq 2\) then \(x \geq 1\) or \(y \geq 1\).

  1. 1 A direct attack has nowhere to go: the hypothesis is one inequality and the conclusion is a choice between two.
  2. 2 Negate the conclusion. The negation of "\(x \geq 1\) or \(y \geq 1\)" is "\(x < 1\) and \(y < 1\)" — now you have two facts to use.
  3. 3 Contrapositive: if \(x < 1\) and \(y < 1\), then \(x + y < 2\). Adding the two inequalities gives exactly that.
  4. ✓ So \(x + y < 2\), and the contrapositive is proved. ∎
Why this one is easy backwards

Negating an "or" conclusion hands you an "and" hypothesis — two usable facts instead of a fork in the road. Whenever the thing you must prove is a disjunction, try the contrapositive first.

Checkpoint 2 — your turn

Try it! (≈5 min)
  • (a) Prove by contrapositive: if \(n^2\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd.
  • (b) Prove by contrapositive: if \(5n + 3\) is even, then \(n\) is odd.
  • (a) Contrapositive: \(n\) even \(\Rightarrow n^2\) even. \(n = 2k \Rightarrow n^2 = 2(2k^2)\). ∎
  • (b) Contrapositive: \(n\) even \(\Rightarrow 5n+3\) odd. \(n = 2k \Rightarrow 5n + 3 = 10k + 3 = 2(5k+1) + 1\) — odd. ∎
Lesson 3 · 2A

Proof by contradiction

  • Negate precisely → deduce honestly → hit an impossibility.
  • The classic: \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.

Proof by contradiction — worked examples

Example 3A

Prove that \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.

  1. 1 Suppose not: \(\sqrt{2} = \dfrac{p}{q}\) with \(p, q\) integers sharing no common factor.
  2. 2 Then \(p^2 = 2q^2\): \(p^2\) is even, so \(p\) is even (Lesson 2's theorem) — write \(p = 2m\).
  3. 3 Substitute: \(4m^2 = 2q^2 \Rightarrow q^2 = 2m^2\), so \(q\) is even too.
  4. ✓ Now \(p\) and \(q\) share the factor 2 — contradicting the "no common factor" setup. So no such fraction exists. ∎
Anatomy

Negate precisely → deduce honestly → hit an impossibility → conclude. Note how the contrapositive result from Lesson 2 was a lemma here — proofs stack.

Example 3B

Prove that \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational.

  1. 1 Suppose \(\sqrt{3} = \dfrac{p}{q}\) in lowest terms, so \(p, q\) share no common factor.
  2. 2 Then \(p^2 = 3q^2\), so \(3 \mid p^2\), and by Example 2B that forces \(3 \mid p\). Write \(p = 3m\).
  3. 3 Substitute: \(9m^2 = 3q^2 \Rightarrow q^2 = 3m^2\), so \(3 \mid q^2\) and hence \(3 \mid q\).
  4. ✓ Both \(p\) and \(q\) are divisible by 3 — contradicting lowest terms. So \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational. ∎
The step students skip

\(3 \mid p^2 \Rightarrow 3 \mid p\) is not obvious and must be quoted or proved — that is exactly what Example 2B is for. Notice the same argument would collapse for \(\sqrt{4}\): there, "\(4 \mid p^2 \Rightarrow 4 \mid p\)" is false (take \(p = 2\)), which is the proof correctly refusing to show that \(\sqrt 4\) is irrational.

Example 3C

Prove that there are infinitely many prime numbers. (Euclid, c. 300 BC.)

  1. 1 Suppose not: there are finitely many primes, and they are exactly \(p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_r\).
  2. 2 Build a witness. Let \(N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_r + 1\). Note \(N > 1\).
  3. 3 \(N\) has some prime factor \(p\), and by assumption \(p\) is one of the \(p_i\) — so \(p\) divides the product \(p_1 p_2 \cdots p_r\).
  4. 4 But \(p\) also divides \(N\), so \(p\) divides the difference \(N - p_1 p_2 \cdots p_r = 1\).
  5. ✓ No prime divides 1, since every prime is at least 2. Contradiction — so the list of primes cannot be finite. ∎
A myth to avoid

This proof does not claim \(N\) is prime — often it isn't. \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 + 1 = 30031 = 59 \times 509\). All the argument needs is that \(N\) has some prime factor, and that no prime on the list can be it.

Example 3D

Prove that the sum of a rational number and an irrational number is irrational.

  1. 1 Let \(a\) be rational and \(b\) irrational, and suppose for contradiction that \(a + b\) is rational.
  2. 2 Write \(a = \dfrac{p}{q}\) and \(a + b = \dfrac{r}{s}\) with \(p,q,r,s\) integers, \(q, s \neq 0\).
  3. 3 Then \(b = (a + b) - a = \dfrac{r}{s} - \dfrac{p}{q} = \dfrac{rq - ps}{sq}\).
  4. 4 \(rq - ps\) and \(sq\) are integers and \(sq \neq 0\), so this exhibits \(b\) as a ratio of integers — \(b\) is rational.
  5. ✓ That contradicts \(b\) being irrational. So \(a + b\) is irrational. ∎
Test the neighbouring claims

"Irrational + irrational is irrational" is false: \(\sqrt2 + (-\sqrt2) = 0\). "Rational × irrational is irrational" is false too, at \(a = 0\). Only the statement above survives — which is why the hypotheses are worded so tightly.

Example 3E

Prove that \(\log_2 3\) is irrational.

  1. 1 \(\log_2 3 > 0\), so suppose for contradiction \(\log_2 3 = \dfrac{p}{q}\) with \(p, q\) positive integers.
  2. 2 By the definition of a logarithm, \(2^{p/q} = 3\). Raise both sides to the power \(q\): \(2^{p} = 3^{q}\).
  3. 3 Since \(p \geq 1\), the left side is even. Since \(3^q\) is a product of odd numbers, the right side is odd.
  4. ✓ An integer cannot be both even and odd. Contradiction — so \(\log_2 3\) is irrational. ∎
A different flavour of impossibility

The \(\sqrt{2}\) proofs end by contradicting a setup condition ("lowest terms") that you chose. This one ends by contradicting a fact about integers. Both are valid endings — a contradiction is a contradiction.

Checkpoint 3 — your turn

Try it! (≈6 min)
  • (a) Prove by contradiction: there is no largest integer.
  • (b) Prove by contradiction: there is no smallest positive rational number.
  • (a) Suppose \(N\) is the largest integer. Then \(N + 1\) is an integer and \(N + 1 > N\) — contradiction. ∎
  • (b) Suppose \(q\) is the smallest positive rational. Then \(\tfrac{q}{2}\) is rational, positive, and smaller — contradiction. ∎
  • Both proofs manufacture a better witness from the assumed extreme one — a standard contradiction pattern.
Lesson 4 · 2B

Quantifiers and counterexamples

  • \(\forall\) and \(\exists\): what each demands of a proof.
  • Negation swaps the quantifier.
  • One counterexample kills a universal claim.

For all, there exists

  • \(\forall\): "for all" — a universal claim. \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\; x^2 \geq 0\).
  • \(\exists\): "there exists" — an existence claim. \(\exists n \in \mathbb{Z},\; n^2 = 49\).
  • Order matters: \(\forall x\, \exists y\, (y > x)\) is true in \(\mathbb{R}\); \(\exists y\, \forall x\, (y > x)\) is false.
Negation rules

\(\neg\big(\forall x, P(x)\big) \equiv \exists x, \neg P(x)\)

\(\neg\big(\exists x, P(x)\big) \equiv \forall x, \neg P(x)\)

Negation swaps the quantifier.

Proof consequences

To prove \(\forall\): argue for an arbitrary element. To disprove \(\forall\): exhibit one counterexample. To prove \(\exists\): exhibit one witness. To disprove \(\exists\): argue for all.

Disproof by counterexample — worked examples

Example 4A

Claim: "for all primes \(p\), \(2^p - 1\) is prime." Disprove it.

  1. 1 Test small primes: \(p = 2, 3, 5, 7\) give \(3, 7, 31, 127\) — all prime. A suspicious streak.
  2. 2 \(p = 11\): \(2^{11} - 1 = 2047 = 23 \times 89\).
  3. ✓ Check both parts explicitly: \(11\) is prime (hypothesis ✓) and \(2047\) is composite (conclusion ✗). One counterexample; claim dead. ∎
Example 4B

Claim: "\(n^2 + n + 41\) is prime for every non-negative integer \(n\)." Disprove it.

  1. 1 Testing is discouraging: it is prime at \(n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\) — in fact for every \(n\) from 0 to 39.
  2. 2 Stop testing and think. The constant is 41, so try to make 41 a factor: at \(n = 41\), every term is divisible by 41.
  3. 3 That works, but there is a smaller one. At \(n = 40\): \(40^2 + 40 + 41 = 1600 + 40 + 41 = 1681\).
  4. ✓ \(1681 = 41^2\), which is composite. Hypothesis satisfied (40 is a non-negative integer), conclusion violated. ∎
Forty examples prove nothing

This is the single best argument in the course for why proof exists. A pattern that holds for the first forty cases can still be false — and no amount of further testing would ever have settled it.

Example 4C

Claim: "for all integers \(a, b, c\), if \(a \mid bc\) then \(a \mid b\) or \(a \mid c\)." Disprove it.

  1. 1 The claim is true when \(a\) is prime, which is why it feels right. So look for a composite \(a\) whose factors can be split between \(b\) and \(c\).
  2. 2 Take \(a = 6\), \(b = 4\), \(c = 3\). Then \(bc = 12\) and \(6 \mid 12\) ✓ — the hypothesis holds.
  3. ✓ But \(6 \nmid 4\) and \(6 \nmid 3\), so the conclusion fails. Claim disproved. ∎
Searching intelligently

Hunting at random rarely finds a counterexample. Ask instead why the claim might be true, find the hypothesis that argument secretly needs — here, that \(a\) is prime — and then break exactly that.

Example 4D

Claim: "for all real \(a, b\), if \(a > b\) then \(a^2 > b^2\)." Disprove it.

  1. 1 Squaring preserves order for non-negative numbers, so any counterexample must use a negative.
  2. 2 Take \(a = 1\), \(b = -2\). Hypothesis: \(1 > -2\) ✓.
  3. ✓ Conclusion: \(a^2 = 1\) and \(b^2 = 4\), so \(a^2 < b^2\). The claim fails. ∎
Repair the claim

A good follow-up to any disproof is to ask what extra hypothesis would rescue it. Here \(a > b \geq 0\) makes it true, and so does \(|a| > |b|\). Finding the correct statement is worth more than merely killing the wrong one.

Example 4E

Claim: "\(n! + 1\) is prime for every integer \(n \geq 1\)." Disprove it.

  1. 1 \(n = 1, 2, 3\) give \(2, 3, 7\) — all prime. \(n = 4\) gives \(25 = 5^2\), and the streak is already over.
  2. 2 Either counterexample will do. Take \(n = 5\): \(5! + 1 = 120 + 1 = 121\).
  3. ✓ \(121 = 11^2\), composite. Hypothesis satisfied, conclusion violated. ∎
Presenting a disproof

You do not need the smallest counterexample, and you do not need to explain how you found it. One fully checked instance, with the factorisation shown, is a complete answer.

Discipline

A counterexample must satisfy the hypothesis and violate the conclusion — check both, every time. And remember: examples can disprove a universal claim, never prove one.

  • (c) Write the negation of: "\(\exists y\) such that \(\forall x\), \(y > x\)." Is the negation true in \(\mathbb{R}\)?
  • (a) \(p = 2\): prime and even. ∎   (b) \(x = \tfrac12\): \(\tfrac14 < \tfrac12\) (or \(x = 0\): \(0 \not> 0\)). ∎

Week 8 summary

  • Contrapositive is equivalent; converse is not. "Iff" needs both directions.
  • Contradiction: negate precisely, push to impossibility.
  • Quantifier negation swaps \(\forall \leftrightarrow \exists\); proof obligations follow the quantifier.
  • One counterexample kills a universal claim; no number of examples proves one.
