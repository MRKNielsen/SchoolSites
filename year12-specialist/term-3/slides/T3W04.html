Key idea

A new sample gives a new \(\bar{x}\) — so \(\bar{X}\) has a distribution of its own. Its centre matches the population, but its spread shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{n}\).

Independent \(X, Y\): \(\text{Var}(aX+bY) = a^2\text{Var}(X) + b^2\text{Var}(Y)\) — always a plus.

\(X \sim \text{Bi}(n,p)\) with \(E(X) = np\) and \(\text{Var}(X) = np(1-p)\) — Lesson 4 needs both.

Why it matters here

\(\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}(X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n)\) is nothing but a linear combination of \(n\) independent copies of \(X\). Everything this week falls out of last week's two rules applied to that one line.

Lesson 1 · 15D

The sample mean is a random variable

  • Derive \(E(\bar{X}) = \mu\) and \(\text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\) from last week's rules.
  • Normal population → \(\bar{X}\) exactly normal, for every \(n\).
  • Compare individual vs average probabilities.

\(\bar{X}\) as a linear combination

\[\bar{X} = \frac{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}{n} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad E(\bar{X}) = \mu, \qquad \text{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

  • This is last week's algebra, applied once. \(\text{Var}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum X_i\right) = \tfrac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2 = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\). Follow the two steps: the coefficient \(\tfrac1n\) on each term gets squared, giving \(\tfrac{1}{n^2}\); and the \(n\) independent copies contribute \(n\sigma^2\) between them. The \(n\) and the \(n^2\) then cancel down to a single \(n\) in the denominator. Every surprising thing about the sample mean comes out of that one cancellation.
  • The mean does not shrink, only the spread does. \(E(\bar{X}) = \tfrac1n \cdot n\mu = \mu\): averaging leaves the centre exactly where the population's was. So \(\bar{X}\) and \(X\) are aimed at the same place, and the only difference between them is how tightly they cluster around it. That is the entire reason samples are useful — a sample mean is a better-behaved version of the same quantity, not a different one.
  • If the population is normal, \(\bar{X}\) is exactly normal — \(\bar{X} \sim N\!\left(\mu, \tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\) — because \(\bar{X}\) is a linear combination of independent normals, and last week established that such combinations stay normal. No approximation, no condition on \(n\); this holds for \(n = 2\) as surely as for \(n = 200\). The central limit theorem later in this deck is for the case where the population is not normal, and knowing which of the two you are relying on is a marked step in Chapter 16.
  • Precision is bought at a square-root rate. \(\text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\), so to halve it you must quadruple \(n\); to divide it by 10 you need a hundred times the data. This is the single most practically important fact in the topic: it explains why large studies are expensive, why doubling a sample is disappointing, and — in Chapter 16 — why halving the width of a confidence interval costs four times the sampling effort.

Worked example: individual vs average

Example 1

IQ scores are \(N(100,\, 15^2)\). Nine people are chosen at random. Find \(P(\bar{X} > 105)\), and compare with \(P(X > 105)\) for one person.

  • \(\bar{X} \sim N\!\left(100,\, \dfrac{15^2}{9}\right) = N(100,\, 5^2)\) — exactly normal, since the population is.
  • \(P(\bar{X} > 105) = P\!\left(Z > \dfrac{105-100}{5}\right) = P(Z > 1) \approx 0.159\).
  • One person: \(P(X > 105) = P\!\left(Z > \dfrac{1}{3}\right) \approx 0.369\).
Interpretation

An average of nine is much less likely to stray 5 points from the mean than a single individual is — 16% against 37%, better than a halving.

The reason is cancellation, exactly as in last week's \(2X\) versus \(X_1 + X_2\). For a single person to score above 105, that one person must be unusual. For the average of nine to exceed 105, the group as a whole must be unusual — and the high scorers have to outweigh the low ones rather than being offset by them. Individually unremarkable groups are common; collectively unremarkable groups that happen to average 5 points high are rare.

This is why the same numerical gap of 5 points is a routine event for one person and a notable one for a sample. Whenever a question asks you to "compare and comment", the comparison it wants is this: the same distance from \(\mu\) is measured against a smaller standard deviation, so it corresponds to a larger \(z\) and a smaller tail.

The distribution of \(\bar{X}\) narrows as \(n\) grows

The area beyond \(\bar{x} = 105\) shrinks from 0.369 (\(n=1\)) to 0.252 (\(n=4\)) to 0.159 (\(n=9\))

Lesson 2 · 15D

Totals and averages

  • Totals are sample means in disguise: \(T = n\bar{X}\).
  • Solve capacity problems via \(T\) or via \(\bar{X}\) — same \(z\), same answer.
  • Dodge the \(n\sigma^2\) vs \((n\sigma)^2\) trap.

Worked example: totals are sample means in disguise

Example 2

A lift's capacity is 1200 kg. Sixteen adults board; adult masses are \(N(70,\, 10^2)\) kg, independent. Find the probability the lift is overloaded.

  • Total \(T = X_1 + \cdots + X_{16}\): \(E(T) = 16 \times 70 = 1120\), \(\text{Var}(T) = 16 \times 100 = 1600\), so \(T \sim N(1120,\, 40^2)\).
  • \(P(T > 1200) = P\!\left(Z > \dfrac{1200 - 1120}{40}\right) = P(Z > 2) \approx 0.023\).
  • Equivalent route: \(T > 1200 \iff \bar{X} > 75\), and \(\bar{X} \sim N(70,\, 2.5^2)\) — same \(z\), same answer.
Note

Var of the total is \(n\sigma^2 = 1600\), not \((n\sigma)^2 = 25600\). Sixteen independent people, not one person weighed sixteen times.

This is last week's \(2X\) versus \(X_1 + X_2\) wearing different clothes, and the wrong route is seductive because \((n\sigma)^2\) comes from a rule that is correct — just for a different situation. Sixteen separate adults each contribute their own independent variation, so the variances add sixteen times over; sixteen copies of one adult's mass would scale a single variation by sixteen and square it. The lift is loaded by sixteen different people, so \(n\sigma^2\) is the right reading. Taking the wrong one gives \(\text{sd} = 160\) instead of \(40\) and turns a 2.3% overload risk into 31%.

The two routes shown above — via the total \(T\) and via the mean \(\bar{X}\) — are genuinely the same calculation, since \(T > 1200\) and \(\bar{X} > 75\) describe the identical event and produce the identical \(z\). Use whichever the question phrases itself in, and use the other as a check when you have time.

Lesson 3 · 15E–15F

Simulation and the central limit theorem

  • Watch the distribution of \(\bar{X}\) take shape by simulation — with the CAS commands to do it.
  • State the CLT precisely — what it claims, and what it doesn't.
  • Know when the normal claim is exact vs approximate.
  • Apply the CLT when the population is given by a density function.

Simulating \(\bar{X}\)

  • Start from a decidedly non-normal population — rolls of a die, or a skewed waiting-time distribution. The point of the exercise is lost if you begin with something already bell-shaped, because then the result is guaranteed by last week's work and the theorem is doing none of the lifting.
  • Draw a sample of size \(n\), record \(\bar{x}\), and repeat a few thousand times. Each repetition contributes one dot to the histogram — not \(n\) dots. This is the step worth being slow about: you are building a distribution whose individual observations are themselves averages, which is why it is called a sampling distribution rather than simply a distribution.
  • What you see is the theorem in three parts. The histogram sits centred at \(\mu\), no matter what \(n\) is; its spread is about \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), shrinking as \(n\) grows; and its shape approaches a normal curve as \(n\) grows, whatever shape the population had. The first two are exact results from 15D and hold for every \(n\); only the third is the central limit theorem, and only the third is an approximation.

The central limit theorem

Theorem (informal)

For a random sample of size \(n\) from any distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the distribution of \(\bar{X}\) is approximately \(N\!\left(\mu,\, \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\) when \(n\) is large.

  • Notice what the theorem does not say. It makes no claim about the population, which stays exactly as skewed or lumpy as it always was, and no claim about the individual values in your sample. The only thing that becomes normal is the distribution of the sample mean — a distribution of summaries, each one computed from a whole sample. Losing that distinction is what makes the theorem sound like magic instead of mathematics.
  • Working guideline: unless the population is very skewed, \(n\) between 25 and 30 is enough — and the approximation keeps improving as \(n\) grows. The reason there is no single number is that "large enough" genuinely depends on shape: a population already roughly symmetric needs very little help, while a badly skewed one needs a larger sample before the skew averages out. The live simulation on the previous slide is worth returning to for exactly this comparison.
  • Normal population → \(\bar{X}\) exactly normal for every \(n\); the CLT is only needed when the population isn't normal. Two different justifications for the same-looking statement, and Chapter 16 expects you to name the one you are using. If the question says "weights are normally distributed", cite that; if it gives you a density function or says nothing about shape and hands you \(n = 50\), cite the CLT. Citing the CLT for a normal population is not wrong so much as unnecessary — but citing normality for a population that was never claimed to be normal is wrong, and it is a marked error.
  • This is what makes next week possible. Every confidence interval and every hypothesis test in Chapter 16 needs to know the distribution of \(\bar{X}\) in order to convert a sample mean into a probability. Without the CLT that would require knowing the population's shape, which in real problems nobody does. With it, \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) suffice — which is why a single method covers examination marks, battery lifetimes and pollutant emissions alike.

Seeing it: a skewed population, a normal-looking mean

Waiting times (skewed, mean 1, sd 1) — yet \(\bar{X}\) for \(n = 30\) is already close to \(N(1,\, 1/30)\). The population stays skewed; only \(\bar{X}\) turns normal.

The standard CLT question: a population given by a density function

The CLT needs only \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) — so the usual exam setup hands you a probability density function and makes you extract them first. That first step is the whole difficulty.

Example 3

The length of a phone call, \(X\) minutes, has density \(f(x) = \dfrac{x}{8}\) for \(0 \le x \le 4\), and \(0\) elsewhere. Find the probability that the average length of 36 randomly chosen calls exceeds 3 minutes.

  1. \(E(X) = \displaystyle\int_0^4 x \cdot \frac{x}{8}\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{24}\right]_0^4 = \frac{8}{3} \approx 2.667\).
  2. \(E(X^2) = \displaystyle\int_0^4 x^2 \cdot \frac{x}{8}\,dx = \left[\frac{x^4}{32}\right]_0^4 = 8\), so \(\text{Var}(X) = 8 - \left(\tfrac83\right)^2 = \tfrac89\) and \(\sigma = \tfrac{2\sqrt2}{3} \approx 0.9428\).
  3. The population is not normal, but \(n = 36\) is large — by the central limit theorem, \(\bar{X} \mathrel{\dot\sim} N\!\left(\tfrac83,\; \tfrac{\sigma^2}{36}\right)\) with \(\text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \tfrac{0.9428}{6} = 0.1571\).
  4. \(P(\bar{X} > 3) \approx P\!\left(Z > \dfrac{3 - 2.667}{0.1571}\right) = P(Z > 2.121) \approx 0.017\).
  5. About 1.7% of such samples. Compare a single call: \(P(X > 3) = \int_3^4 \tfrac{x}{8}\,dx = 0.4375\).
Two things markers look for

Show \(E(X)\) and \(E(X^2)\) as integrals, and name the central limit theorem when you assert normality — the population here is a straight line, nothing like a bell.

Checkpoint 3 — true or false?

Try it! (≈4 min)
  • (a) \(\bar{X}\) is exactly normal only when the population is normal.
  • (b) The CLT says the population becomes normal when \(n\) is large.
  • (c) \(\text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \sigma/n\).
  • (d) For a normal population and \(n = 4\), we may use \(\bar{X} \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2/4)\) without invoking the CLT.
  • (a) True.   (b) False — only the distribution of \(\bar{X}\) is approximately normal; the population never changes.
  • (c) False — \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).   (d) True: exactness comes from the normal population, no CLT needed.
Lesson 4 · 15F

Normal approximation to the binomial

  • Why the CLT applies to binomial counts.
  • \(X \mathrel{\dot\sim} N(np,\, np(1-p))\) for large \(n\) — and the guideline for "large".
  • The sample proportion \(\hat{P}\) version.

Normal approximation to the binomial

  • A binomial is a sum, which is why the CLT reaches it. Write \(X = Y_1 + Y_2 + \cdots + Y_n\), where each \(Y_i\) is 1 for a success and 0 for a failure. The trials are independent and identically distributed, so this is precisely the situation the theorem describes — and \(X/n\), the proportion of successes, is literally a sample mean of the \(Y_i\). Nothing new is being asserted here; the binomial simply turns out to have been a sum of independent variables all along.
  • For large \(n\): \(X \mathrel{\dot\sim} N\big(np,\; np(1-p)\big)\) — matching the binomial's own mean and variance, since an approximation that got the centre or the spread wrong would be no use. Guideline: acceptable when \(np\) and \(n(1-p)\) are both above about 5.
  • That condition is really a condition about symmetry. A binomial is skewed when \(p\) is near 0 or 1, and requiring both \(np\) and \(n(1-p)\) to be comfortably large is what rules out the cases where the distribution is squashed against an end and cannot resemble a symmetric bell. With \(n = 100\) and \(p = 0.02\), \(np = 2\) and the count is bunched at 0, 1, 2 — no normal curve fits that. Raise \(n\) far enough and even a small \(p\) eventually qualifies, which is the theorem's promise in action.
Example 4

A fair coin is tossed 100 times. Estimate \(P(X \geq 60)\).

  • \(np = 50\), \(np(1-p) = 25\): \(X \mathrel{\dot\sim} N(50,\, 5^2)\).
  • \(P(X \geq 60) \approx P\!\left(Z \geq \dfrac{60-50}{5}\right) = P(Z \geq 2) \approx 0.023\). (Exact binomial: 0.028 — close, and far quicker.)
Sample proportion

Dividing by \(n\): \(\hat{P} = X/n \mathrel{\dot\sim} N\big(p,\, \tfrac{p(1-p)}{n}\big)\) — the same fact in proportion form.

The parameters follow from 15A alone: dividing by the constant \(n\) divides the mean by \(n\), giving \(np/n = p\), and divides the variance by \(n^2\), giving \(np(1-p)/n^2 = p(1-p)/n\). Note the \(\tfrac{1}{n}\) again — a proportion, like any other average, becomes more precise at a square-root rate.

Whether a question is about a count or a proportion decides which of the two forms you want. "How many of the 100 people" is a count and uses \(N(np, np(1-p))\); "what percentage of the sample" is a proportion and uses \(N(p, p(1-p)/n)\). Both give the same answer if you convert consistently, so the safest habit is to work in whichever the question states and convert only at the end.

Week 5 summary

  • \(\bar{X}\) is a random variable, with a distribution of its own: \(E(\bar{X}) = \mu\) and \(\text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\). A different sample would have produced a different \(\bar{x}\), and it is that sample-to-sample variation the distribution describes. Same centre as the population, smaller spread — and the spread shrinks only at a square-root rate, so precision is genuinely expensive.
  • Two routes to normality, and you must name the one you used. A normal population gives \(\bar{X}\) exactly normal for every \(n\), by last week's closure result. Any other population gives \(\bar{X}\) approximately normal provided \(n\) is large, by the central limit theorem. The distinction is a marked step in Chapter 16, and it is the first thing to settle when you read a question: does it tell me the population is normal, or does it only tell me \(n\) is big?
  • A binomial with large \(n\) is approximately normal with matching mean and variance, because a binomial count is itself a sum of \(n\) independent trials. The guideline \(np > 5\) and \(n(1-p) > 5\) is really asking whether the distribution is symmetric enough for a bell to fit it.
  • Everything in Chapter 16 rests on this one distribution. A confidence interval runs the logic of \(\bar{X} \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)\) forwards to bracket an unknown \(\mu\); a hypothesis test runs it backwards to ask how surprising an observed \(\bar{x}\) would be if a proposed \(\mu\) were true. Both are this week's result rearranged — so if the distribution of \(\bar{X}\) is secure, next week is mostly notation.
