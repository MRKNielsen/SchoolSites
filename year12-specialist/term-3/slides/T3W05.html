Key idea

Estimation asks "what values of \(\mu\) are plausible?" (an interval). Testing asks "is this particular \(\mu_0\) still believable?" (a decision). Same distribution, two questions.

Before we start: the three things Chapter 16 rests on

1 · The distribution of \(\bar{X}\)

\(\bar{X} \sim N\!\left(\mu, \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\), so \(\text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\).

Every interval and every test this week is this one fact, rearranged.

2 · Standardising

\(z = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\) — the same \(z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) you already know, with \(\bar{X}\)'s own spread on the bottom.

3 · Tail areas

\(z = 1.96\) leaves 2.5% in each tail (95%); \(z = 1.645\) leaves 5% in one tail; \(z = 2.576\) gives 99%.

A \(p\)-value is just a tail area found with normCdf.

Catch-up

Shaky on the normal distribution or \(z\)-scores? Part 3 of the Probability & Statistics Primer (T3W00) is the ten-minute version.

Lesson 1 · 16A

Confidence intervals for the mean

  • Build \(\bar{x} \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) from the distribution of \(\bar{X}\).
  • Say what "95% confident" means — and what it doesn't.
  • Other confidence levels: 90%, 99%.

The 95% confidence interval

\[\left(\bar{x} - 1.96\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\;\; \bar{x} + 1.96\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\]

  • Where does 1.96 come from? \(P(-1.96 < Z < 1.96) = 0.95\) for the standard normal.
  • If \(\sigma\) is unknown and \(n\) is large, use the sample standard deviation \(s\) in its place.
  • Other levels: 90% ↔ 1.645, 99% ↔ 2.576 — wider confidence costs a wider interval.
Example 1

\(n = 36\) batteries last \(\bar{x} = 25\) hours on average; \(\sigma = 6\) hours. Find the 95% CI for \(\mu\).

  • \(\text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \dfrac{6}{\sqrt{36}} = 1\); interval \(= 25 \pm 1.96 \times 1 = (23.04,\; 26.96)\) hours.

What "95% confident" actually means

  • The interval is random; \(\mu\) is fixed. Before sampling, there is a 95% chance the interval we will construct captures \(\mu\).
  • Correct sentence: "If we repeated this sampling process many times, about 95% of the intervals produced would contain \(\mu\)."
  • Not correct: "There is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) lies in \((23.04, 26.96)\)" — once computed, the interval either contains \(\mu\) or it doesn't.
Width levers

Width \(= 2z\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\). To halve the width: quadruple \(n\), or accept lower confidence. Exam questions love "find \(n\) so the width is at most …".

If the question gives you raw data

Several exercise questions hand you a list of 22 or 30 measurements. Put the list in a spreadsheet column and run one-variable statistics to get \(\bar{x}\); \(\sigma\) is given separately in the question, so use the given \(\sigma\), not the sample \(s_x\) your CAS reports.

The \(z\) values, to four decimals

90% → \(z = 1.6449\)  ·  95% → \(z = 1.9600\)  ·  99% → \(z = 2.5758\)

For any other level, \(z = \) invNorm\(\left(\tfrac{1 + C}{2},\, 0,\, 1\right)\) — for a 98% interval that is invNorm(0.99) \(= 2.3263\).

Twenty samples, twenty intervals

Each sample of \(n=36\) gives its own interval \(\bar{x} \pm 1.96\); in the long run about 1 in 20 miss.

Try it! (≈5 min)

A sample of \(n = 64\) test scores gives \(\bar{x} = 102\); the population sd is \(\sigma = 16\).

  • (a) Find the 95% confidence interval for \(\mu\).
  • (b) Find the 90% interval. Which is wider, and why?
  • (a) \(\text{sd}(\bar{X}) = \dfrac{16}{8} = 2\); \(102 \pm 1.96(2) = (98.08,\; 105.92)\).
  • (b) \(102 \pm 1.645(2) = (98.71,\; 105.29)\) — narrower: less confidence buys a tighter interval.
Lesson 2 · 16A

Margin of error, width and sample size

  • Margin of error \(= z\sigma/\sqrt{n}\); width \(= 2 \times\) margin of error.
  • Solve for \(n\) given a target — read carefully which target.
  • Scale width and \(n\) against each other without recomputing.

Margin of error — and the factor-of-4 trap

Margin of error

The distance from \(\bar{x}\) to either endpoint of the interval:

\[M = z\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad\qquad \text{width} = 2M\]

Questions state the target in one of two ways, and they are not the same number:

WordingWhat it fixesCondition on \(n\)
"the interval has width at most \(w\)"width\(2z\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le w\)
"\(\bar{x}\) is within \(d\) of \(\mu\)" · "the estimate is within \(d\)" · "the difference between \(\bar{x}\) and the true mean is at most \(d\)"margin of error\(z\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le d\)
Example 2

Delivery times have \(\sigma = 12\) minutes. How large a sample makes us 95% confident that \(\bar{x}\) is within 3 minutes of \(\mu\)?

  • Margin of error: \(1.96 \times \dfrac{12}{\sqrt{n}} \le 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; \sqrt{n} \ge \dfrac{1.96 \times 12}{3} = 7.84 \;\Longrightarrow\; n \ge 61.47\), so \(n = 62\).
  • Had the question said "width at most 3", the 3 would be shared between two sides: \(\sqrt{n} \ge 15.68\), \(n = 246\). Four times as many.
Round up, always

\(n\) must be a whole number of items, and rounding down produces an interval slightly too wide. 61.47 → 62, never 61.

Worked example: choosing the sample size

Example 3

Battery lifetimes have \(\sigma = 6\) hours. How many batteries must we test so that a 95% confidence interval for \(\mu\) has width at most 2 hours?

  • Width \(= 2 \times 1.96\,\dfrac{6}{\sqrt{n}} \leq 2 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \sqrt{n} \geq \dfrac{2 \times 1.96 \times 6}{2} = 11.76\).
  • \(n \geq 138.3\), and \(n\) is a whole number of batteries: \(n = 139\).
Round the right way

Always round up — 138 batteries gives an interval slightly too wide. And note the square-root law: halving the width quadruples the sample.

Scaling width against \(n\) — without any numbers

\(\sigma\) and \(z\) cancel whenever you compare two intervals at the same confidence level, so questions of the form "by what factor must \(n\) change…" are pure algebra:

\[W_1 = 2z\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n_1}}, \quad W_2 = 2z\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n_2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{W_2}{W_1} = \sqrt{\frac{n_1}{n_2}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \left(\frac{W_1}{W_2}\right)^{\!2}\]

Example 4

Read each of these off the relationship above.

  • (a) By what factor must \(n\) increase to decrease the width by 80%?
  • (b) What percentage increase in \(n\) decreases the width by 20%?
  • (c) \(n\) is increased by a factor of 9 — what happens to the width?
  • (d) \(n\) is decreased by a factor of 16 — what happens to the width?
  • (a) "Decrease by 80%" leaves \(W_2 = 0.2W_1\), so \(n_2 = (1/0.2)^2 n_1 = 25n_1\) — a factor of 25.
  • (b) \(W_2 = 0.8W_1 \Rightarrow n_2 = (1/0.8)^2 n_1 = 1.5625n_1\) — an increase of 56.25%.
  • (c) \(W_2/W_1 = 1/\sqrt{9} = \tfrac13\): the width is divided by 3.
  • (d) \(W_2/W_1 = \sqrt{16} = 4\): the width is multiplied by 4.
Read the direction carefully

"Decrease the width by 80%" means the new width is 20% of the old, not 80% of it. Write \(W_2 = 0.2W_1\) explicitly before squaring — that one line is where the marks are lost.

Checkpoint 2 — your turn

Try it! (≈5 min)

Delivery times have \(\sigma = 12\) minutes.

  • (a) How large a sample is needed for a 99% CI of width at most 4 minutes?
  • (b) How does the answer change at 95% confidence?
  • (c) How large a sample is needed to be 99% confident that \(\bar{x}\) is within 2 minutes of \(\mu\)? Explain why this matches one of your earlier answers.
  • (a) \(2 \times 2.576 \times \dfrac{12}{\sqrt{n}} \leq 4 \Rightarrow \sqrt{n} \geq \dfrac{2 \times 2.576 \times 12}{4} = 15.456 \Rightarrow n \geq 238.9\): \(n = 239\).
  • (b) \(\sqrt{n} \geq \dfrac{2 \times 1.96 \times 12}{4} = 11.76 \Rightarrow n \geq 138.3\), so \(n = 139\) — a hundred fewer: high confidence is expensive.
  • (c) \(2.576 \times \dfrac{12}{\sqrt{n}} \leq 2 \Rightarrow \sqrt{n} \geq 15.456\), the same condition as (a) — because a margin of error of 2 is a width of 4.
Lesson 3 · 16B

The logic of a hypothesis test

  • \(H_0\) is the status quo; \(H_1\) is the suspicion — fixed before seeing data.
  • The \(p\)-value: how surprising is our sample, if \(H_0\) is true?
  • Grade the strength of the evidence, not just reject / do not reject.
  • The decision rule, and why "do not reject" ≠ "accept".

The logic of a hypothesis test

  • Null hypothesis \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\) — the status quo, "nothing has changed".
  • Alternative \(H_1\) — what we suspect: \(\mu < \mu_0\), \(\mu > \mu_0\) or \(\mu \neq \mu_0\).
  • Strategy: assume \(H_0\), then measure how surprising the data would be.
p-value

The probability, assuming \(H_0\) is true, of observing a sample mean at least as extreme as the one we got.

Decision rule

Small \(p\)-value (below the significance level, usually \(\alpha = 0.05\)) → the data are too surprising for \(H_0\) → reject \(H_0\). Otherwise: do not reject \(H_0\) — which is not the same as proving it true.

Strength of evidence — the other way to answer

Not every question names a significance level. Some just give you a \(p\)-value and ask what it tells you. There is an agreed vocabulary for that:

\(p\)-valueStandard wording
\(p > 0.05\)insufficient evidence against \(H_0\)
\(p < 0.05\)  (5%)good evidence against \(H_0\)
\(p < 0.01\)  (1%)strong evidence against \(H_0\)
\(p < 0.001\)  (0.1%)very strong evidence against \(H_0\)
  • Two different questions, two different answers. "Test at the 5% level" wants a decision (reject / do not reject). "Interpret this \(p\)-value" wants a sentence from this table, plus what it means for the claim.
  • Model sentence: "\(p = 0.003\) is strong evidence against the null hypothesis, and in support of the claim that mean recovery time has decreased."
Careful

A small \(p\)-value is evidence against \(H_0\) — it is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true, and a large \(p\)-value is not evidence that \(H_0\) is true.

Worked example: a full test

Example 5

Recovery takes 5 days on average with the current drug (\(\sigma = 1\) day). On a new drug, 25 patients average \(\bar{x} = 4.6\) days. Test at the 5% level whether recovery time has reduced (\(\sigma\) unchanged).

  • \(H_0: \mu = 5\); \(H_1: \mu < 5\) (one-tail — we're asking about a reduction).
  • Under \(H_0\): \(\bar{X} \sim N\!\left(5,\, \left(\tfrac{1}{5}\right)^2\right)\). Test statistic: \(z = \dfrac{4.6 - 5}{1/\sqrt{25}} = -2\).
  • \(p = P(Z \leq -2) \approx 0.0228 < 0.05\): reject \(H_0\) — evidence, at the 5% level, that the new drug reduces mean recovery time. (Graded: \(0.01 < p < 0.05\), so good evidence.)
Write the conclusion in context

Always end with a sentence about drugs and days — not just "reject \(H_0\)".

The \(z\)-test on CAS

Its name

A hypothesis test for the mean of a sample drawn from a normal population with known standard deviation is called a \(z\)-test. Thanks to the central limit theorem, the same test is used for a large sample from any population.

The dropdown is the whole test

Choosing the wrong alternative hypothesis silently halves or doubles your \(p\)-value, and the CAS will not warn you. Decide the direction from the wording before you open the menu, and still write out the five lines — the CAS output alone earns almost none of the marks.

Seeing it: the \(p\)-value is a tail area

Under \(H_0\), \(\bar{X} \sim N(5,\, 0.2^2)\). Our \(\bar{x} = 4.6\) sits at \(z = -2\); the shaded tail (0.0228) is the \(p\)-value.

Lesson 4 · 16B

Full tests, written properly

  • Run complete tests start to finish, both directions of \(H_1\).
  • Practise the five-line setting-out that earns full marks.

The five-line setting-out

  1. Hypotheses: \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\); \(H_1\) with direction justified by the question's wording.
  2. Distribution under \(H_0\): \(\bar{X} \sim N(\mu_0,\, \sigma^2/n)\) — cite normal population or CLT.
  3. Test statistic: \(z = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\) with the numbers shown.
  4. p-value: the correct tail probability, compared against \(\alpha\).
  5. Conclusion in context: reject / do not reject, then a sentence with units.
Marker's-eye view

Each line carries marks independently. A correct \(p\)-value after wrong hypotheses still loses the setup marks — write line 1 before touching the CAS.

Worked example: testing an increase

Example 6

A machine fills bags with \(\mu = 500\) g of flour, \(\sigma = 8\) g. After maintenance, a sample of 16 bags has \(\bar{x} = 504.5\) g. At the 5% level, is there evidence the machine now overfills?

  • \(H_0: \mu = 500\); \(H_1: \mu > 500\) ("overfills" → one-tail, upper).
  • \(z = \dfrac{504.5 - 500}{8/\sqrt{16}} = \dfrac{4.5}{2} = 2.25\).
  • \(p = P(Z \geq 2.25) \approx 0.0122 < 0.05\) → reject \(H_0\): evidence, at the 5% level, that the machine now overfills bags.

Checkpoint 4 — your turn

Try it! (≈6 min)

Cereal boxes should contain \(\mu = 750\) g with \(\sigma = 10\) g. Consumer-watch suspects underfilling. A sample of 25 boxes gives \(\bar{x} = 745.2\) g. Test at the 1% level, writing all five lines.

  • \(H_0: \mu = 750\); \(H_1: \mu < 750\). Under \(H_0\): \(\bar{X} \sim N(750,\, 2^2)\).
  • \(z = \dfrac{745.2 - 750}{10/5} = -2.4\); \(p = P(Z \leq -2.4) \approx 0.0082\).
  • \(p < 0.01\) → reject \(H_0\): evidence, at the 1% level, that boxes are being underfilled on average.

Week 6 summary

  • 95% CI: \(\bar{x} \pm 1.96\sigma/\sqrt{n}\); interpret via repeated sampling, and know the width levers.
  • Tests: state \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) before touching the data; compute \(z\); convert to a \(p\)-value.
  • \(p < \alpha\) → reject \(H_0\); otherwise "do not reject" — never "accept".
  • Conclusions live in context: days, grams, hours — not just symbols.
